Chapter Six The People We Made Uncomfortable Discomfort has a remarkable ability to disguise itself as discernment. A person may enter a room, speak differently, carry a history unfamiliar to those gathered, dress in a manner outside accepted expectations, or express a life experience that challenges the assumptions of others. Before a meaningful conversation begins, someone may already have formed an opinion. The unfamiliar becomes suspicious, the unexpected becomes concerning, and personal uneasiness begins to acquire the language of spiritual conviction. Over time, feelings that were never examined can become standards by which people are evaluated, welcomed, or quietly excluded. The church must be willing to examine the difference between biblical conviction and personal discomfort. Scripture gives believers clear responsibilities concerning holiness, truth, repentance, love, humility, and the treatment of others. Personal preferences, cultural expectations, and emotional reactions also influence human behavior, yet those influences do not carry the same authority as the Word of God. Confusing the two can create serious consequences, especially when the people being judged are sincerely seeking Jesus. Jesus repeatedly encountered people whose presence challenged the expectations of religious society. His ministry brought Him into conversations, homes, and relationships that others questioned. The response of those around Him often revealed more about their assumptions than about the people receiving His attention. Their concern frequently centered on the company He kept, while His attention remained directed toward the spiritual needs of those standing before Him. Mark 2:13–17 records the calling of Levi, a tax collector, and the meal Jesus shared afterward. Tax collectors carried a reputation that made their presence unwelcome among many religious people. Their profession was associated with corruption, exploitation, and collaboration with Roman authority. Levi was not introduced as someone whose reputation had already earned public approval. Jesus called him while he was sitting at the tax office, and Levi responded by following Him. The gathering that followed placed Jesus among tax collectors and sinners. The religious observers questioned His association with such people. Their objection was not that Jesus had failed to preach. Their concern was that His ministry had placed Him in the company of people they considered unsuitable. Jesus answered by describing the purpose of His ministry through the image of a physician. Those who recognize their need seek help, while those who consider themselves well may fail to recognize their need for healing. His response redirected the conversation away from the reputation of the people at the table and toward the reason He had come. The scene exposes a danger that remains relevant. A congregation may become so concerned about maintaining an appearance of spiritual respectability that the presence of people needing spiritual care begins to feel like a threat. The church may speak extensively about holiness while becoming increasingly uncomfortable with the very people who need to hear the gospel. The problem is not the desire to honor God. The problem arises when preserving the comfort of the religious community begins to take priority over the mission of Christ. A church should never measure spiritual faithfulness by how familiar or comfortable the people inside the building feel. Familiarity can create confidence, yet confidence does not guarantee understanding. People can become accustomed to particular expressions of worship, family structures, clothing, language, testimony, and behavior until those preferences begin to feel inseparable from biblical truth. When someone arrives outside those expectations, the congregation may respond as though the unfamiliar person has already crossed a spiritual boundary. Such reactions deserve honest examination. A person who has never encountered a particular background, experience, or expression of life may feel uncertain. Uncertainty is a human response, and honest acknowledgment can become the beginning of learning. The danger lies in allowing uncertainty to become a judgment before the person has been heard. A congregation that treats unfamiliarity as evidence of spiritual danger may find itself defending assumptions rather than examining Scripture. Jesus did not allow the opinions of others to determine the direction of His ministry. His presence among people who carried difficult reputations was not accidental, nor was His willingness to engage them a departure from holiness. He understood the purpose of His coming. His ministry required Him to reach people who needed truth, mercy, repentance, restoration, and a relationship with God. The religious leaders often viewed the presence of certain people as a problem requiring separation. Jesus viewed the spiritual condition of humanity as a reason to come near. The difference between those responses deserves more than a passing observation. The church is called to represent Christ, and the manner in which believers approach people must reflect the character of the One they proclaim. A congregation may possess sincere convictions and still mishandle the people standing before them. A believer may accurately quote Scripture while communicating contempt through facial expressions, tone, silence, avoidance, or public embarrassment. The words spoken may appear defensible, yet the manner of delivery can reveal that the person has become more concerned with expressing disapproval than serving the spiritual needs of another human being. Christian conduct cannot be reduced to having the correct statement ready for every disagreement. Scripture calls believers to speak truth in love, to be gentle, to show mercy, to exercise patience, and to treat others with dignity. Those responsibilities remain present even during difficult conversations. The truth of God’s Word does not excuse cruelty, and conviction does not remove the obligation to love. The people we make uncomfortable may also be people whose presence exposes something within us. Their experiences may challenge the way we understand our own history. Their questions may reveal gaps in our knowledge. Their circumstances may require us to reconsider assumptions that have remained unexamined for years. Such moments can be uncomfortable for reasons that have nothing to do with the spiritual condition of the person before us. A person seeking Jesus should not have to carry the additional burden of managing the emotional reactions of everyone around them. The church should be a place where people can hear the gospel, ask sincere questions, receive instruction, encounter prayer, and discover the mercy of God. Spiritual growth requires truth, and truth requires a willingness to listen, learn, and remain accountable to Scripture. This does not mean every belief must be accepted, every behavior celebrated, or every disagreement abandoned. Christian discipleship includes correction, repentance, obedience, and transformation. The responsibility to teach those things belongs within the church’s mission. The manner of teaching, however, must remain consistent with the character of Christ. People should encounter the gospel through believers who are committed to truth and who recognize the dignity of those receiving that truth. The distinction matters greatly when people whose lives have been misunderstood or judged enter a church. LGBTQIA+ people, people with histories of addiction, individuals recovering from abuse, people whose marriages have failed, those carrying grief, and people whose experiences differ from the expectations of a congregation may arrive already anticipating rejection. Some have heard religious language used to condemn them before anyone asked about their faith, their questions, or their desire to know God. A congregation cannot assume that every person arriving through the doors has the same understanding of Scripture, the same spiritual history, or the same immediate needs. The church’s task is to proclaim the gospel, teach the Word, make disciples, and demonstrate the love of Christ. Those responsibilities require people to be treated as human beings rather than as problems to be solved before they are permitted to participate in the life of the community. Jesus knew the hearts of people, yet He did not demand that every person approaching Him first satisfy the expectations of those observing the encounter. He spoke to individuals, addressed their needs, revealed truth, and called them toward God. His ministry demonstrated that the presence of a person in need did not diminish His holiness. The church should not behave as though the presence of someone unfamiliar threatens the holiness of Christ. There is also a difference between protecting a congregation and protecting personal comfort. Genuine protection may require responsible boundaries, safeguarding, accountability, and careful leadership. Personal comfort may simply require that nothing challenge the familiar environment. Those purposes should never be confused. Spiritual leadership must exercise wisdom without allowing preferences to become unwritten laws that determine who receives kindness, patience, or attention. The church should be especially careful when its discomfort is directed toward people who have little power within the congregation. The person with the least influence may be the one most affected by a careless remark, an unanswered greeting, a public correction, or a decision that communicates their presence is unwelcome. Those with authority may never fully understand the weight of such an experience unless they are willing to listen to the people affected. Jesus consistently directed attention toward the responsibilities of those who held influence. Religious knowledge carried responsibility. Leadership carried responsibility. The ability to teach carried responsibility. The more influence a person possessed, the greater the need for humility and careful examination. A position within the church does not place someone beyond the possibility of misunderstanding God’s heart. Believers must therefore examine the reasons behind their reactions. A strong emotional response does not automatically establish a biblical conclusion. A feeling of offense does not necessarily mean that an offense against God has occurred. A preference for a particular environment does not establish that everyone outside that environment is spiritually deficient. Discernment requires more than instinct. Discernment requires prayer, Scripture, humility, and a willingness to recognize personal limitations. The question is not whether the church will encounter people whose lives, questions, or circumstances challenge its members. Such encounters are inevitable in a world filled with people carrying different histories and experiences. The question is whether those encounters will become opportunities for ministry or occasions for exclusion. The church was not established to preserve an atmosphere in which every member remains comfortable with every person present. The church exists to worship God, proclaim Jesus Christ, teach His Word, make disciples, serve others, and carry the gospel into the world. Those responsibilities will sometimes require believers to move beyond familiar surroundings, listen to people whose experiences differ from their own, and offer the same patience they once needed. Our discomfort deserves examination before becoming someone else’s burden. The people standing before us deserve to be heard before assumptions determine their place. Scripture deserves careful study before tradition becomes the final authority. Jesus deserves to be represented faithfully, not merely defended through reactions that may have more to do with us than with Him. A person who approaches the church seeking Jesus should encounter believers willing to reflect His character. The congregation may need to teach, guide, correct, and encourage that person along the journey of discipleship. Such responsibilities begin with recognizing that the person is worthy of compassion, worthy of being heard, and worthy of receiving the gospel without unnecessary barriers created by human preference. The people we made uncomfortable may have been the people we were called to serve. Their presence may have required patience we had not developed, understanding we had not pursued, or humility we had not practiced. Their arrival may have revealed whether our faith had prepared us to minister beyond the boundaries of familiarity. The heart of Christ calls the church to look beyond its own comfort and remember the purpose for which the gospel was entrusted to believers. Truth must remain central. Love must remain evident. Humility must remain present. The invitation to know Jesus must never be overshadowed by the reactions of people who have forgotten that every soul standing before them is someone for whom Christ’s ministry matters. The church must learn to distinguish the discomfort of being challenged from the conviction of being corrected by God. One may invite defensiveness, while the other calls for repentance. A congregation willing to make that distinction can grow in spiritual maturity and become a more faithful witness to the love and truth of Jesus Christ. People do not need a church that has mastered every question before opening its doors to compassion. They need believers committed to walking faithfully with Christ, teaching Scripture carefully, and recognizing that ministry often begins when someone unfamiliar enters the room. The gospel was never intended to remain confined within the comfort of those who already understand the language, customs, and expectations of the congregation. Jesus went where people needed Him. His followers must continually examine whether their own response to people reflects that same purpose. The church cannot allow personal discomfort to become the voice that answers a person seeking God. Our responsibility is to carry the gospel faithfully, and that responsibility requires us to make room for the people Christ came to reach.
