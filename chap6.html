CHAPTER SIX The People We Made Uncomfortable Discomfort can disguise itself as discernment. Someone may enter a room speaking differently, carrying an unfamiliar history, or expressing an experience that challenges our expectations. Before a conversation begins, an opinion may already be forming. The unfamiliar can become suspicious, and personal uneasiness can begin to sound like spiritual conviction. When those feelings go unexamined, preferences may become unwritten standards for deciding who is welcomed, heard, or quietly excluded. The church must distinguish biblical conviction from personal discomfort. Scripture calls believers to holiness, truth, repentance, love, humility, and care for others. Cultural expectations and emotional reactions also influence us, but those influences do not carry the authority of God’s Word. Confusing them can harm people who are sincerely seeking Jesus. Jesus repeatedly encountered people whose presence challenged religious expectations. Mark 2:13–17 describes Him calling Levi, a tax collector, and later eating with tax collectors and sinners. The religious observers questioned His association with people they considered unsuitable. Jesus responded with the image of a physician: those who recognize their need seek help, and He had come to call sinners. The exchange redirected attention from the reputation of the people at the table to the purpose of Christ’s ministry. Jesus did not abandon truth or holiness. His willingness to engage those people was part of the mission He came to fulfill. When familiarity becomes the standard A congregation can become so concerned with preserving an atmosphere of familiarity that the presence of people needing spiritual care begins to feel threatening. Worship styles, language, clothing, family backgrounds, testimonies, and customary behavior may become so familiar that preferences are mistaken for biblical requirements. Someone outside those expectations may be judged before anyone has taken time to listen. Uncertainty is a human response. A person encountering an unfamiliar background or experience may need time to understand. The danger lies in turning uncertainty into a conclusion about someone’s spiritual condition. A congregation that treats unfamiliarity as evidence of danger may find itself defending assumptions rather than examining Scripture. Comfort is not a reliable measure of truth. A familiar practice may be helpful, but familiarity alone does not make a practice biblical. A new circumstance may feel uncomfortable without being wrong. Our response should be guided by the Word, prayer, wisdom, and humility rather than by the immediate ease of the encounter. The religious observers focused upon the company Jesus kept, while He addressed the spiritual need before Him. Their opinions did not become the authority over His mission. Fear of being misunderstood Sometimes discomfort arises because we fear being misunderstood by others. A believer may worry that showing kindness will be interpreted as agreement, or that allowing a conversation will compromise a conviction. Such fears can cause people to withdraw before any meaningful interaction has occurred. Compassion does not require a believer to misrepresent their convictions, and honest conviction does not require withholding ordinary kindness. We can be clear about what we believe while refusing to make another person's presence a spectacle. Fear may also cause us to avoid the person altogether. We may become unusually quiet, redirect a conversation, refuse to offer a greeting, or allow someone else to handle the interaction because we are uncertain how to respond. Avoidance can seem safer than saying the wrong thing, but silence and distance may communicate a judgment we never intended to express. A lack of experience should not be disguised as certainty. Someone may not know what to say, how to respond to a question, or how to navigate a circumstance unfamiliar to them. Acknowledging that limitation is more honest than pretending expertise. We can ask respectful questions, listen without demanding private disclosure, and seek guidance when a situation requires more understanding. The goal is not to make every encounter effortless, but to remain willing to learn while treating people with care. The atmosphere our conduct creates A church's culture is shaped not only by sermons and policies, but also by ordinary interactions. Repeated jokes, assumptions, whispered conversations, visible reactions, unanswered greetings, and careless remarks can tell people that their presence is tolerated only so long as they remain unseen. Such conduct should not be dismissed as harmless simply because no formal policy of exclusion exists. Members may not recognize the effect of a comment because the comment was familiar within their own circle. A phrase used casually can carry a different weight for someone who has repeatedly been treated as an outsider. A conversation conducted in public may expose information that should have remained private. A reaction intended to express surprise may leave someone feeling scrutinized. The church should consider not only what was meant, but whether the conduct was appropriate and what repair may be needed. A faithful community can communicate its convictions openly while establishing a culture of respectful conversation. Members should know how to disagree without ridicule, how to address concerns privately when appropriate, and how to avoid using someone else's story as a public illustration. Leaders can model those practices by speaking carefully, refusing gossip, and correcting conduct that degrades people. Truth and the manner in which we carry it A believer may quote Scripture accurately while communicating contempt through tone, silence, avoidance, facial expression, or public embarrassment. The words may appear defensible, yet the manner of delivery can reveal a desire to express disapproval rather than serve the person receiving the message. Scripture calls believers to speak truth in love, exercise patience, show mercy, and treat others with dignity. Conviction does not remove those responsibilities during difficult conversations. Discipleship includes correction, repentance, obedience, and transformation. The church must teach faithfully, while ensuring the manner of teaching reflects Christ. People should encounter truth from believers who recognize the dignity of those receiving the message. Protection and preference are not the same Responsible boundaries, safeguarding, accountability, and careful leadership may be necessary. Protecting people is a legitimate responsibility. Protecting personal comfort is a different matter. The two should never be confused. A boundary should have a clear and responsible purpose. A preference may simply preserve the familiar. Leaders must exercise wisdom without allowing unwritten expectations to determine who receives kindness, patience, or attention. Those with the least influence in a congregation may be especially affected by careless remarks, unanswered greetings, public correction, or decisions that communicate their presence is unwelcome. Those affected may carry experiences leaders will never understand without listening. Listening does not require agreement with every conclusion, but it does require consideration rather than dismissal. Examining our reactions A strong emotional response does not automatically establish a biblical conclusion. Feeling offended does not necessarily mean God has been offended, and preferring a particular environment does not establish that everyone outside that environment is spiritually deficient. Discernment requires prayer, Scripture, humility, and awareness of our own limitations. The church will encounter people whose lives, questions, and circumstances challenge its members. Those encounters can become opportunities for ministry or occasions for exclusion. Our response must be shaped by Christ rather than by reflex. The church exists to worship God, proclaim Jesus, teach His Word, make disciples, serve others, and carry the gospel into the world. That work may require us to move beyond familiar surroundings and listen to people whose experiences differ from ours. Our discomfort deserves examination before becoming someone else’s burden. People deserve to be heard before assumptions determine how they are treated, and Scripture deserves careful study before custom becomes the final authority. The people we made uncomfortable may have revealed patience we had not developed or humility we had not practiced. Jesus went where people needed Him. Personal discomfort cannot become the voice that answers someone seeking God. Our responsibility is to carry the gospel faithfully and respond with the character of Christ.
