CHAPTER SIX The People We Made Uncomfortable Discomfort can disguise itself as discernment. Someone may enter a room speaking differently, carrying an unfamiliar history, or expressing an experience that challenges our expectations. Before a conversation begins, an opinion may already be forming. The unfamiliar can become suspicious, and personal uneasiness can begin to sound like spiritual conviction. When those feelings go unexamined, preferences may become unwritten standards for deciding who is welcomed, heard, or quietly excluded. The church must distinguish biblical conviction from personal discomfort. Scripture calls believers to holiness, truth, repentance, love, humility, and care for others. Cultural expectations and emotional reactions also influence us, but those influences do not carry the authority of God’s Word. Confusing them can harm people who are sincerely seeking Jesus. Jesus repeatedly encountered people whose presence challenged religious expectations. Mark 2:13–17 describes Him calling Levi, a tax collector, and later eating with tax collectors and sinners. The religious observers questioned His association with people they considered unsuitable. Jesus responded with the image of a physician: those who recognize their need seek help, and He had come to call sinners. The exchange redirected attention from the reputation of the people at the table to the purpose of Christ’s ministry. Jesus did not abandon truth or holiness. His willingness to engage those people was part of the mission He came to fulfill. When comfort becomes the standard A congregation can become so concerned with preserving an atmosphere of familiarity that the presence of people needing spiritual care begins to feel threatening. Worship styles, language, clothing, family backgrounds, testimonies, and customary behavior may become so familiar that preferences are mistaken for biblical requirements. Someone outside those expectations may be judged before anyone has taken time to listen. Uncertainty is a human response. A person encountering an unfamiliar background or experience may need time to understand. The danger lies in turning uncertainty into a conclusion about someone’s spiritual condition. A congregation that treats unfamiliarity as evidence of danger may find itself defending assumptions rather than examining Scripture. The people Jesus encountered were often viewed through reputations and social expectations. The religious observers focused upon the company He kept, while Jesus addressed the spiritual need before Him. His ministry did not make the opinions of others the authority over His mission. The church should consider whether a person’s presence is being treated as a problem simply because that person challenges what members are accustomed to seeing. Our discomfort may reveal an area where we need greater understanding, patience, or humility. It should not automatically become someone else’s burden. Truth and the manner in which we carry it A believer may quote Scripture accurately while communicating contempt through tone, silence, avoidance, facial expression, or public embarrassment. The words may appear defensible, yet the manner of delivery can reveal a desire to express disapproval rather than serve the person receiving the message. Christian conduct cannot be reduced to having the correct statement ready for every disagreement. Scripture calls believers to speak truth in love, exercise patience, show mercy, and treat others with dignity. Those responsibilities remain during difficult conversations. Conviction does not remove the obligation to love. This does not mean every belief must be accepted, every behavior celebrated, or every disagreement abandoned. Discipleship includes correction, repentance, obedience, and transformation. The church must teach those responsibilities faithfully. The manner of teaching, however, should reflect the character of Christ. People should encounter truth from believers who recognize the dignity of those receiving the message. Some people enter church already anticipating rejection because of experiences involving their identity, addiction, abuse, grief, failed relationships, or circumstances that differ from congregational expectations. The church cannot assume every person has the same spiritual history or immediate needs. Our task is to proclaim the gospel, teach Scripture, make disciples, and demonstrate Christ’s love, treating people as human beings rather than problems to resolve before compassion is offered. Protection and preference are not the same Responsible boundaries, safeguarding, accountability, and careful leadership may be necessary. Protecting people is a legitimate responsibility. Protecting personal comfort is a different matter. The two should never be confused. A boundary should have a clear and responsible purpose. A preference may simply preserve the familiar. Leaders must exercise wisdom without allowing unwritten expectations to determine who receives kindness, patience, or attention. Those with the least influence in a congregation may be especially affected by careless remarks, unanswered greetings, public correction, or decisions that communicate their presence is unwelcome. People affected by those decisions may carry an experience that leaders never fully understand unless they are willing to listen. Listening does not require agreeing with every conclusion. It does require recognizing that another person’s experience deserves consideration rather than dismissal. Examining our reactions A strong emotional response does not automatically establish a biblical conclusion. Feeling offended does not necessarily mean God has been offended, and preferring a particular environment does not establish that everyone outside that environment is spiritually deficient. Discernment requires prayer, Scripture, humility, and awareness of our own limitations. The church will encounter people whose lives, questions, and circumstances challenge its members. Those encounters can become opportunities for ministry or occasions for exclusion. The question is whether we will allow Christ to shape our response. The church exists to worship God, proclaim Jesus, teach His Word, make disciples, serve others, and carry the gospel into the world. Those responsibilities may require us to move beyond familiar surroundings and listen to people whose experiences differ from ours. We may need to offer the patience we once needed ourselves. Our discomfort deserves examination before becoming someone else’s burden. People deserve to be heard before assumptions determine how they are treated. Scripture deserves careful study before custom becomes the final authority. Jesus deserves faithful representation, not reactions driven by preferences we have never examined. The people we made uncomfortable may have been people we were called to serve. Their presence may have revealed patience we had not developed, understanding we had not pursued, or humility we had not practiced. The church must distinguish the discomfort of being challenged from the conviction of being corrected by God. Jesus went where people needed Him. His followers must examine whether their response reflects that same purpose. Personal discomfort cannot become the voice that answers someone seeking God. Our responsibility is to carry the gospel faithfully and make room for the people Christ came to reach.
