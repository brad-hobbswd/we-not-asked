CHAPTER FIVE Their Past Was Not Our Permission A person’s history can become the first thing others notice, especially when that history includes failure, addiction, broken relationships, public mistakes, or decisions that affected others. The details may be accurate, misunderstood, exaggerated, or repeated long after circumstances have changed. The danger begins when what we know about someone’s past becomes permission to decide how that person deserves to be treated now. The gospel places every human being in need of mercy. Our failures differ, their consequences vary, and the circumstances of our lives are not identical, yet none of us approaches Christ through personal righteousness. Grace is not a reward for having a history that appears respectable. We cannot use our knowledge of another person’s past to claim authority over the invitation of Jesus. A reputation may remain long after a person has begun rebuilding a life. Someone may seek help, repent, repair relationships, and pursue a different direction while others continue responding as though the earlier circumstances remain the only truth. Accountability may still be necessary, and trust may take time to restore. Those realities do not require us to deny the possibility of change or treat a person’s past as the permanent measure of their worth. What we carry into an encounter People rarely meet without some prior understanding influencing the conversation. Family experiences, community knowledge, earlier conflict, and stories repeated over time can shape a response before anyone has listened. Familiarity may offer context, but can also prevent us from recognizing change. A person entering a congregation may already be known by someone present. Their history may have been discussed years earlier, and the details may still follow their name. Another person may arrive with a history unknown to the congregation while carrying shame that makes compassionate reception difficult to imagine. Both may need the church to remember that people are more than the information others possess about them. History can help explain circumstances, but it cannot reveal the entirety of a person’s present life, spiritual longing, intentions, or the work God may be doing. Human judgment is limited. The confidence with which we sometimes describe another person can exceed what we actually know. Discernment is necessary, particularly when safety, accountability, or restoration is involved. Discernment must not become a reason to reduce someone to remembered failures. We should take care with the stories we tell, the conclusions we reach, and the assumptions we carry. Truthfulness does not remove our responsibility to speak with care. Information about another person's life should be handled with restraint. Knowing something does not mean we are entitled to repeat it. A private conversation may have been shared for the purpose of receiving counsel, not for becoming part of a congregation's ongoing discussion. Even information that is widely known should not be repeated merely to establish that we remember it. The church must consider whether sharing a detail serves a legitimate purpose, protects someone, or supports responsible care. When the answer is simply curiosity, the information should remain unspoken. Stories can change as they move from person to person. Details may be removed, motives supplied, and context lost. Someone who was not present may speak with certainty about an event they understand only through another person's account. Such repetition can create a reputation more difficult to correct than the original circumstance. Believers should be slow to speak, careful about what they know firsthand, and willing to acknowledge when they do not know the whole story. There is also a difference between remembering a past event for responsible reasons and bringing the event into every new interaction. A history may be relevant to a particular decision, yet irrelevant to an ordinary greeting, worship, prayer, or conversation. Treating every encounter as though the person must answer again for an old chapter can communicate that no present effort will ever be recognized. The church should not make someone's entire life revolve around the matter that once required attention. When failure becomes the whole identity Scripture does not minimize sin. The Bible speaks plainly about wrongdoing, repentance, and the need for a changed life. The message of redemption also declares that God forgives, restores, and transforms. The seriousness of sin and the possibility of redemption belong together within the gospel. When a person is defined exclusively by failure, the possibility of growth can become obscured. A name may remain attached to an event, a character may be summarized by one decision, and efforts to move forward may be met with suspicion. Accountability can then become a means of prolonging humiliation rather than addressing wrongdoing responsibly. The church must distinguish between confronting sin and perpetuating shame. Correction should serve truth, responsibility, and restoration where restoration is appropriate. Correction should not become a way to keep someone permanently beneath the weight of a past mistake. We cannot proclaim forgiveness while treating people as though their past remains the only truth about them. Redemption is not an empty word. The Lord’s work in a person’s life deserves neither careless assumption nor cynical dismissal. People also deserve the opportunity to speak for themselves when their circumstances are being considered. A decision made from secondhand information may be incomplete. Listening does not require accepting every statement without discernment; it allows facts, context, and concerns to be considered fairly. Those responsible for pastoral care should resist making a person's story a foregone conclusion before hearing them, especially when the consequences of that conclusion affect relationships, participation, or spiritual support. The church must be cautious when testimony becomes entertainment or when details of someone's former life are repeatedly retold to demonstrate how far they supposedly fell. A testimony should glorify the work of God, not expose a person beyond what they have chosen to share. The individual should not be pressured to disclose painful or private details in order to prove that change has occurred. The mercy of Christ does not depend upon the public display of someone's wounds. Jesus and the Samaritan woman John 4:7–26 records Jesus meeting a Samaritan woman at a well. Their conversation unfolds amid cultural divisions, religious differences, personal history, and questions concerning worship. Jesus speaks with her, addresses matters of truth, and reveals Himself through the encounter. The passage should not be reduced to the claim that every circumstance surrounding the woman was irrelevant. Jesus addressed her life directly. Yet He did not avoid the conversation because of her background or make her history a reason to deny engagement. He spoke truth and offered living water. The conversation became an opportunity for revelation. The woman heard Jesus speak concerning worship and His identity, then returned to the city and told others about Him. Many came to believe through her testimony. The narrative shows that a person who might be approached through assumptions about her circumstances became a witness to what she had encountered. The church must not allow knowledge of someone’s history to prevent meaningful spiritual engagement. People need the truth of Christ. That truth cannot be replaced by judgments formed before we have listened or considered what God may be doing. Redemption is not erased by remembered failure Paul reminded the Corinthians of lives marked by sin and then declared that some had been washed, sanctified, and justified in the name of Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of God (1 Corinthians 6:9–11, AMPC). The passage addresses the seriousness of sin while also affirming the transforming work of God. The text does not promise that every consequence disappears or that growth is always immediate. The passage declares that believers’ identity cannot be confined to the circumstances from which God has called them. Christ changes the standing of those who come to Him, and the Spirit continues shaping their lives. We should not assume someone remains unchanged simply because we remember who they once were. Nor should we presume to know the entirety of God’s work in another life. The church must recognize sin honestly, proclaim redemption clearly, and encourage faithful growth. A person’s past may remain part of a testimony. The past must never become a weapon used to deny dignity, discourage spiritual pursuit, or claim authority over whether someone may approach Jesus. Accountability is not contempt Some circumstances require boundaries. Trust may need rebuilding, harm may require acknowledgment, and protecting others may call for responsible action. Compassion does not mean ignoring harm, and forgiveness does not automatically restore every relationship or remove every consequence. The distinction is between appropriate accountability and a refusal to recognize the humanity of the person being held accountable. Someone may need to address wrongdoing while still being treated with dignity. A boundary can be necessary without expressing contempt. A difficult conversation can take place without declaring someone beyond God’s mercy. Faithfulness to truth requires honesty about wrongdoing. Faithfulness to Christ requires care in the way people are treated. Neither responsibility should be abandoned. When a person’s history becomes the basis for a permanent verdict about spiritual worth, we have moved beyond accountability and into presumption. No believer possesses authority to declare another person’s past has closed the invitation of Jesus. The mercy we have received Examining someone else’s history should also lead us to examine our own. We know the mercy of God through recognizing our need for forgiveness. Our circumstances may differ from another person’s, but grace is not evidence of our superiority. In Matthew 18:21–35 (AMPC), the parable of the unforgiving servant confronts the contradiction between receiving mercy and refusing to extend mercy. A servant forgiven a great debt would not show compassion toward someone who owed him far less. The parable exposes a failure to understand the weight of mercy. The passage does not erase accountability. Rather, it challenges those who have received mercy to consider how they treat others. We cannot celebrate the grace that reached us while treating another person’s need for grace as evidence of unique unworthiness. Their past was not our permission The church must engage people with truth, recognize their humanity, and direct them toward Christ. A person’s history may require conversation; circumstances may require discernment; actions may require accountability; spiritual growth may require teaching. None of those responsibilities grants permission to treat someone as though their past permanently closes the invitation of Jesus. The question is not whether history matters. The question is whether we have allowed remembered history to become more authoritative in our judgment than the mercy of Christ. We must take care with the conclusions we reach, the stories we repeat, and the attitudes we carry toward people whose lives differ from our own. Redemption belongs to God, and the church is called to proclaim the gospel faithfully. The past may explain where someone has been. A person's history does not grant us authority to determine whether they may approach the Savior. Their past was not our permission to stand between them and Jesus.
