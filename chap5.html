CHAPTER FIVE Their Past Was Not Our Permission A person’s history can become the first thing others see, particularly when that history includes failure, painful circumstances, broken relationships, addiction, public mistakes, or decisions that have affected the lives of others. The past may be known through personal testimony, family conversations, public records, community familiarity, or stories repeated long after the circumstances themselves have changed. Sometimes the information is accurate. Sometimes portions have been misunderstood, exaggerated, or carried forward without consideration of the full story. Regardless of how the history became known, the danger arises when knowledge of another person’s past begins functioning as permission to determine how that person should be treated in the present. The gospel confronts humanity with a reality that prevents any person from standing above another through personal righteousness. Every human being needs the mercy of God. The circumstances surrounding our failures may differ, the consequences may vary, and the ways our lives have been affected by sin may not be identical, but our need for salvation remains shared. No one approaches Christ with a history so perfect that grace becomes unnecessary, and no one should be treated as though another person’s knowledge of their past grants authority over the invitation of Jesus. The church must be careful when the history of a person becomes the defining explanation for their presence. A reputation may continue long after a person’s circumstances have changed. Someone who has made destructive decisions may spend years attempting to rebuild relationships, restore trust, and establish a different direction for life, yet those who remember the earlier circumstances may continue responding as though the past remains the only relevant truth. The person may have grown, learned, repented, sought help, or experienced a genuine transformation, while the surrounding community remains unwilling to recognize anything beyond what it once knew. Such circumstances reveal the difference between remembering what happened and allowing what happened to become the permanent measure of another person’s worth. Accountability may remain necessary. Consequences may continue. Relationships may require time and rebuilding. None of those realities requires treating a person’s past as though the possibility of God’s work in the present has disappeared. The history we carry into the room People rarely encounter one another without bringing some understanding of what they already know. Family relationships, community experiences, previous conflicts, and personal observations can influence the way a person responds before a conversation has even begun. Familiarity may provide important context, but familiarity can also make it difficult to recognize change. A person entering a congregation may already be known by someone in the room. Their history may have been discussed years earlier, and the details may remain attached to their name. Another person may arrive with a history unknown to everyone present, yet carry shame so deeply that the possibility of being received with compassion feels uncertain. Both circumstances reveal the weight people may carry when they approach a community of faith. The church must recognize that people are more than the information others possess about them. A person’s history may help explain certain circumstances, but history alone cannot reveal the entirety of their present life, their spiritual longing, their intentions, or the work God may be doing within them. Human judgment is limited, and the confidence with which people sometimes speak about another person’s character can exceed what they actually know. Scripture calls believers to exercise discernment, yet discernment must never become an excuse for reducing another human being to a collection of remembered failures. We must be careful with the stories we tell, the conclusions we reach, and the assumptions we carry into our interactions. The responsibility to speak truthfully does not remove the responsibility to speak with care. The past may be relevant to a particular conversation, especially when accountability, safety, restoration, or the rebuilding of trust is involved. The concern is not that history should never be acknowledged. The concern is that history should not become a permanent declaration that a person is unworthy of compassion, incapable of change, or beyond the reach of Jesus. When failure becomes an identity There is a difference between acknowledging that a person has committed wrongdoing and defining that person exclusively by the wrongdoing. The distinction is essential to the way the gospel speaks about sin, repentance, forgiveness, and transformation. Scripture does not minimize sin. The Bible speaks plainly about the seriousness of wrongdoing and the need for repentance. Yet the message of redemption also reveals that the Lord is able to forgive, restore, and transform lives. The reality of sin and the possibility of redemption are not competing truths. Both belong within the message of the gospel. When people are reduced to their failures, the possibility of meaningful change can become obscured by the judgments surrounding them. Their names may become associated with a particular event, their character may be summarized through a past decision, and their attempts to move forward may be met with suspicion rather than thoughtful consideration. Such treatment can make the consequences of a past failure extend far beyond what is necessary for accountability. The church must recognize the difference between addressing wrongdoing and perpetuating a person’s humiliation. Correction should be rooted in the purpose of bringing people toward truth, responsibility, and restoration where restoration is appropriate. The pursuit of accountability cannot become a means of maintaining someone’s shame indefinitely. The message of the gospel calls us to recognize that transformation is possible through the work of God. We cannot proclaim forgiveness while treating forgiven people as though their past remains the only truth about them. We cannot speak of redemption while refusing to acknowledge that the Lord may be working in lives we once judged. The Samaritan woman and the assumptions surrounding her John 4 provides an account of Jesus meeting a Samaritan woman at a well. The encounter takes place within circumstances shaped by cultural divisions, religious differences, personal history, and questions concerning worship. Jesus engages her in conversation, reveals truths concerning her life, and directs the discussion toward the living water He offers. The encounter demonstrates that Jesus was willing to engage a person whose circumstances could easily have become the focus of someone else’s judgment. He did not ignore the questions surrounding her life, nor did He treat those questions as a reason to avoid the conversation. He spoke with her, addressed matters of truth, and revealed Himself through the encounter. The passage should not be reduced to a simplistic argument that every circumstance surrounding the woman was irrelevant. Jesus addressed her life directly. Yet the way He engaged her reveals the difference between confronting truth and refusing to engage a person because of the circumstances through which that person is known. The conversation became an opportunity for revelation. The woman heard Jesus speak concerning worship, living water, and His identity. She eventually went into the city and spoke to others about the encounter, and many came to believe because of her testimony. The narrative demonstrates that a person who may be approached through assumptions concerning their history can become a witness through an encounter with Christ. The church must be careful not to allow knowledge of another person’s circumstances to prevent the possibility of meaningful spiritual engagement. People need the truth of Christ, and the proclamation of that truth cannot be replaced by the judgments we form before we have listened, understood, or considered what the Lord may be doing. The past cannot become a permanent verdict The apostle Paul wrote about the transformation of those who had come to faith in Christ, reminding the Corinthians of the kinds of lives from which some had been brought and declaring that they had been washed, sanctified, and justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of God. His words in 1 Corinthians 6:9–11 address the seriousness of sin while also declaring the transforming work of God among people whose former lives had included circumstances requiring redemption. The passage does not suggest that every consequence disappears or that the process of spiritual growth is always immediate. It establishes that the identity of believers cannot be confined to the sinful circumstances from which God has called them. The work of Christ changes the standing of those who come to Him, and the work of the Spirit continues shaping their lives. This truth should influence the way believers regard others. We must not assume that a person remains unchanged simply because we remember the circumstances through which they once lived. Nor should we presume to know the entirety of God’s work in another person’s life. The church is called to recognize the seriousness of sin, proclaim the possibility of redemption, and encourage the pursuit of a life transformed through Christ. A person’s past may remain part of their testimony, but their past does not belong to us as a weapon. We cannot use remembered failures to deny dignity, discourage spiritual pursuit, or establish ourselves as the authority over whether someone may approach Jesus. The difference between accountability and rejection There are circumstances in which accountability requires careful boundaries. Trust may need to be rebuilt, harm may require acknowledgment, and the protection of others may require responsible action. The gospel does not eliminate those responsibilities. Compassion does not require ignoring the realities of harm, and forgiveness does not automatically restore every relationship or remove every consequence. The distinction must remain clear, however, between appropriate accountability and a refusal to recognize the humanity of the person being held accountable. A person may be required to address the consequences of wrongdoing while still being treated with dignity. A boundary may be necessary without becoming an expression of contempt. A difficult conversation may be needed without communicating that the person is beyond the reach of God’s mercy. The church must be able to hold these responsibilities together. Faithfulness to truth requires honesty about wrongdoing, while faithfulness to Christ requires care in the way people are treated. Neither responsibility should be abandoned in favor of the other. When a person’s history is used to establish a permanent judgment concerning their spiritual worth, the discussion has moved beyond accountability into the territory of human presumption. No believer possesses the authority to declare that another person’s past has placed them beyond the invitation of Jesus. The mercy we received The examination of another person’s past should lead us toward an examination of our own. We know the mercy of God through the recognition that we, too, needed forgiveness. The circumstances of our lives may not resemble those of another person, yet the grace through which we stand before God is not the result of our superiority. The parable of the unforgiving servant in Matthew 18:21–35 confronts the contradiction between receiving mercy and refusing to extend mercy. The servant who had been forgiven a tremendous debt failed to show compassion toward someone who owed him far less. His response revealed that the mercy he had received had not shaped the way he regarded another person’s obligation. The parable is not a declaration that accountability disappears. It confronts the failure to recognize the significance of mercy when dealing with another person. Those who understand the mercy of God should be careful not to approach others through an attitude of superiority, particularly when the circumstances involve failure, debt, wrongdoing, or the need for forgiveness. We cannot honestly celebrate the mercy that reached us while treating another person’s need for mercy as though such a need makes them uniquely unworthy. Our responsibility is to recognize the seriousness of sin without forgetting the seriousness of our own need for grace. Their past was not our permission The people Jesus encountered did not arrive with histories that required the approval of those surrounding Him. His ministry reveals the importance of engaging people with truth, recognizing their humanity, and directing them toward the kingdom of God. The church must carry that responsibility without assuming that knowledge of another person’s past gives us authority to obstruct their approach to Christ. A person’s history may require conversation. Their circumstances may require discernment. Their actions may require accountability. Their spiritual life may require teaching and discipleship. None of those responsibilities establishes permission to treat the person as though their past has permanently closed the invitation of Jesus. The question is not whether their history matters. The question is whether we have allowed their history to become more authoritative in our judgment than the invitation of Christ. We must resist the temptation to make remembered failures the defining measure of another person’s worth. We must remain careful with the conclusions we reach, the stories we repeat, and the attitudes we carry toward those whose lives have included circumstances different from our own. We must recognize that the work of redemption belongs to God and that the responsibility of the church is to proclaim the gospel faithfully. The past may explain where someone has been. It does not grant us authority to determine whether they may approach the Savior. Their past was not our permission to stand between them and Jesus.
