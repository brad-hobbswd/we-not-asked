CHAPTER THIRTEEN The People Who Stopped Coming A congregation may notice when someone who once worshiped regularly, served, asked questions, or stood among the people is no longer present. The routine adjusts. Names are mentioned less often, conversations move toward those who remain, and absence becomes familiar. People leave for many reasons. A move, changing schedules, family responsibilities, health concerns, financial circumstances, or a different season may affect attendance. Some find another congregation. Others step away temporarily while navigating life. There are also people who leave because something happened: a conversation damaged trust, a decision affected them, a public correction caused humiliation, or a private struggle was handled without care. Not every departure tells the same story. We should not assume every absence represents a wound, nor conclude that everyone who leaves has abandoned God. The danger lies in the certainty with which explanations are sometimes assigned without asking the person who left. When absence becomes an assumption Church communities can begin explaining departures through familiar conclusions: someone became offended, lacked commitment, could not accept correction, or wanted to live differently. Such explanations may sound plausible, but the person may never have been given an opportunity to describe what happened. Absence becomes evidence, and evidence becomes a verdict. When every departure is attributed to personal weakness or spiritual immaturity, a congregation never has to examine whether something within the community contributed to the separation. Self-examination becomes unnecessary when every problem is assigned to someone who is no longer present to speak. A person may leave a congregation while continuing to pray, read Scripture, worship, and believe in Jesus. They may distance themselves from leadership while still longing for fellowship. Another person may need time to recover from an experience that made spiritual community difficult. We cannot determine another person’s spiritual condition merely by whether they occupy a seat in our sanctuary. The church should care for people and examine circumstances honestly, rather than create explanations that make departures easier to dismiss. Missing someone and pursuing attendance There is meaning in knowing that our absence has been noticed by people who care. A message asking how someone is doing can communicate concern without pressure. A phone call may create space for honest conversation. A welcomed visit may remind someone that relationships are not limited to attendance. Concern can become complicated when the primary objective is getting someone back into the building. Repeated questions about when they will return, where they are attending, or how many services they have missed may feel like pressure. The conversation can focus on attendance rather than the person’s circumstances. There is a difference between missing someone and treating their absence as a problem to correct. Someone who has experienced spiritual harm may need a listener who does not immediately defend the congregation. A person grieving may need compassion. Someone exhausted may need practical support. A person disillusioned with leadership may need an honest conversation about the questions they carry. The more meaningful question may be, “How are you doing?” rather than, “When are you coming back?” One centers the congregation’s attendance; the other centers the person. Jesus noticed people others overlooked. His ministry did not reduce human beings to their usefulness within a religious community. The church should care about someone without making their return the condition of that care. Leaving may involve grief Leaving a church can require considerable courage. People may have invested years in relationships, ministry, worship, friendships, and spiritual growth. They may have considered the congregation family and trusted its leadership. A departure can involve grief, even when leaving has become necessary. Someone may miss familiar worship, relationships, routines, and experiences of genuine connection with God. They may remember spiritual blessings while also carrying painful interactions. Both experiences can be true. Some leave quietly because explaining their decision requires conversations they no longer have the emotional capacity to undertake. Others tried to raise concerns and felt unheard. Some have learned that speaking honestly can lead to further conflict. Silence does not mean the absence of a story. The church should not make a departure appear uncomplicated merely because the congregation did not witness the struggle leading to the decision. Someone may have prayed, sought counsel, attempted reconciliation, and hoped circumstances would improve. The visible action of leaving may represent the end of a long process. The shepherd who notices John 10:11–18 (AMPC) describes Jesus as the Good Shepherd, whose relationship with the sheep involves knowledge, care, protection, and sacrifice. The image provides a standard for considering those who have become absent from a congregation. A shepherd’s concern is not limited to counting sheep within an enclosure. Care involves recognizing their condition and responding to their needs. The church must not confuse spiritual care with institutional control. Attendance records, membership lists, schedules, and organizational systems may serve useful purposes, but they do not replace personal concern. Someone may be absent because they are struggling; another may be flourishing in a different season or setting. The shepherding responsibility begins with care, not assumptions. A person should not have to return to the building before the church becomes concerned about their life. Their value should not diminish when participation changes. Genuine Christian love does not disappear when organizational involvement ends. Those who still believe but no longer trust Some people who stop attending have not stopped believing in Jesus. They may still pray, read Scripture, and desire fellowship while struggling to trust religious leadership. The distinction between faith in Christ and confidence in a particular institution matters. When someone describes a painful church experience, the response should not automatically assume defective faith. Concerns about leadership may be legitimate. The need for boundaries may represent a responsible decision rather than rebellion. The church must not use faithfulness to demand that people remain in circumstances where repeated harm has occurred. Forgiveness does not require ignoring wrongdoing. Reconciliation requires honesty, accountability, and willingness to address the conditions that damaged a relationship. Trust cannot be demanded as though automatically owed to every person holding authority. The burden of repair cannot rest exclusively upon the person who was wounded. Leaders who caused harm must be willing to acknowledge responsibility. Scripture’s calls to submission, forgiveness, and unity must never become instruments for silencing people who are trying to explain what happened. Some relationships may require distance. Some circumstances may remain unresolved. Some people may choose not to return. Christian love does not require pretending trust has never been broken. When the wounded person is blamed One painful experience after leaving a church is hearing that departure proves something negative about one’s character. A person may be described as bitter, rebellious, offended, unfaithful, or unwilling to submit. Such labels can replace listening. A concern may be raised, and the conversation may quickly center upon the person’s attitude rather than the circumstances described. Their words may be scrutinized while the conduct that prompted the concern receives little attention. Personal responsibility should never be ignored. People can misunderstand, react poorly, communicate imperfectly, and need correction. Accountability must be honest and consistent, however. The person raising a concern should not automatically become the person blamed for creating the concern. Galatians 6:1 (AMPC) instructs believers to restore someone caught in wrongdoing with gentleness while considering their own vulnerability. Restoration, humility, and accountability belong together. Correction should seek healing and growth, and leaders must remain willing to examine their own conduct. The door should not become a test of loyalty Some people may eventually consider returning. Their decision can depend upon healing, relationships, time, spiritual growth, and the congregation’s willingness to address previous concerns. A return should not require pretending nothing happened. Honest conversations may be necessary. Clarification, accountability, changed behavior, or restored boundaries may be part of rebuilding. The purpose is not to restore appearances, but to pursue relationships that are truthful and consistent with Christ. Some may return, and others may not. A congregation should not measure the success of its care solely by whether someone comes back. Listening, acknowledging mistakes, offering apologies, and respecting another person’s decision may represent meaningful growth even when a relationship does not return to its former form. The church must learn to care without controlling the outcome. The people we may never hear from again Some people leave and never explain why. The congregation may never learn what happened. Such uncertainty should encourage humility rather than speculation. We do not know every story, struggle, prayer, conversation, or reason someone found necessary to leave. The absence of an explanation does not authorize us to invent one. Their names should not become examples used to reinforce assumptions, and their private circumstances should not become material for gossip. A congregation can learn from departures without becoming consumed by blame. It can examine patterns, evaluate leadership, improve communication, strengthen accountability, and become more attentive to the way people experience fellowship, teaching, correction, and care. Honest reflection does not require assuming every criticism is accurate. It requires taking the possibility of error seriously enough to investigate. The invitation remains Christ’s People who stopped coming may have stories the church will never fully understand. Some may return, others may find fellowship elsewhere, and some may remain distant from religious communities for a season. Their spiritual journeys cannot be measured by attendance alone. Jesus remains the Savior. His mercy does not become limited by a congregation’s decisions, and His ability to reach someone does not depend upon the approval of religious observers. The church is called to proclaim the gospel, teach Scripture, serve people, encourage discipleship, and cultivate fellowship. Those responsibilities are meaningful, but people who leave should not become enemies simply because their absence is difficult to understand. Those who remain should not be discouraged from honest examination. Leaders who have caused harm should not be protected through spiritual language, and wounded people should not be required to deny their experiences to preserve appearances. The church should care beyond the attendance record, the organizational structure, and its ability to observe someone’s spiritual journey. A congregation should be able to miss someone without condemning them, reach out without manipulating them, listen without immediately defending itself, and acknowledge mistakes without fearing that humility weakens its authority. Some people may never return to the place where they once worshiped. Their absence does not mean Jesus has stopped seeking them. Their silence does not mean their story no longer matters. Their departure does not grant the church permission to decide who they have become. They remain people worthy of love, dignity, patience, compassion, and honest concern. Fellowship should not be maintained through pressure, fear, or shame. Its purpose is to help people grow in Christ, care for one another, and live faithfully. The church must remember that the invitation of Jesus remains greater than our institutions. The people who stopped coming are not beyond His reach. Our responsibility is to ensure that the way we represent Him never becomes the reason someone believes His invitation is no longer available.
