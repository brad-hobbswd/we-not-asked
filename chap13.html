PART III · THE COST OF INTERFERENCE

The People Who Stopped Coming

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 13 of 17Reading edition

Churches notice when someone stops coming. Sometimes the absence is obvious; sometimes a person gradually becomes less visible until no one remembers the last time they were there. We may assume they moved, became busy, lost interest, or made a decision about faith. Any of those things may be true, but we do not know until we ask.

People leave for many reasons. A new job, illness, family responsibility, grief, conflict, exhaustion, or a painful experience may change someone's ability or desire to attend. Some are still seeking God but no longer know how to remain connected to a congregation. Others may simply need a season of quiet. Their absence should not automatically become a judgment about their spiritual condition.

A thoughtful message can mean more than a public announcement. Reaching out without pressure, asking how someone is doing, and being willing to listen may open a conversation. The goal is not to collect an explanation or make someone feel guilty for missing services. The goal is to let them know they have not become invisible.

We should also be prepared to hear something difficult. Someone may tell us they felt overlooked, misunderstood, or hurt. They may describe a concern we never knew about. Defending ourselves before hearing the whole story can close the conversation. We can listen, ask clarifying questions, and take responsibility where responsibility is ours.

Not every person wants to return, and we should respect that. Care is not manipulation. We can offer prayer, practical help, or a conversation without making attendance the condition for being treated kindly. Their worth does not rise or fall with a church schedule.

Jesus spoke of seeking the lost, and the church should care about people beyond the moment they are present in a building. That care is expressed through patient, genuine relationships rather than pressure. Sometimes the most faithful thing we can do is reach out, listen, and let the person decide what they are ready to share.

An empty seat may remind us that someone is missing, but the person is more than the seat. We should remember their name, their life, and the relationship we have with them. If we have contributed to their distance, we need the courage to acknowledge that. If we have not, we can still offer sincere care.
