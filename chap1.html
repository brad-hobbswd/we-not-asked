CHAPTER ONE We Were Not Asked The moment the disciples attempted to prevent people from bringing children to Jesus, a question of authority emerged that would remain relevant long after the scene had passed. They were close to the Master, familiar with His ministry, and responsible for accompanying Him through the demands of His work. Their position among His followers, however, did not grant them authority to determine who could approach Him. The people were bringing children for Jesus to touch, yet the disciples responded as though the decision belonged to them. Mark records the encounter with a detail that deserves particular attention: Jesus became indignant. His response was not an expression of mild disagreement or a passing preference for a different arrangement. He addressed the disciples directly, instructing them to allow the children to come and forbidding them from hindering those who were being brought to Him. The correction revealed that their actions had crossed a boundary established by the Lord Himself. They had taken upon themselves a responsibility that did not belong to them. The disciples may have believed they were acting in the best interest of Jesus. The demands surrounding His ministry were considerable, and people frequently gathered around Him seeking teaching, healing, answers, and help. Their actions could have arisen from a desire to maintain order, protect His time, or prevent what they considered an unnecessary interruption. Scripture does not provide an explanation for their reasoning, and the absence of such an explanation should caution us against assigning motives the text does not establish. What Scripture does make unmistakably clear is that Jesus rejected their response. The distinction between intention and obedience becomes significant here. A person may sincerely believe a decision serves a worthwhile purpose while failing to recognize that the decision contradicts the direction of Christ. Good intentions cannot establish authority where authority has not been given. Religious responsibility does not make every judgment correct, and closeness to Jesus does not exempt His followers from correction. The disciples were not outsiders attempting to disrupt the ministry. They were followers who had already received instruction, witnessed miracles, heard His teaching, and watched Him respond to people whose needs often interrupted the ordinary rhythm of life. Their failure was not caused by a lack of proximity to Jesus. Their proximity simply did not prevent them from misunderstanding what He expected. That distinction deserves consideration within the church today. Spiritual familiarity can create confidence, and confidence can gradually become assumption. People who have served for years, studied Scripture extensively, held leadership positions, or inherited longstanding religious practices may begin believing their judgments carry a weight beyond examination. Familiarity with the language of faith can sometimes make a person less attentive to the possibility of misunderstanding the very Savior whom they claim to represent. The disciples’ actions reveal the danger of allowing our understanding of responsibility to exceed the authority Christ has actually entrusted to us. They were following Jesus, yet their response to those approaching Him was inconsistent with His own response. The correction was necessary precisely because they were His followers. Their position did not make the obstruction acceptable. The presumption of authority Authority is not inherently harmful. Scripture presents leadership, teaching, shepherding, correction, and accountability as meaningful responsibilities within the body of Christ. The problem begins when authority becomes detached from the purpose for which God entrusted such responsibility. A person may be appointed to teach without being appointed to control the Savior’s invitation. A shepherd may guide a congregation without claiming ownership over the people whom Christ calls. A minister may address questions of doctrine and conduct without assuming the right to place personal judgment between another soul and Jesus. The distinction is not merely theoretical. The way authority is exercised can influence whether people feel encouraged to seek God, whether they receive instruction with dignity, and whether the church accurately represents the character of the One it proclaims. Responsibility requires discernment, yet discernment must remain submitted to Scripture rather than becoming a justification for personal preference. The disciples had no recorded instruction from Jesus directing them to prevent the children from approaching Him. Their action arose within the moment, yet Christ’s response established the correction. They had presumed to regulate an encounter that belonged to Jesus. The children were not seeking the disciples’ approval. They were being brought to the Savior, and the disciples had made themselves an unnecessary obstruction. Such presumption can become deeply embedded in religious life when a person’s position begins to influence how unquestionable their decisions appear. A leader may speak, and the congregation may accept the conclusion without carefully examining the Scripture involved. A family may preserve a conviction for generations, and the belief may become so familiar that questioning the interpretation feels equivalent to questioning faith itself. A community may develop expectations surrounding who belongs, who is acceptable, or whose presence is welcomed, even when those expectations have not been carefully distinguished from the teaching of Christ. The authority of Scripture must remain greater than the authority of our assumptions. Respect for leadership does not require abandoning biblical examination. Loyalty to a church family does not require accepting every inherited interpretation without considering the Word. Faithfulness to Christ demands the humility to recognize that human understanding remains subject to His correction. The disciples were corrected in a moment when their actions prevented others from approaching Jesus. The significance of the moment extends beyond their particular mistake. Their example reminds us that spiritual responsibility cannot be measured merely by position, title, years of service, or familiarity with religious practices. The question must always remain whether our actions faithfully reflect the Lord whom we serve. The invitation belonged to Jesus The children were being brought to Jesus, not to establish their standing among the disciples. Their purpose was to encounter Him. The disciples’ response introduced a condition that Jesus had not imposed. The people who brought the children were met with resistance from those who believed they were managing the situation, yet Jesus removed that resistance and called the children near. The invitation was His to extend. The authority to receive them belonged to Him. This truth must remain central whenever the church encounters people seeking the Lord. Human beings may have complicated histories, painful circumstances, difficult questions, or lives that differ significantly from the expectations of those surrounding them. Such differences do not transfer ownership of the invitation from Christ to the people observing them. The church is entrusted with proclaiming the gospel and making disciples, while the Lord remains the Savior who receives, convicts, transforms, and directs those who come to Him. A person seeking Jesus should not have to establish personal acceptability before another human being grants permission to approach the Savior. The gospel calls people to repentance, faith, obedience, and a transformed life, yet those calls must be proclaimed as the message of Christ rather than converted into personal barriers that discourage people from seeking Him. We must never confuse the responsibility to teach with the presumption that we control the beginning of someone’s encounter with God. The disciples’ correction was not a declaration that every question concerning conduct, doctrine, or discipleship had disappeared. Jesus did not abandon His teaching or the responsibilities He would give His followers. He addressed the immediate obstruction. Those children were being prevented from approaching Him, and He commanded the disciples to stop hindering them. The distinction matters. The passage does not authorize believers to disregard Scripture. The passage does confront believers who presume the authority to obstruct access to Jesus. Our responsibility is to proclaim His Word faithfully, demonstrate His love sincerely, and remain humble enough to recognize the difference between the instruction He has given and the restrictions we have created. When familiarity becomes a substitute for understanding The disciples had heard Jesus teach about the kingdom, yet their conduct in this encounter revealed that their understanding required further correction. They had witnessed His compassion, but their response did not reflect the compassion He was demonstrating toward those being brought to Him. The moment exposes a truth that cannot be dismissed simply because the disciples eventually became leaders of the early church: walking with Jesus does not guarantee that every assumption we carry has been shaped by His heart. Religious familiarity can become a powerful influence upon interpretation. A person may know the passages commonly quoted within a congregation while remaining unfamiliar with the broader context of those passages. A belief may be repeated so often that repetition begins functioning as evidence. A practice may be defended because previous generations maintained the practice, even though the reasons supporting the practice have never been carefully examined. Such circumstances do not automatically make every tradition false, but they demonstrate why believers must remain willing to examine the Scriptures rather than relying solely upon inherited conclusions. Jesus repeatedly confronted circumstances in which religious expectations obscured the purpose of God’s Word. His ministry called people to recognize the difference between outward appearances and the condition of the heart, between human traditions and divine commandments, and between religious activity and genuine obedience. The disciples’ response to the children belongs within the larger biblical concern for the way human understanding can interfere with the purposes of God. The church must never become so certain of its own interpretations that the words of Jesus cease to challenge its conduct. The Scriptures are not merely a collection of passages used to defend positions already established. They are the Word through which our beliefs, practices, motives, and attitudes must continually be examined. A faithful reading of Scripture requires more than finding support for what we already believe. Faithfulness also requires the willingness to recognize when the Word confronts us. The mission was never ours to narrow The Great Commission establishes the responsibility of the church to carry the gospel beyond familiar surroundings and into the lives of people throughout the world. Jesus commanded His disciples to make disciples of all nations, baptize them, and teach them to observe His commandments. The commission places the church in the position of witness and servant. We carry the message; we do not become the source of the invitation. The scope of the commission challenges every attempt to narrow the reach of the gospel according to personal comfort. People encountered through ministry may possess different histories, cultures, circumstances, questions, and experiences. The messenger cannot assume that familiarity determines who should receive the message. Nor can the church confuse the discomfort of engaging someone different from ourselves with a biblical prohibition against reaching that person. The mission of Christ calls believers outward. The invitation of Jesus calls people toward Him. The responsibility of the church is to faithfully proclaim the message and teach those who respond, not to make personal approval the prerequisite for hearing the gospel. The disciples’ actions in Mark 10 remind us that obstruction can occur even among people who believe they are serving Jesus. Their correction provides a necessary warning against allowing religious expectations to overshadow the mission entrusted to us. The church must examine whether its attitudes, practices, and interpretations encourage people to seek the Lord or create unnecessary resistance along the way. The correction we must be willing to receive Jesus did not leave the disciples’ obstruction unaddressed. He corrected them, called the children near, and demonstrated the response His followers were expected to understand. His actions placed the needs of those seeking Him before the assumptions of those attempting to regulate the encounter. The passage leaves us with a responsibility that cannot be transferred entirely to leaders, institutions, denominations, or previous generations. Each believer must consider personal conduct. We may never have stood physically in front of children being brought to Jesus, yet the principle requires us to examine the ways our words, attitudes, judgments, and decisions affect people seeking the Lord. Have we communicated that certain people should remain distant from Jesus until they satisfy our expectations? Have we treated a person’s past as though the past determines whether the gospel may be offered? Have we allowed inherited beliefs to prevent careful examination of Scripture? Have we assumed that our position within a church gives us authority to make decisions Christ never entrusted to us? These questions require humility rather than defensiveness. The purpose of correction is not merely to expose failure, but to bring our understanding and conduct into alignment with the Lord. The disciples were not instructed to defend their decision. They were told to stop hindering those who were coming. Their response needed to change. The church must likewise remain willing to receive correction whenever its actions contradict the mission and heart of Christ. Our calling is not fulfilled merely by maintaining religious order, preserving familiar practices, or defending our reputation. Our calling is fulfilled through faithful obedience to the Lord, the proclamation of His gospel, the making of disciples, and the love through which His character becomes evident. The invitation remains His. The responsibility to proclaim remains ours. The authority to save belongs to Him alone. We were not asked to decide who was worthy of hearing His invitation. We were not commissioned to make ourselves the guardians of His mercy. We were called to follow the One who receives those who come, to proclaim the truth He entrusted to us, and to serve without claiming authority that belongs exclusively to Him. The disciples learned that lesson when Jesus corrected them. The church must continue learning the same lesson whenever our assumptions threaten to obstruct those seeking the Savior. We were asked to follow Him. We were asked to carry His message. We were asked to make disciples. We were not asked to stand in His way.
