CHAPTER ONE We Were Not Asked The encounter in Mark 10:13–16 exposes a mistake that can arise wherever responsibility is confused with authority. The disciples were close to Jesus and had been entrusted with meaningful work, yet they acted in a way He corrected. Their response reminds us that serving within a ministry does not make every decision right, nor does responsibility permit us to assume authority Christ has not given. Jesus’ instruction was direct: allow the children to come, and do not hinder them. The original encounter concerned children, and the passage should not be stretched beyond its context. The principle nevertheless invites us to examine the way we respond to people seeking Christ and whether our actions add resistance He did not command. The account does not tell us why the disciples acted as they did, and we should not claim certainty about their motives. What Scripture establishes is enough: their response was wrong, and Jesus corrected them. The lesson does not depend upon reconstructing their reasoning. The correction was not directed toward the children or those bringing them. Jesus did not require the people to defend their request, explain their circumstances, or satisfy the disciples before approaching. He addressed the obstruction and welcomed those who had been hindered. That order matters. The people seeking Him were not made responsible for correcting the misunderstanding of His followers. The authority to receive belonged to Christ The disciples were part of Jesus’ ministry, but they did not possess authority to decide that He should not receive the children. Their position did not make their judgment correct. They were servants within a mission directed by Christ, and their action contradicted what He wanted. The church has responsibilities that must be taken seriously. We are called to proclaim the gospel, teach Scripture, make disciples, provide pastoral care, and address matters of conduct and accountability. Those responsibilities do not make us owners of the people we serve. They do not authorize us to replace the direction of Christ with personal preference, nor to make our approval the condition for someone beginning to seek Him. Religious service can create a sense of ownership when people invest time, energy, resources, and emotion in a congregation. They may teach, lead, counsel, worship, organize, and care for others. Such service can be faithful and valuable. Yet the church belongs to Christ. The message belongs to Christ. The people who come seeking Him remain people whom God knows and whom we are called to serve. The title of this book is not a declaration that the church has no authority or that every decision is automatically wrong. It is a reminder that authority has boundaries. We were asked to follow Christ and carry His message. We were not asked to take possession of His invitation or make ourselves the final gate through which people must pass to reach Him. The obstruction was unnecessary The disciples’ conduct created resistance where Jesus had given no instruction to create one. That distinction remains important: a barrier may arise not from a biblical command or a genuine responsibility, but from a decision someone assumes they are entitled to make. Obstruction is not always a formal prohibition. A dismissive response, an assumption about someone's motives, or a conversation conducted about them rather than with them can discourage a person from seeking help or asking questions. Conduct can communicate rejection even when no policy says the person is unwelcome. We should not assume that every difficult encounter is evidence of wrongdoing, nor that every person who feels uncomfortable has been treated unjustly. Ministry can involve complex decisions, legitimate boundaries, and responsibilities that require discernment. The question is whether the barrier serves a responsibility grounded in Scripture or has arisen from personal discomfort, fear, preference, or an assumption that has never been examined. A person seeking Jesus should not be made to believe that human approval is the prerequisite for approaching Him. The gospel calls people to repentance, faith, obedience, and transformed lives. Those calls belong to the message we proclaim. Our own approval does not belong among the conditions we invent. The difference between welcoming and discipleship Jesus’ welcome of the children does not mean that His teaching or the demands of following Him can be ignored. The passage is not a complete statement about every doctrine, behavior, ministry role, or church decision. Its immediate concern is clear: the children were being hindered, and Jesus commanded His disciples to stop. The church must preserve the distinction between allowing people to hear the gospel and teaching them to live as disciples. We are responsible to proclaim the message faithfully and to teach the commandments of Christ. We should not weaken Scripture to avoid a difficult conversation. Nor should we make the beginning of an encounter unnecessarily difficult by treating our expectations as though Jesus Himself had established them. Welcome can be honest. A congregation can explain its convictions, expectations, and responsibilities without humiliating people or making private circumstances public. Teaching can be clear without contempt. Correction can be necessary without becoming an occasion to shame. Boundaries may be appropriate when they serve genuine responsibilities, but boundaries should not be confused with barriers created merely because someone is unfamiliar or uncomfortable to us. The way we respond matters Jesus’ indignation reveals that the disciples’ action mattered. The people were being hindered, and He addressed the conduct of those who had caused the obstruction. Sincere intentions could not make the response acceptable. A person may believe a decision is protective or necessary and still need correction. The church should consider the effect of its conduct, not only the explanation offered for it. Have careless words discouraged someone from seeking God? Have we allowed a preference to become an expectation imposed upon others? Have we spoken about someone without giving them an opportunity to be heard? These questions do not settle every situation, but they belong in honest self-examination. When we discover that our conduct has been wrong, faithfulness requires more than defending our intention. We may need to acknowledge what happened, listen to the person affected, apologize, correct misinformation, or change a practice. Such actions do not diminish biblical conviction. They demonstrate that our conviction is willing to submit to the commands of Christ. The people were received Jesus’ welcome made clear what the disciples had failed to recognize. The children were not left defined by the obstruction; Christ gave them the attention and blessing they had been brought to receive. That movement should shape the way we think about ministry. Removing an unnecessary barrier is important, but a congregation should also consider what faithful welcome looks like. People need to be treated with dignity, listened to, taught, prayed for, and served. They should not be reduced to a history, a question, a category, or a disagreement. The church is not called to make every person’s journey effortless, but to represent Christ faithfully as people encounter the gospel. The people we meet may have questions we cannot answer immediately. Some may be carrying experiences we do not know. Others may need pastoral care, accountability, or time to understand what discipleship requires. We can acknowledge those realities without treating the person as an interruption. Listening gives us a better understanding of the need; faithful teaching provides direction; compassion makes the manner of our ministry consistent with the Savior we proclaim. The correction remains The disciples were told to stop hindering those coming to Jesus. The correction was not complicated, yet receiving correction can be difficult when our decisions have become attached to our identity, reputation, or position. We may be tempted to explain why we acted, defend the tradition we followed, or point toward the mistakes of someone else. The passage calls us to a more honest response. We must be willing to examine whether our actions have added an obstacle that Christ did not command. We must be willing to distinguish Scripture from assumptions and legitimate responsibilities from personal control. When the Lord exposes a misunderstanding, the faithful response is to receive correction and change course. The invitation belongs to Christ. The responsibility to proclaim remains ours. We are to follow Him, teach His Word, make disciples, love our neighbors, and serve with humility. We were not appointed to decide who is worthy of hearing His invitation or to become guardians of His mercy. The disciples’ mistake reminds us that following Jesus does not give us permission to stand between Him and those seeking His presence. We were asked to follow Him and carry His message. We were not asked to stand in His way.
