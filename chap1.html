CHAPTER ONE We Were Not Asked The disciples’ response to the children being brought to Jesus exposes a mistake that can arise wherever responsibility is confused with authority. People came hoping Jesus would touch and bless their children. The disciples rebuked those bringing them, acting as though they had the right to decide whether the approach should continue. Jesus corrected them. Their position among His followers did not authorize them to regulate an encounter He was willing to receive. Mark records that Jesus became indignant and instructed the disciples to allow the children to come, telling them not to hinder them (Mark 10:13–16). The correction was direct. The disciples had assumed a role that did not belong to them, and their actions conflicted with the Lord’s intention. Scripture does not explain why the disciples responded as they did. They may have thought they were maintaining order or protecting Jesus from demands upon His time, but the text does not establish their motives. What it does establish is that Jesus rejected their response. We need no imagined explanation to understand the central issue: they attempted to prevent an approach He welcomed. Sincere intention does not make every decision faithful. A person may believe a response is necessary and still be wrong about the responsibility being exercised. Good intentions cannot create authority, and religious service does not place anyone beyond correction. The disciples were followers of Jesus, yet in this moment their conduct required Him to intervene. The presumption of a role never given Authority has a rightful place within the church. Scripture describes responsibilities of teaching, shepherding, correction, leadership, and accountability. Those responsibilities are not the problem. The problem begins when we use a role entrusted to us for purposes beyond its design, or when personal judgment is treated as though the Lord Himself has spoken. A teacher is responsible for helping people understand Scripture, not replacing the Word with personal preference. A shepherd is called to care for the flock, not claim ownership over Christ’s invitation. A minister may address doctrine and conduct, yet must remain accountable to the Scriptures and to the One who called him or her to serve. The disciples’ decision illustrates how quickly responsibility can become presumption. They were present with Jesus, but presence did not grant them authority to determine who could approach Him. They acted without recorded instruction to do so, and Jesus made clear that their action was unacceptable. The same danger can develop in religious life when position begins to make decisions appear unquestionable. A leader’s conclusion may be accepted without examining the Scripture involved. A family conviction may be carried forward for generations until questioning the interpretation feels like questioning faith itself. A congregation may develop expectations about who is welcome, whose presence is acceptable, or who deserves attention without distinguishing those expectations from the teaching of Christ. Respect for leadership does not require abandoning biblical examination. Loyalty to a church family does not mean every inherited interpretation must remain beyond reconsideration. Scripture must remain the authority by which our beliefs and conduct are tested. Humility includes recognizing that our understanding, however sincere, may need correction. The invitation was not theirs to manage The children were being brought to Jesus. Their purpose was not to establish their standing with the disciples, but to encounter the Lord. The disciples introduced resistance that Jesus had not requested, and He removed that resistance by calling the children near. The invitation belonged to Christ. The authority to receive them belonged to Him. This distinction matters when the church encounters people whose lives, questions, histories, or circumstances differ from our expectations. Such differences do not transfer ownership of Christ’s invitation to the people observing them. The church is entrusted with proclaiming the gospel and making disciples; Jesus remains the Savior who receives, convicts, transforms, and directs those who come. A person seeking Jesus should not be made to believe that personal acceptability to another human being is the prerequisite for approaching Him. The gospel calls people to repentance, faith, obedience, and a transformed life. Those calls must be communicated faithfully, without turning our own approval into a barrier that Christ has not established. The passage does not resolve every question concerning doctrine, conduct, or discipleship. Nor does the welcome Jesus extended mean that His teaching or the call to follow Him can be ignored. Mark’s account addresses the obstruction before Him: the children were being hindered, and He commanded the disciples to stop. Our application must preserve that distinction. We are called to teach what Scripture teaches, while refusing to presume that our personal judgments give us control over the beginning of someone’s encounter with Christ. When familiarity becomes confidence without examination The disciples had already heard Jesus teach and had witnessed His ministry. Their proximity did not prevent misunderstanding. That detail should make every believer cautious about assuming that years of service, extensive knowledge, or familiarity with religious language guarantees that our response in a particular moment reflects the heart of Christ. Familiarity can influence how we read Scripture. A passage may be quoted repeatedly without careful attention to context. An interpretation may be defended because respected people have held the position, rather than because the believer has examined the text. A custom may become so established that its origin and purpose are no longer considered. These practices are not automatically wrong, but they demonstrate why faithfulness requires more than repetition. The Scriptures are not merely a collection of passages used to defend conclusions already reached. They must also examine our beliefs, motives, attitudes, and actions. We should approach the Word willing to learn, not simply searching for confirmation. A sincere believer can misunderstand, and correction is not a threat to faith when the correction leads us toward obedience. The disciples’ mistake was not recorded to establish a new group of people for us to condemn. Their experience reveals that followers of Jesus remain in need of instruction. When the Lord exposes an error in our conduct, we should respond with the same readiness He required of them: stop what is wrong, receive correction, and change course. The mission does not belong to us Jesus’ commission to make disciples of all nations establishes the church’s responsibility to carry the gospel, baptize, and teach people to observe His commandments (Matthew 28:18–20). The commission places believers in the role of witnesses and servants. We carry the message; we do not become its source or the owners of the invitation. People encountered through ministry may have different backgrounds, cultures, questions, and experiences. The messenger cannot assume that familiarity determines who should hear the gospel. Nor should discomfort with someone different from ourselves be mistaken for a biblical prohibition against reaching that person. The mission calls the church outward. Our responsibility is to proclaim Christ faithfully and teach those who respond. That work requires discernment, patience, and compassion, but it does not authorize us to place ourselves in control of whom Jesus receives. The correction we must be willing to receive Jesus addressed the disciples’ obstruction rather than asking the people bringing the children to solve the disciples’ misunderstanding. He told His followers to stop hindering them, then received the children. His response makes the responsibility clear: those who created the obstruction needed to change their conduct. The principle requires personal examination. We may never have stood physically in the way of children approaching Jesus, yet our words, attitudes, judgments, and decisions can discourage people seeking the Lord. Have we made someone feel that the gospel was beyond their reach until they satisfied our expectations? Have we treated a person’s past as though the past determined whether grace could be offered? Have we mistaken our position, tradition, or preference for authority established by Scripture? Such questions should lead us toward humility rather than defensiveness. Correction is not simply exposure of failure; correction is an opportunity to bring our understanding and conduct into alignment with Christ. The disciples were not instructed to defend their decision. They were instructed to stop hindering those who were coming. The church must remain willing to receive the same correction. Our calling is not fulfilled merely by preserving familiar practices or defending our reputation. We are called to obey the Lord, proclaim His gospel, make disciples, and love people with the care and dignity Scripture requires. The invitation remains Christ’s, and the responsibility to proclaim remains ours. The authority to save belongs to Him alone. We were not asked to decide who was worthy of hearing His invitation or to become guardians of His mercy. We were asked to follow the One who receives those who come, faithfully teach the Word He entrusted to us, and serve without claiming authority that belongs exclusively to Him. The disciples learned through correction that following Jesus did not give them permission to stand between Him and those seeking His presence. The church must remain willing to learn that lesson. We were asked to follow Him and carry His message. We were not asked to stand in His way.
