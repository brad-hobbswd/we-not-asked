PART I · THE AUTHORITY WE ASSUMED

We Were Not Asked

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 1 of 17Reading edition

Mark gives us a scene that does not require much explanation to understand. People bring children to Jesus. The disciples rebuke them. Jesus becomes indignant and tells the disciples to stop hindering the children. Then He receives and blesses them. Somewhere between the people seeking Jesus and Jesus welcoming them, His own followers had created resistance.

We do not know why the disciples acted as they did. Mark does not tell us whether they thought the moment was too busy, whether they were trying to protect Jesus’ time, or whether something else was behind their response. Guessing at their motives can take us away from what the passage actually says. They hindered the children, and Jesus told them not to.

That is the part worth sitting with. The disciples were close to Jesus and had been given responsibilities in His ministry. Yet, in this moment, they acted against what He wanted. Their position did not make their judgment right. Their intention, whatever it may have been, did not excuse the result.

Church life can create a similar confusion. We invest in a congregation, serve for years, teach, lead, organize, counsel, and give. Over time, that investment may make us feel that we know what should happen and who should be allowed to participate. Experience can be useful, but experience is not ownership. The church belongs to Christ, and those who serve are still servants.

Sometimes obstruction is obvious: someone is directly told to leave or is denied access. Other times the message comes through a dismissive tone, a private assumption, an unnecessary demand, or a conversation about a person that never includes the person. A congregation may say the door is open while the way people are treated makes approaching feel impossible.

Not every difficult decision is a barrier, and not every uncomfortable experience means someone has been treated wrongly. Churches must make decisions about safety, teaching, conduct, and accountability. The question is whether a decision is grounded in a genuine responsibility and Scripture, or whether personal preference has quietly taken the place of a reason.

The disciples were corrected by Jesus. The people bringing the children were not required to fix the disciples’ misunderstanding before receiving His attention. That matters when we realize our own conduct has hurt someone. We cannot make the affected person responsible for convincing us that we need to listen. We need to be willing to hear, acknowledge, and change.

Welcome does not mean hiding what we believe. A church can be honest about its convictions and still speak with dignity. Teaching can be clear without humiliating anyone. Accountability can be serious without contempt. The beginning of a person's encounter with the gospel should not be made unnecessarily difficult by obstacles we have added ourselves.

The scene ends with Jesus doing what the disciples had tried to prevent. He receives the children and blesses them. The focus returns to Him, where it belongs. Our responsibility is not to manage His invitation, but to represent Him faithfully. When we discover that we have stood in the way, the faithful response is to move, receive correction, and let people see Christ.
