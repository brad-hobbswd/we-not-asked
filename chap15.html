Chapter 15 Truth Without Human Obstruction Truth does not need to be protected through cruelty. The Word of God does not become more powerful when delivered with contempt, and biblical conviction does not become more faithful when accompanied by humiliation. Scripture remains authoritative regardless of the temperament of the person communicating it. The responsibility of the believer is to represent the truth faithfully while allowing the character of Christ to govern the manner in which the truth is presented. Throughout the history of the church, disagreements concerning Scripture, doctrine, morality, tradition, and spiritual authority have shaped the way believers understand their responsibilities. Such disagreements are not always evidence of wrongdoing. Christians are called to study the Word, discern faithfully, teach sound doctrine, and remain accountable to what God has revealed. The difficulty arises when the way we communicate truth begins to contradict the very principles Scripture teaches. A person may speak accurately about repentance while displaying no patience toward someone who needs instruction. Someone may quote Scripture while using the passage to embarrass another person. A leader may insist upon obedience while refusing accountability for personal decisions. A believer may defend holiness while speaking about another human being in a manner that disregards dignity. The presence of Scripture in a conversation does not automatically make the conversation faithful. The truth of God’s Word deserves reverence. The people receiving that Word deserve to be treated with dignity. The church must understand both responsibilities rather than using one to excuse the neglect of the other. Truth Does Not Require Our Pride Jesus never demonstrated insecurity concerning the truth He taught. He spoke with authority, corrected misunderstanding, confronted hypocrisy, and called people toward repentance. His authority did not depend upon the approval of the crowds, the recognition of religious leaders, or the ability to win every argument. The confidence of Christ was rooted in His identity and purpose. Believers, however, can sometimes approach disagreement as though the truth itself were threatened by the presence of another person’s questions. The conversation becomes a contest. The goal shifts from understanding Scripture to demonstrating personal superiority. The person asking the question becomes an opponent, and the opportunity to teach becomes an opportunity to win. Pride can distort the way truth is communicated. Someone who is genuinely seeking understanding may be treated as though they are challenging the authority of God. A person who disagrees may be dismissed before their reasoning is considered. A question that could have led to meaningful discussion may be met with an answer designed to end the conversation rather than illuminate the subject. The believer must remember that confidence in Scripture does not require confidence in every personal interpretation. We remain students of the Word. We remain accountable to God. We remain capable of misunderstanding, misapplying, overlooking context, and allowing personal assumptions to influence our conclusions. Humility does not weaken conviction. Humility recognizes that the authority belongs to God rather than to the individual attempting to explain His Word. A believer who understands this can approach difficult conversations without needing to defend personal pride. The purpose of biblical instruction is not to establish the superiority of the teacher. The purpose is to help people understand truth and live faithfully before God. When pride becomes the governing influence, even correct statements may be communicated in a manner that obstructs understanding. When We Use Scripture Without Understanding Its Purpose The Word of God should never be reduced to isolated sentences used to settle personal disagreements. Scripture was given to reveal God, teach His people, establish truth, correct error, guide conduct, and direct humanity toward the purposes of God. The passages we quote belong within the larger message of the Bible, and faithful interpretation requires attention to context, audience, purpose, and the relationship between the passage and the rest of Scripture. A verse removed from its context can be used to support conclusions the passage was never intended to establish. A warning concerning sin can become an excuse for condemnation without restoration. A passage concerning authority can be used to discourage accountability. A teaching concerning holiness can become a means of elevating personal preferences above the commandments of God. A Scripture concerning correction can be used to justify humiliation. The problem is not the Scripture. The problem is the way Scripture is being handled. Paul instructed Timothy in 2 Timothy 2:15 to be diligent in presenting himself approved to God, a worker who did not need to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth. The instruction emphasizes responsibility in handling God’s Word. The believer must not approach Scripture casually. The responsibility to interpret and communicate truth faithfully requires diligence. This diligence should extend to the passages we use when addressing people whose lives, circumstances, or beliefs differ from our own. We must ask what the passage actually says, what the passage addresses, how the passage fits within the surrounding context, and whether our application faithfully reflects the purpose of the text. We should also recognize the difference between the explicit teaching of Scripture and conclusions we have developed through interpretation, tradition, or personal conviction. Such distinctions do not diminish biblical authority. They protect our communication from claiming more authority than the text itself provides. The church must never use the Word of God as though our personal opinions possess the same authority as Scripture. The Burden of Being Right Without Being Faithful There are conversations in which a person may technically state something accurate while communicating in a manner that damages the purpose of the conversation. A correction may contain truth while being delivered with unnecessary humiliation. A warning may be biblically grounded while being expressed without compassion. A discussion about conduct may become so focused upon the perceived failures of another person that the speaker forgets the need for personal examination. The question cannot be limited to whether a statement was factually correct. We must also consider whether the truth was represented faithfully. Ephesians 4:15 instructs believers to speak the truth in love, growing toward Christ. The passage connects truthfulness with spiritual maturity and the character of the Christian community. Truth and love are not competing responsibilities. Love does not require the believer to abandon truth. Truth does not grant permission to abandon love. The church must reject the idea that a harsh manner demonstrates courage or that compassion necessarily indicates compromise. Such assumptions create unnecessary division between responsibilities Scripture places together. Speaking truth in love requires more than choosing pleasant words. It involves a genuine concern for the person receiving the message. It requires recognizing that the person is not merely an example of a theological disagreement. The person has a life, a history, relationships, questions, and an understanding that may require patient engagement. A believer can be direct without being degrading. A believer can address serious concerns without treating someone as though their dignity has disappeared. A believer can disagree without turning disagreement into a personal attack. The manner in which truth is communicated should reflect the seriousness of the message and the value of the person receiving it. When Tradition Becomes More Influential Than Scripture In Mark 7, Jesus confronted religious leaders concerning traditions that had become influential in their understanding of obedience. The issue involved practices associated with the washing of hands and the broader traditions of the elders. Jesus addressed the way human traditions could become elevated in a manner that compromised the commandment of God. His response was serious. He exposed the danger of allowing religious traditions to take precedence over what God had commanded. The problem was not that traditions existed. The problem was the authority those traditions were given when they interfered with obedience to God. This passage remains important because religious communities can develop expectations that become deeply ingrained over time. People may begin to assume that a particular way of speaking, dressing, worshiping, responding, or participating represents the only faithful expression of Christianity. Practices that once served a purpose may eventually become standards by which people judge one another’s spiritual condition. Traditions can provide continuity, structure, and shared identity. They can help communities remember important principles and preserve meaningful practices. Yet traditions must remain subject to Scripture. The church must be willing to examine the difference between what God has commanded and what a community has become accustomed to requiring. A preference may be meaningful without being universally binding. A custom may be valuable without becoming a measure of salvation. A practice may be familiar without representing the full extent of biblical faithfulness. The danger arises when people are judged according to standards that Scripture does not establish as universal requirements. Jesus’ confrontation in Mark 7 reminds believers that religious familiarity can create confidence without guaranteeing faithful interpretation. The church must continually examine its traditions rather than assuming that the age, popularity, or familiarity of a practice establishes its authority. The Word of God must remain the standard. Correction Should Lead Toward Restoration The Bible does not avoid the responsibility of correction. Scripture repeatedly addresses sin, accountability, discipline, repentance, and restoration. The church must teach these responsibilities faithfully. However, correction should not become an excuse for treating people carelessly. The purpose of correction matters. The spirit in which correction is offered matters. The willingness of the person offering correction to examine their own conduct matters. Galatians 6:1 instructs believers that when someone is caught in a wrongdoing, those who are spiritual should restore the person in a spirit of gentleness while considering themselves. The passage places restoration at the center of the responsibility. The instruction does not encourage believers to approach someone else’s failure with arrogance. It reminds the person offering correction that their own vulnerability must remain in view. The believer who corrects another person is not standing outside the need for grace. The believer who offers instruction remains accountable to God. The believer who addresses wrongdoing must recognize that personal conduct can also require correction. A church that understands restoration will not treat correction as an opportunity to establish superiority. The purpose is to help someone return toward faithful living, not to create a public demonstration of the corrector’s authority. There may be circumstances requiring serious intervention. Some situations involve safety, misconduct, abuse, or responsibilities that demand immediate and appropriate action. Gentleness should never be confused with neglecting protection or accountability. Even in serious circumstances, the church should communicate responsibly, protect those affected, avoid unnecessary humiliation, and pursue justice according to appropriate procedures. Faithful correction must never become careless cruelty. The Difference Between Conviction and Condemnation Conviction and condemnation are frequently discussed as though the terms always describe identical experiences. Scripture provides a more careful distinction. Conviction can bring awareness of wrongdoing and lead a person toward repentance, correction, and reconciliation with God. The recognition of sin may be uncomfortable, yet discomfort can serve a meaningful purpose when someone is responding to the truth. Condemnation, however, can become a message that communicates hopelessness, permanent rejection, or the belief that a person has no meaningful opportunity for mercy. The church must be careful about the messages communicated through its words and conduct. A person who needs correction should not be led to believe that correction means the end of hope. A person who recognizes wrongdoing should be directed toward repentance and the mercy of God rather than treated as though their failure has permanently removed the possibility of restoration. Romans 8:1 declares that there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. The surrounding context addresses the work of God through Christ, the contrast between life according to the flesh and life according to the Spirit, and the freedom found through the work of Jesus. The passage does not encourage believers to ignore sin. The broader chapter addresses the transforming work of the Spirit and the life of those who belong to Christ. The church must communicate the reality of sin without obscuring the reality of grace. People should understand the seriousness of repentance while also understanding that the gospel announces hope through Jesus Christ. A message that communicates only accusation may fail to explain the purpose of the Savior. The church must faithfully communicate both the need for salvation and the hope of salvation. When We Confuse Our Interpretation With God’s Final Word Every believer must take Scripture seriously. The responsibility of studying, interpreting, and applying the Word of God is not optional for those who seek to live faithfully. However, the seriousness of biblical interpretation should produce humility rather than arrogance. People may disagree concerning the interpretation of passages, the application of principles, the relationship between particular texts, or the implications of biblical teachings for contemporary circumstances. Such disagreements require thoughtful examination rather than immediate assumptions about the spiritual condition of everyone involved. A person may sincerely hold a conviction while still needing to examine the reasoning supporting that conviction. A person may confidently teach an interpretation while overlooking important context. A person may inherit a conclusion from a religious community without ever having studied the passages independently. The church should encourage believers to examine Scripture carefully and responsibly. The authority of God’s Word should lead us toward greater diligence, not less. We should be willing to study passages that challenge our assumptions. We should be willing to reconsider applications that have been shaped more by tradition than by Scripture. We should be willing to acknowledge when our understanding requires correction. Such willingness is not weakness. The person who can say, “I need to examine that passage more carefully,” demonstrates a commitment to truth that is greater than the desire to preserve personal certainty. The church should cultivate an environment where faithful study is welcomed, where questions are addressed thoughtfully, and where disagreement does not automatically become a declaration of spiritual failure. The goal is not to make Scripture less authoritative. The goal is to ensure that our interpretations remain accountable to Scripture. Truth Should Never Become a Weapon Against Human Dignity People have sometimes experienced Scripture being used in conversations where their humanity seemed to disappear. Their circumstances become the subject of debate. Their lives become illustrations. Their questions become accusations. Their presence becomes an opportunity for someone to demonstrate theological certainty. The person may leave the conversation feeling that no one was interested in understanding them as a human being. Such experiences should cause the church to examine its conduct. Biblical truth should never be used to justify dehumanization. The church must not speak about people as though they are objects of contempt, problems to be solved, or examples of everything the congregation opposes. Every person is created by God and possesses human dignity. The recognition of dignity does not require believers to approve every choice or abandon every conviction. The recognition of dignity requires believers to remember that people are not disposable and that the manner in which we treat others remains subject to the commandments of Christ. Jesus taught His followers to love their neighbors. He called them to mercy, humility, forgiveness, and service. He demonstrated concern for people who were suffering, rejected, misunderstood, and marginalized. The church must not use its commitment to biblical truth as a reason to disregard the biblical responsibility to love. The two responsibilities belong together. The Gospel Must Not Be Distorted by the Messenger The gospel is the message of Jesus Christ, His death, His resurrection, and the salvation offered through Him. The church has been entrusted with proclaiming this message. Our responsibility is serious, and our conduct should reflect the seriousness of that trust. When a believer communicates truth through pride, contempt, humiliation, or unnecessary hostility, the message may become obscured by the conduct of the messenger. The person listening may struggle to understand how the words being spoken relate to the actions being demonstrated. The church should never assume that accurate quotations automatically produce faithful representation. The message deserves careful teaching. The people listening deserve thoughtful engagement. The believer communicating the message deserves the responsibility of self examination. We must consider whether our words accurately represent Scripture, whether our interpretations are responsibly grounded, whether our attitudes reflect the character of Christ, and whether our conduct supports the purpose of the gospel. The responsibility is not to make every conversation easy. The responsibility is to remain faithful. Some conversations will involve disagreement. Some will require correction. Some will reveal circumstances requiring accountability. Some will challenge our understanding. Some will require patience beyond what we initially expected. The church must remain committed to truth throughout those circumstances without allowing pride to become the governing influence. Removing the Obstruction Without Removing the Truth The church must not respond to the failures of religious conduct by abandoning Scripture. The solution to harshness is not indifference. The solution to judgmental attitudes is not the refusal to address wrongdoing. The solution to misuse of authority is not the abandonment of responsible leadership. The solution to religious pride is not the rejection of biblical conviction. The solution is a return to faithful representation of Christ. We must teach the Word carefully. We must examine context. We must distinguish God’s commandments from personal preferences. We must remain accountable. We must recognize the dignity of people whose lives differ from our own. We must communicate correction with humility. We must pursue restoration whenever appropriate. We must refuse to use spiritual authority as a means of protecting personal pride. Truth remains truth. The church does not need to add humiliation to make truth powerful. The church does not need to add suspicion to make discernment responsible. The church does not need to add contempt to make conviction clear. The church does not need to create unnecessary barriers to demonstrate holiness. Our responsibility is to faithfully carry what God has given us. A Faithful Witness The people who encounter the church should be able to recognize that biblical truth is taken seriously. They should also be able to recognize that the people communicating truth understand their responsibility to love, listen, serve, and remain accountable. A faithful witness does not require perfect people. The church is composed of people who continue to depend upon grace, seek understanding, receive correction, and grow in Christ. A faithful witness requires honesty concerning our failures. A faithful witness requires humility concerning our interpretations. A faithful witness requires compassion toward people whose circumstances we may not fully understand. A faithful witness requires courage to address wrongdoing without using wrongdoing as an excuse to abandon dignity. The Word of God deserves to be handled faithfully. The people hearing the Word deserve to be treated responsibly. The church must never allow its manner of communication to contradict the message it proclaims. Truth should lead people toward understanding God. Truth should reveal the need for repentance. Truth should direct people toward the Savior. Truth should be communicated with the humility of people who recognize that grace has also been extended to them. We were entrusted with the gospel. We were entrusted with Scripture. We were entrusted with the responsibility of teaching, serving, loving, and making disciples. We were never entrusted with permission to distort the truth through pride, misrepresent Christ through contempt, or create unnecessary obstacles through the misuse of authority. The truth does not need human obstruction. The truth needs faithful witnesses.
