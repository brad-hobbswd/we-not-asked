PART IV · RETURNING TO THE HEART OF JESUS

Truth Without Human Obstruction

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 15 of 17Reading edition

Truth does not need to be protected through cruelty. The Word of God does not become more powerful when delivered with contempt, and biblical conviction does not become more faithful when accompanied by humiliation. Scripture remains authoritative regardless of the temperament of the person communicating it. The responsibility of believers is to handle the Word faithfully while recognizing that the people receiving the Word deserve dignity.

Disagreements concerning Scripture, doctrine, morality, tradition, and spiritual authority have shaped the way believers understand their responsibilities. Such disagreements are not always evidence of wrongdoing. Christians are called to study the Word, discern faithfully, teach sound doctrine, and remain accountable to God. The difficulty arises when the way we communicate truth begins to contradict the very principles Scripture teaches.

A person may speak accurately about repentance while displaying no patience toward someone who needs instruction. Someone may quote Scripture while using the passage to embarrass another person. A leader may insist upon obedience while refusing accountability for personal decisions. A believer may defend holiness while treating someone with contempt. The truth of God’s Word deserves reverence, and the people receiving that Word deserve to be treated with dignity. Neither responsibility should be used to excuse neglect of the other.

Jesus never demonstrated insecurity concerning the truth He taught. He spoke with authority, corrected misunderstanding, confronted hypocrisy, and called people toward repentance. His authority did not depend upon the approval of crowds, the recognition of religious leaders, or the ability to win every argument. Believers, however, can sometimes approach disagreement as though the truth itself were threatened by another person’s questions. The conversation becomes a contest, and the goal shifts from understanding Scripture to demonstrating personal superiority. The person asking the question becomes an opponent rather than someone who may need instruction.

Someone genuinely seeking understanding may be treated as though they are challenging the authority of God. A person who disagrees may be dismissed before their reasoning is considered. A question that could have led to meaningful discussion may receive an answer designed to end the conversation rather than explain the Word.

The believer must remember that confidence in Scripture does not require confidence in every personal interpretation. We remain students of the Word, accountable to God and capable of misunderstanding, misapplying, overlooking context, and allowing personal assumptions to influence our conclusions. Humility recognizes that the authority belongs to God rather than to the individual attempting to explain His Word.

A believer who understands this can approach difficult conversations without needing to defend personal pride. The purpose of biblical instruction is not to establish the superiority of the teacher. The purpose is to help people understand truth and live faithfully before God. When pride becomes the governing influence, even correct statements may be communicated in a manner that obstructs understanding.

Handling the Word responsibly

Scripture was given to reveal God, teach His people, establish truth, correct error, guide conduct, and direct humanity toward His purposes. The passages we quote belong within the larger message of the Bible, and faithful interpretation requires attention to context, audience, purpose, and the relationship between one passage and the rest of Scripture.

A verse removed from its context can be used to support conclusions the passage was never intended to establish. A warning concerning sin can become an excuse for condemnation without restoration. A passage concerning authority can be used to discourage accountability. A teaching concerning holiness can become a means of elevating personal preferences above the commandments of God. A Scripture concerning correction can be used to justify humiliation.

Paul instructed Timothy in 2 Timothy 2:15 (AMPC) to be diligent in presenting himself approved to God, a worker who did not need to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth. The instruction emphasizes responsibility in handling God’s Word. Believers must not approach Scripture casually. The responsibility to interpret and communicate truth faithfully requires diligence, including when addressing people whose lives, circumstances, or beliefs differ from our own.

We must ask what a passage actually says, what circumstances it addresses, how the passage fits within its surrounding context, and whether our application faithfully reflects the purpose of the text. We should also recognize the difference between explicit teaching in Scripture and conclusions developed through interpretation, tradition, or personal conviction. Such distinctions do not diminish biblical authority. They protect our communication from claiming more authority than the text itself provides.

The church must never use the Word of God as though personal opinions possess the same authority as Scripture.

Truth and love belong together

There are conversations in which a person may technically state something accurate while communicating in a manner that damages the purpose of the conversation. A correction may contain truth while being delivered with unnecessary humiliation. A warning may be biblically grounded while expressed without compassion. A discussion about conduct may become so focused upon another person’s perceived failures that the speaker forgets the need for personal examination.

Ephesians 4:15 (AMPC) instructs believers to speak the truth in love, growing toward Christ. The passage connects truthfulness with spiritual maturity and the character of the Christian community. Love does not require the believer to abandon truth. Truth does not grant permission to abandon love.

The church must reject the idea that a harsh manner demonstrates courage or that compassion necessarily indicates compromise. Speaking truth in love requires more than choosing pleasant words. Speaking truth in love involves genuine concern for the person receiving the message, who is not merely an example of a theological disagreement. They have a life, history, relationships, questions, and understanding that may require patient engagement.

A believer can address serious concerns without treating someone as though their dignity has disappeared. The manner in which truth is communicated should reflect the seriousness of the message and the value of the person receiving it.

When tradition is elevated

In Mark 7:6–13, Jesus confronted religious leaders concerning traditions that had become influential in their understanding of obedience. The issue involved practices associated with the washing of hands and broader traditions of the elders. Jesus addressed the way human traditions could become elevated in a manner that compromised the commandment of God.

The problem was not simply that traditions existed. The problem was the authority those traditions were given when they interfered with obedience to God. This passage remains important because religious communities can develop expectations that become deeply ingrained over time. People may begin to assume that a particular way of speaking, dressing, worshiping, responding, or participating represents the only faithful expression of Christianity. Practices that once served a purpose may eventually become standards by which people judge one another’s spiritual condition.

Traditions can provide continuity, structure, and shared identity. They can help communities remember important principles and preserve meaningful practices. Yet traditions must remain subject to Scripture. The church must be willing to examine the difference between what God has commanded and what a community has become accustomed to requiring.

The danger arises when people are judged according to standards Scripture does not establish as universal requirements. Jesus’ confrontation in Mark 7 reminds believers that religious familiarity can create confidence without guaranteeing faithful interpretation. The church must continually examine its traditions rather than assume that the age, popularity, or familiarity of a practice establishes its authority.

Correction must seek restoration

The Bible does not avoid the responsibility of correction. Scripture repeatedly addresses sin, accountability, discipline, repentance, and restoration. Correction should not become an excuse for treating people carelessly. The purpose of correction matters, the spirit in which correction is offered matters, and the willingness of the person offering correction to examine their own conduct matters.

Scripture directs correction toward restoration and requires humility from the person offering it. The corrector’s own vulnerability must remain in view; personal conduct may also require examination. The purpose is not to establish superiority, but to help someone return toward faithful living.

Some circumstances require serious intervention. Situations involving safety, misconduct, abuse, or responsibilities that demand immediate action must be addressed appropriately. Gentleness should never be confused with neglecting protection or accountability. Even in serious circumstances, the church should communicate responsibly, protect those affected, avoid unnecessary humiliation, and pursue justice through appropriate procedures.

Faithful correction must not become careless cruelty.

Conviction and condemnation

Conviction and condemnation are frequently discussed as though the terms always describe identical experiences. Scripture provides a more careful distinction. Conviction can bring awareness of wrongdoing and lead a person toward repentance, correction, and reconciliation with God. Recognition of sin may be uncomfortable, yet discomfort can serve a meaningful purpose when someone responds to truth.

Condemnation can become a message communicating hopelessness, permanent rejection, or the belief that mercy is no longer available. A person who needs correction should not be led to believe that correction means the end of hope. Someone who recognizes wrongdoing should be directed toward repentance and the mercy of God rather than treated as though failure has permanently removed the possibility of restoration.

Romans 8:1 (AMPC) declares that there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. The surrounding context addresses the work of God through Christ, the contrast between life according to the flesh and life according to the Spirit, and the freedom found through Jesus. The passage does not encourage believers to ignore sin. The broader chapter addresses the transforming work of the Spirit and the life of those who belong to Christ.

People should understand the seriousness of repentance while also understanding that the gospel announces hope through Jesus Christ. Correction should point toward the possibility of faithful living rather than leave someone believing that their failure defines the entirety of their future.

Our interpretations remain accountable

Every believer must take Scripture seriously. The responsibility of studying, interpreting, and applying the Word of God is not optional for those who seek to live faithfully. People may disagree concerning interpretation, application, the relationship between particular texts, or the implications of biblical teachings for contemporary circumstances. Such disagreements require thoughtful examination rather than immediate assumptions about someone’s spiritual condition.

A person may sincerely hold a conviction while still needing to examine the reasoning supporting that conviction. Someone may inherit a conclusion from a religious community without ever having studied the passages independently. The authority of God’s Word should lead us toward greater diligence, not less. We should be willing to study passages that challenge our assumptions, reconsider applications shaped more by tradition than Scripture, and acknowledge when our understanding requires further examination.

The person who can say, “I need to examine that passage more carefully,” demonstrates a commitment to truth greater than the desire to preserve personal certainty. The church should cultivate an environment where faithful study is welcomed, questions are addressed thoughtfully, and disagreement does not automatically become a declaration of spiritual failure.

The willingness to reconsider an interpretation is not a rejection of biblical authority. A sincere desire to understand Scripture more accurately demonstrates respect for the authority of the Word.

Human dignity must remain

People have sometimes experienced Scripture being used in conversations where their humanity seemed to disappear. Their circumstances become the subject of debate, their lives become illustrations, their questions become accusations, and their presence becomes an opportunity for someone to demonstrate theological certainty. The person may leave feeling that no one was interested in understanding them as a human being.

Biblical truth should never be used to justify dehumanization. The church must not speak about people as though they are objects of contempt, problems to be solved, or examples of everything a congregation opposes. Recognizing dignity does not require believers to approve every choice or abandon every conviction. Recognition of dignity requires remembering that people are not disposable and that the manner in which we treat others remains subject to the commandments of Christ.

Jesus taught His followers to love their neighbors. He called them to mercy, humility, forgiveness, and service. He demonstrated concern for people who were suffering, rejected, misunderstood, and marginalized. The church must not use commitment to biblical truth as a reason to disregard the biblical responsibility to love.

The Word remains our standard

The church must be willing to distinguish what Scripture explicitly teaches, what a passage establishes through its context, and what conclusions we have drawn through interpretation. That distinction is especially important when a decision affects another person. We should not present a personal inference as though the words of Scripture leave no room for examination, nor should uncertainty about an application become an excuse to ignore the clear teaching of the Word.

Careful interpretation requires patience. We may need to read the passage in its setting, compare related Scriptures, consider the audience and purpose, and seek wise counsel. A conclusion reached quickly may still be correct, but confidence alone does not establish accuracy. The desire to be faithful should make us more diligent, not less willing to examine the reasoning behind our position.

A believer who asks for clarification should not be treated as though the question itself were a rejection of God. The church should be able to explain its convictions, acknowledge the distinction between biblical command and congregational practice, and admit when a matter requires further study. Such honesty does not weaken the authority of Scripture. Such honesty demonstrates that Scripture, rather than personal certainty, remains the authority.

The responsibility to handle truth faithfully also requires us to consider the consequences of our application. We should ask whether our teaching accurately reflects the passage, whether our decision is consistent with the principles we claim to uphold, and whether the way we communicate leaves room for responsible questions and correction. Our interpretation must remain accountable to the Word, and our conduct must remain accountable to the commands of Christ.

Truth without human obstruction does not mean truth without responsibility. Truth without human obstruction requires us to stop adding the weight of pride, contempt, personal preference, or unexamined assumptions to the Word God has entrusted to us. We are called to study faithfully, teach carefully, correct humbly, and remain willing to be corrected ourselves.

The authority of Scripture is not diminished when its servants approach it with humility. The Word remains true, and our responsibility is to handle the Word in a manner worthy of the One who gave it. Chapter Sixteen turns from the way truth is communicated to the decisions made by those entrusted with responsibility, asking whether stewardship has remained service or become control.
