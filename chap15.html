CHAPTER FIFTEEN Truth Without Human Obstruction The church must remain faithful to Scripture. The gospel cannot be rewritten according to cultural pressure, personal preference, or the desire to avoid difficult conversations. Repentance, holiness, obedience, salvation, and transformation remain central to Christian discipleship. Truth can nevertheless be obstructed by the way believers handle it. A passage may be quoted without context, a personal interpretation treated as though it were explicit biblical instruction, or correction delivered in a manner that humiliates rather than restores. The problem is not Scripture. The problem is how Scripture is being used. Handling the Word responsibly Paul instructed Timothy to be diligent in presenting himself approved to God, rightly dividing the word of truth (2 Timothy 2:15 (AMPC)). Believers must not approach the Word casually or use isolated sentences to settle disagreements without considering context, audience, purpose, and the relationship of a passage to the rest of Scripture. A warning concerning sin may become an excuse for condemnation without restoration. A passage concerning authority may be used to discourage accountability. A teaching about holiness may elevate personal preferences above God’s commandments. Scripture concerning correction may be used to justify humiliation. The Word remains the authority. Our interpretations remain accountable to the Word. We should ask what a passage actually says, what circumstances it addresses, and whether our application faithfully reflects its purpose. We must recognize the distinction between explicit biblical teaching and conclusions developed through interpretation, tradition, or personal conviction. Such care does not diminish Scripture; it prevents us from claiming more authority than the text provides. Truth and love belong together Ephesians 4:15 (AMPC) calls believers to speak the truth in love and grow toward Christ. Truth and love are not competing responsibilities. Love does not require abandoning truth, and truth does not grant permission to abandon love. A correction may contain accurate information while being delivered with unnecessary humiliation. A warning may be grounded in Scripture yet expressed without compassion. A discussion about conduct may become so focused upon another person’s failures that the speaker forgets the need for personal examination. The person receiving correction is not merely an example of a theological disagreement. They have a life, history, relationships, questions, and understanding that may require patient engagement. A believer can be direct without degrading someone, address serious concerns without treating dignity as expendable, and disagree without turning the conversation into a personal attack. The manner in which truth is communicated should reflect the seriousness of the message and the value of the person receiving it. When tradition is elevated In Mark 7:6–13 (AMPC), Jesus confronted traditions that had been elevated in a way that compromised obedience to God’s commandments. The issue was not simply the existence of tradition, but the authority given to human practices when they interfered with God’s Word. Traditions can provide continuity, structure, and meaningful expressions of faith. However, their age or familiarity does not establish biblical authority. Religious communities may develop expectations concerning speech, dress, worship, participation, or behavior until those practices become standards by which people judge one another’s spiritual condition. A preference may be meaningful without being universally binding. A custom may be valuable without becoming a measure of salvation. A familiar practice may still require examination. The church must distinguish God’s commandments from expectations it has developed. Religious familiarity can create confidence without guaranteeing faithful interpretation. Correction must seek restoration Scripture addresses sin, accountability, discipline, repentance, and restoration. Galatians 6:1 (AMPC) instructs believers to restore someone caught in wrongdoing with gentleness while considering themselves. The passage places restoration at the center and reminds the person offering correction of their own vulnerability. Correction is not an opportunity to establish superiority. The believer who addresses wrongdoing remains accountable to God and may also require correction. The objective should be spiritual growth, not personal satisfaction. Some situations require serious intervention, especially where safety, abuse, misconduct, or protection of others is involved. Gentleness must never become neglect of safeguarding or accountability. Even in serious circumstances, the church should communicate responsibly, protect those affected, avoid unnecessary humiliation, and pursue appropriate procedures. Faithful correction must not become careless cruelty. Conviction and condemnation Conviction can bring awareness of wrongdoing and lead toward repentance, correction, and reconciliation with God. Such recognition may be uncomfortable, yet discomfort can serve a meaningful purpose when someone responds to truth. Condemnation can communicate hopelessness, permanent rejection, or the belief that mercy is no longer available. Romans 8:1 (AMPC) declares there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. The surrounding chapter addresses the work of God through Christ, life according to the Spirit, and the freedom found through Jesus. The passage does not encourage ignoring sin; it places the transforming work of the Spirit within the life of those who belong to Christ. The church must communicate the seriousness of sin without obscuring the reality of grace. People should understand the call to repentance and the hope of salvation. Our interpretations are not God’s final word The seriousness of biblical interpretation should produce humility rather than arrogance. Believers may disagree concerning interpretation, application, the relationship between passages, or the implications of biblical teaching for contemporary circumstances. Such questions require thoughtful examination rather than assumptions about everyone’s spiritual condition. A person may sincerely hold a conviction and still need to examine the reasoning behind it. An interpretation may overlook context. A conclusion may have been inherited without independent study. The church should encourage careful examination and remain willing to reconsider applications shaped more by tradition than Scripture. The willingness to say, “I need to examine that passage more carefully,” demonstrates a commitment to truth greater than the desire to preserve personal certainty. Faithful study should be welcomed, questions addressed thoughtfully, and disagreement should not automatically become a declaration of spiritual failure. The goal is not to make Scripture less authoritative. The goal is to ensure our interpretations remain accountable to Scripture. Human dignity must remain People have sometimes experienced Scripture being used in conversations where their humanity seemed to disappear. Their circumstances became the subject of debate, their lives became illustrations, and their questions became accusations. They left feeling that no one was interested in understanding them. Biblical truth must never be used to justify dehumanization. People are not objects of contempt or examples of everything a congregation opposes. Every person is created by God and possesses human dignity. Recognizing dignity does not require approving every choice or abandoning conviction. It requires remembering that people are not disposable and that our treatment of others remains subject to Christ’s commandments. The church must not use its commitment to truth as a reason to disregard its responsibility to love. A faithful witness The gospel is the message of Jesus Christ, His death, resurrection, and salvation offered through Him. The church has been entrusted with proclaiming this message, and our conduct should reflect the seriousness of that trust. When truth is communicated through pride, contempt, humiliation, or unnecessary hostility, the message may become obscured by the messenger. We must consider whether our words represent Scripture accurately, whether our interpretations are responsibly grounded, whether our attitudes reflect Christ, and whether our conduct supports the purpose of the gospel. Some conversations involve disagreement, correction, accountability, or circumstances that challenge our understanding. Faithfulness does not require making every conversation easy. It requires remaining truthful, humble, and responsible. The church must not remove Scripture to avoid discomfort. Neither should we add humiliation to make truth appear powerful. Discernment does not require suspicion, conviction does not require contempt, and holiness does not require dehumanization. Our responsibility is to teach the Word carefully, examine context, distinguish commandments from preferences, remain accountable, recognize the dignity of people whose lives differ from ours, and pursue restoration whenever appropriate. The truth does not need human obstruction. It needs faithful witnesses.
