Chapter 14 He Never Told Them to Leave The Gospels contain encounter after encounter in which people approached Jesus carrying circumstances that made others uncomfortable. Some were known for their reputations. Some were considered ceremonially unclean. Some were burdened by physical suffering. Some were socially marginalized. Others were simply people whose presence interrupted the expectations of those who believed they understood how the Messiah should conduct Himself. The reactions surrounding Jesus often reveal a tension between the people who believed they understood holiness and the Savior who demonstrated holiness through His own actions. Jesus did not approach people carelessly. He did not abandon the purpose of His ministry to accommodate every demand placed upon Him. He taught truth, called people to repentance, confronted hypocrisy, and made clear the seriousness of following God. Yet throughout His ministry, He repeatedly demonstrated a willingness to engage people whom others considered undesirable, inconvenient, or unworthy of attention. The religious expectations surrounding Him were often challenged by the people He received. A tax collector could become a disciple. A Samaritan woman could engage Him in a conversation about worship. A man suffering from leprosy could approach Him and receive compassion. A woman known publicly for her sinful life could find herself in His presence while those observing the encounter questioned His willingness to receive her. The pattern deserves careful attention. Jesus never allowed another person’s reputation to determine whether He would engage them. He never treated the discomfort of religious observers as the final authority over His conduct. He never confused the opinions of those watching with the purpose for which He had come. His ministry consistently revealed that people who were accustomed to being dismissed could encounter Him personally. The Man Everyone Knew by His Occupation Matthew 9:9–13 describes Jesus calling Matthew, a tax collector, to follow Him. Tax collectors were despised among many Jewish people because of their association with Roman taxation and the abuses often connected with the system. Their profession carried a reputation that made them socially and religiously suspect. Jesus approached Matthew while he was sitting at the tax collector’s booth and said, “Follow Me.” Matthew responded by following Him. The encounter did not end with the calling of a disciple. Matthew later hosted a meal where Jesus sat with tax collectors and sinners. Religious observers questioned the company Jesus kept, asking why He ate with people they regarded as unacceptable. Jesus answered by using the image of a physician. Those who are well do not need a physician in the same way those who are sick do. He declared that He had come to call sinners rather than those who considered themselves righteous. His answer exposed the purpose of His ministry. Jesus was not associating with people merely to create controversy. He was engaging people who needed spiritual restoration. His presence among them communicated that the opportunity to respond to God’s call was not reserved for those who had already established a respectable reputation. Matthew’s occupation did not prevent Jesus from calling him. The reputation of the people at the meal did not prevent Jesus from sitting with them. The criticism of the religious observers did not change the purpose of the encounter. Jesus knew the people with whom He was sitting. His willingness to receive them was not the result of ignorance concerning their circumstances. He was not unaware of their reputation. His conduct demonstrated that awareness of someone’s past or present condition does not require treating the person as though the opportunity for grace has been removed. The church must consider what happens when a person’s reputation becomes more influential than the possibility of redemption. Some people are known by the worst thing they have done. Others are known by the circumstances surrounding their lives. Some are reduced to the choices they have made, the identities they carry, the struggles they experience, or the opinions others have formed about them. A reputation may contain information, yet a reputation cannot replace the responsibility to see a person as a human being. Jesus called Matthew while Matthew was still sitting at the tax collector’s booth. The invitation preceded the transformation of Matthew’s entire life. The call of Christ became the beginning of a new direction. The church should never assume that someone must first become acceptable to religious observers before an invitation to follow Jesus can be extended. The Woman Who Asked Questions John 4 records Jesus’ encounter with a Samaritan woman at a well. The setting itself carried social and religious significance. Jews and Samaritans had a complicated history involving differences in religious practice, claims concerning worship, and longstanding hostility. Jesus crossed those divisions by engaging the woman in conversation. He asked her for a drink. The woman recognized the significance of the interaction and questioned why a Jewish man would ask a Samaritan woman for water. The conversation developed beyond the immediate request and eventually addressed questions about living water, worship, and the identity of the Messiah. Jesus did not dismiss her questions. He engaged them. The woman raised the question of where worship should take place, referring to the differences between Samaritan and Jewish traditions. Jesus responded by explaining that true worshipers would worship the Father in spirit and truth. His teaching redirected the conversation toward the nature of worship and the revelation of God. The encounter also included a personal conversation concerning her life. Jesus demonstrated knowledge of her circumstances, and the woman recognized that she was speaking with someone who understood more than an ordinary traveler. The conversation moved toward the revelation of Jesus as the Messiah. The woman eventually returned to her community and spoke about the encounter. Her testimony encouraged others to come and hear Jesus for themselves. The significance of this account extends beyond the fact that Jesus spoke with someone from a group commonly viewed with suspicion. He engaged a woman who asked questions, challenged assumptions, and participated in a meaningful theological conversation. Her questions did not disqualify her from the encounter. Her background did not prevent her from hearing His teaching. Her circumstances did not make her incapable of responding to the revelation of Christ. The conversation was not superficial. Jesus addressed worship, spiritual understanding, and the reality of her life. The encounter demonstrates that meaningful instruction and personal engagement can take place with people whose circumstances differ from those of the religious community. The church must be careful not to assume that unfamiliar people are incapable of understanding spiritual truth. Someone may ask questions that challenge our assumptions while genuinely seeking to understand God. Someone may arrive carrying a history that requires sensitivity while remaining capable of receiving Scripture and responding to the message of Jesus. A question should not automatically be interpreted as resistance. A conversation should not be terminated merely because the person asking questions does not fit the expectations of the one answering. Jesus demonstrated that spiritual conversations can move beyond the barriers people assume must remain in place. The Man Who Came Close Despite His Condition In Mark 1:40–45, a man with leprosy approached Jesus and asked Him to make him clean. The man’s condition carried significant social and religious consequences. Leprosy was associated with ceremonial uncleanness, and the law provided instructions concerning the condition and the responsibilities of those affected. People experiencing such conditions could face separation and substantial limitations upon ordinary social interaction. The man approached Jesus with a statement of faith concerning His ability to heal. He said that Jesus could make him clean if He was willing. Jesus responded with compassion, stretched out His hand, touched him, and declared that He was willing. The man was cleansed. The touch itself deserves attention. Jesus did not respond to the man’s approach by retreating in disgust. He did not make the man feel ashamed for seeking help. He did not treat the man’s condition as a reason to withhold compassion. The healing demonstrated the authority of Christ and His willingness to respond to suffering. The account also reminds us that the instructions concerning ceremonial cleanliness were not meaningless. Jesus directed the man to follow the requirements associated with his cleansing and to present himself according to the law. His response was compassionate while remaining connected to the responsibilities described in Scripture. The encounter should not be reduced to a simplistic claim that every boundary in biblical law was irrelevant. The significance lies in the way Jesus responded to a suffering person who approached Him in faith. He demonstrated compassion without contempt. The church encounters people carrying circumstances that may require responsible care, appropriate boundaries, or practical considerations. Such responsibilities should never become excuses for humiliating people or treating them as though their humanity has diminished. There is a difference between recognizing a circumstance and reducing a person to that circumstance. The man came to Jesus asking for cleansing. Jesus responded to the man rather than allowing the social stigma surrounding his condition to determine the encounter. The church must learn to recognize when people are being treated according to the discomfort surrounding their circumstances rather than the dignity of their humanity. The Woman Whose Tears Were Not Welcome to Everyone Luke 7:36–50 describes a woman who entered the house of a Pharisee while Jesus was dining there. The woman was known in the city as a sinner. She approached Jesus carrying an alabaster jar of perfume. She stood behind Him, weeping, washed His feet with her tears, wiped them with her hair, kissed His feet, and anointed them with perfume. The Pharisee observing the encounter questioned Jesus’ understanding of the woman. He reasoned that a prophet should recognize the character of someone touching Him. Jesus responded with a parable about two debtors who had been forgiven debts of different amounts. Neither could repay the creditor, and both were forgiven. Jesus used the illustration to explain the woman’s response and the relationship between forgiveness and love. He then addressed the woman directly, declaring that her sins were forgiven and that her faith had saved her. The people at the table questioned who Jesus was to forgive sins. The encounter reveals a difference between the way the woman approached Jesus and the way the observer interpreted her presence. The woman came expressing a response that Jesus recognized. The observer saw her reputation and questioned the appropriateness of the encounter. The passage does not require the church to ignore sin. Jesus explicitly addressed forgiveness, and the narrative makes clear that the woman’s encounter involved the reality of sin and the mercy of Christ. However, the passage challenges the assumption that someone known for sinful conduct should be treated as though approaching Jesus were inappropriate. The woman was not told to leave. Her expression of devotion was not dismissed simply because her reputation preceded her. Jesus addressed her personally, recognized her faith, and spoke of forgiveness. The church must ask whether we sometimes become more concerned with the appearance of a person approaching Christ than with the possibility of that person encountering His mercy. We must also consider whether our reactions reveal that we have forgotten how deeply we ourselves depend upon forgiveness. The woman came to Jesus knowing that she needed mercy. The religious observer evaluated her from a position that appeared confident in his own assessment. The encounter reminds us that the presence of someone whose life has been marked by sin should not automatically become a reason for religious observers to withdraw compassion. The Savior who forgives sinners is not threatened by the presence of people who need forgiveness. The Man Whose Healing Created Another Controversy John 9 records the healing of a man who had been blind from birth. The disciples initially approached the man’s condition through a question concerning sin. They asked whether the man’s blindness had resulted from his own sin or the sin of his parents. Jesus rejected the assumption that the man’s condition could be explained through that particular accusation. He redirected the conversation toward the works of God being manifested. Jesus healed the man. The healing did not produce universal celebration. The circumstances became the subject of controversy because the healing occurred on the Sabbath. Religious observers questioned the conduct of Jesus, and the man who had been healed was repeatedly interrogated. The man eventually spoke about what had happened to him. His testimony was simple and grounded in his experience. He had been blind, and now he could see. The religious leaders continued questioning him. Their examination became increasingly hostile, and the man was eventually cast out. Jesus later found him. The man had experienced physical healing, endured religious interrogation, and been expelled from the community. Jesus sought him out and revealed Himself more fully. The contrast is significant. The religious authorities focused upon the circumstances surrounding the healing. Jesus focused upon the man. The man who had received sight became the subject of controversy among people who believed they were defending religious authority. The encounter reveals how a religious system can become so focused upon preserving its interpretations that the person experiencing God’s work becomes secondary. Jesus did not abandon the man when the controversy surrounding his healing resulted in exclusion. The Savior sought him out. The church should examine how often people become secondary to the disputes surrounding them. Someone may become the subject of a debate about doctrine, tradition, policy, identity, conduct, or religious interpretation. The discussion may involve sincere convictions. The people participating may believe the stakes are important. Nevertheless, the person at the center of the discussion remains a human being. The church should never allow theological disagreements to erase the responsibility to recognize the dignity, humanity, and spiritual needs of the people involved. Jesus demonstrated that the person matters. The People Who Expected Him to Disapprove Throughout the Gospels, Jesus encountered people who expected religious condemnation. Some came cautiously. Some approached publicly. Some sought help through intermediaries. Some cried out from a distance. Others entered spaces where their presence was questioned. The circumstances differed, yet the encounters consistently reveal His willingness to engage people personally. Jesus did not respond to every situation identically. He spoke differently according to the circumstances. He corrected some people, encouraged others, confronted hypocrisy, answered questions, and called people toward repentance. His responses were not governed by the comfort of the observers. His ministry was governed by His purpose. The church must resist the temptation to turn every unfamiliar encounter into a demonstration of its own authority. People should not have to navigate unnecessary suspicion merely to ask questions, hear Scripture, receive prayer, or seek spiritual guidance. We must remember that the people approaching Jesus were not always confident. Some were afraid. Some were ashamed. Some were desperate. Some were uncertain whether they would be received. The way believers respond to such people matters. A person who approaches a church should encounter an environment where Scripture can be taught faithfully, questions can be addressed responsibly, prayer can be offered sincerely, and human dignity remains intact. The church does not need to approve every decision someone has made to treat the person with compassion. The church does not need to abandon biblical convictions to listen carefully. The church does not need to control the encounter to faithfully represent Christ. Jesus demonstrated that truth and compassion can coexist without requiring contempt. When the Religious Observer Becomes the Obstacle The Gospels repeatedly show people questioning the conduct of Jesus because of the people He received. Those observers were often confident in their own understanding of righteousness. They knew the religious distinctions. They understood the customs. They believed certain circumstances required particular responses. The difficulty arose when their understanding became the lens through which they evaluated the ministry of Jesus. They questioned His association with tax collectors. They criticized His interactions with sinners. They challenged His conduct toward people whose circumstances they believed made the encounters inappropriate. Their objections did not always reveal a desire to protect people. Sometimes the objections revealed a desire to preserve their own expectations concerning how holiness should appear. The church must examine the possibility that similar assumptions can influence our conduct. We may believe we are protecting biblical truth while failing to recognize the way personal discomfort has shaped our reactions. We may believe we are preserving holiness while treating people in ways that contradict the mercy, humility, and compassion Scripture also commands. The answer is not to abandon discernment. The answer is to allow the character and teaching of Jesus to examine our discernment. We should ask whether our responses are helping people understand Christ, whether our conduct reflects His compassion, whether our judgments are grounded in Scripture, and whether our actions are consistent with the purpose of the gospel. The religious observer should never become so confident in evaluating the people approaching Jesus that the observer forgets the responsibility to follow Jesus. He Received People Without Surrendering His Purpose The ministry of Jesus was not defined by a willingness to accommodate every expectation. He remained faithful to the purpose for which He came. He preached the kingdom of God, called people to repentance, taught His disciples, confronted sin, and prepared for the cross. His willingness to receive people did not weaken His authority. His compassion did not eliminate the need for repentance. His engagement with people did not require Him to abandon truth. The church sometimes treats these principles as though they are competing responsibilities. A congregation may believe that maintaining biblical convictions requires a guarded, suspicious, or dismissive attitude toward people whose circumstances are unfamiliar. The Gospels challenge that assumption. Jesus remained faithful to His purpose while engaging people personally. His ministry demonstrates that biblical truth does not require the absence of compassion, and compassion does not require the abandonment of biblical truth. The church should not create a false choice between faithfulness and kindness. We are called to teach Scripture faithfully, care for people sincerely, recognize our own need for grace, and direct people toward Jesus. The responsibility is serious. The invitation is sacred. The people seeking Him deserve to encounter a church that understands the difference between representing Christ and attempting to control the circumstances surrounding His ministry. The Savior Who Keeps Receiving The Gospels reveal a Savior who continually encountered people beyond the expectations of religious observers. He called Matthew. He spoke with the Samaritan woman. He touched the man with leprosy. He received the woman who anointed His feet. He sought out the man who had been healed of blindness. He welcomed people who needed forgiveness, healing, instruction, restoration, and hope. The encounters were not identical, and their outcomes were not identical. The common thread was the willingness of Jesus to engage people personally and reveal the purpose of His ministry. The church must never become so preoccupied with the people it considers difficult that it forgets the people Jesus repeatedly received. We must remember the Savior whose compassion was not limited to those who had already established a favorable reputation. We must remember the Savior who taught with authority while remaining accessible to those seeking Him. We must remember the Savior who corrected religious observers when their assumptions interfered with the purpose of God. We must remember the Savior who remained faithful to truth while demonstrating compassion toward people. He never told the tax collector that his reputation made the invitation unavailable. He never dismissed the Samaritan woman because her questions crossed familiar religious boundaries. He never treated the suffering man as though his condition diminished his humanity. He never refused the woman seeking forgiveness because others considered her unworthy. He never abandoned the man whose healing became the subject of religious controversy. The church must learn from the way Jesus received people. Our responsibility is not to recreate the expectations of the crowds who questioned His conduct. Our responsibility is to follow the Savior whose ministry revealed the heart of God. The people approaching Him were not interruptions to His purpose. They were among the people His purpose reached. The church should never forget that truth. Jesus never told those seeking His presence to leave merely because their circumstances made religious observers uncomfortable. Our calling is to follow Him, represent Him faithfully, proclaim His truth, and demonstrate His compassion. The invitation belongs to Christ. Our responsibility is to make certain that our conduct reflects the Savior who extends the invitation.
