CHAPTER FOURTEEN He Never Told Them to Leave The Gospels repeatedly show Jesus engaging people whose circumstances challenged religious expectations. Some carried difficult reputations, some experienced suffering, and others were marginalized by social or religious divisions. Their presence often unsettled those who believed they understood how holiness should appear. Jesus did not abandon His purpose to accommodate every demand. He taught truth, called people to repentance, confronted hypocrisy, and made clear the cost of following God. Yet the opinions of those watching did not determine whom He would engage. Matthew at the tax collector’s booth Matthew 9:9–13 records Jesus calling Matthew, a tax collector, to follow Him. Tax collectors were often despised because of their association with Roman taxation and abuses connected with the system. Jesus called Matthew while he was sitting at the tax collector’s booth, and Matthew followed. The encounter continued at a meal where Jesus sat with tax collectors and sinners. Religious observers questioned His company. Jesus answered through the image of a physician, explaining that those who recognize their need seek help and declaring that He came to call sinners. His presence was not an endorsement of wrongdoing. He engaged people who needed spiritual restoration. Matthew’s occupation did not prevent Jesus from calling him, and the reputation of those at the table did not prevent Jesus from receiving them. The church must consider what happens when reputation becomes more influential than the possibility of redemption. A person may be known by a failure or circumstance, yet that description cannot replace the responsibility to see a human being in need of Christ. Matthew’s invitation began while he was still at the tax booth. The call of Jesus became the beginning of a new direction. The Samaritan woman who asked questions John 4 records Jesus’ conversation with a Samaritan woman at a well. The setting carried religious and cultural divisions. Jesus initiated conversation and engaged her questions concerning living water, worship, and the Messiah. The woman questioned why a Jewish man would speak with a Samaritan woman. Jesus did not dismiss her questions. The conversation developed into meaningful teaching concerning worship and His identity. He also addressed her personal circumstances, demonstrating knowledge of her life. The woman eventually returned to her community and spoke about the encounter. Others came to hear Jesus, and many believed. The account demonstrates that questions and unfamiliar circumstances did not prevent meaningful engagement. The church should not assume that someone asking difficult questions is resisting truth. A sincere search may be the beginning of understanding, and patient teaching can direct people toward Christ. The man who asked for cleansing Mark 1:40–45 records a man with leprosy approaching Jesus and asking to be made clean. His condition carried social and religious consequences, and the law provided instructions concerning cleanliness. The man expressed confidence in Jesus’ ability while asking whether He was willing. Jesus responded compassionately, touched him, and declared that He was willing. The man was cleansed. Jesus also directed him to follow the requirements associated with his cleansing, demonstrating compassion without disregarding Scripture. The encounter should not be reduced to a claim that every biblical boundary was irrelevant. The point is Jesus’ response toward a suffering person who approached Him. He did not treat the man’s condition as a reason to humiliate him or withhold compassion. The church may encounter circumstances requiring care, practical considerations, or responsible boundaries. Such responsibilities should never become excuses for treating people as though their humanity has diminished. Recognizing a circumstance is not the same as reducing someone to that circumstance. The woman who came in tears Luke 7:36–50 describes a woman known in the city as a sinner who approached Jesus while He was dining at a Pharisee’s home. She wept, washed His feet with her tears, wiped them with her hair, and anointed them with perfume. The Pharisee questioned Jesus’ understanding of the woman. Jesus responded with a parable concerning two debtors who were forgiven, then addressed the woman and declared that her sins were forgiven and that her faith had saved her. Forgiveness is central to the encounter. Yet the woman was not dismissed because of her reputation, and her approach was not treated as inappropriate simply because others knew her history. Jesus addressed her personally and recognized her faith. The church should examine whether we sometimes become more concerned about the appearance of someone approaching Christ than about the possibility of that person encountering mercy. We must remember how deeply we ourselves depend upon forgiveness. The man who was healed and rejected John 9 records Jesus healing a man blind from birth. The disciples initially asked whether the man’s blindness resulted from his own sin or his parents’ sin. Jesus rejected that assumption and directed attention toward the work of God. The healing became controversial because it occurred on the Sabbath. Religious observers questioned Jesus, interrogated the man, and eventually cast him out. Jesus later found the man and revealed Himself more fully. The contrast is striking. Religious authorities focused upon the circumstances surrounding the healing, while Jesus sought the person who had been rejected. The church must consider how often people become secondary to disputes surrounding them. Doctrine, tradition, policy, identity, or conduct may become subjects of serious discussion, but the person at the center remains a human being whose dignity and spiritual needs matter. The person before us Jesus did not respond identically in every circumstance. He corrected some, encouraged others, confronted hypocrisy, answered questions, and called people toward repentance. His responses were governed by His purpose, not the comfort of observers. The church does not need to approve every decision someone has made to treat that person with compassion. We do not need to abandon biblical convictions to listen carefully, and we do not need to control an encounter to represent Christ faithfully. Jesus received people without surrendering His purpose. His compassion did not eliminate repentance, and His truth did not require contempt. The church should not create a false choice between faithfulness and kindness. The religious observer must never become so confident in evaluating the people approaching Jesus that they forget their responsibility to follow Him. We should examine whether our responses help people understand Christ, reflect His compassion, and remain grounded in Scripture. The ministry of Jesus reveals that people accustomed to being dismissed could encounter Him personally. The church must carry that message faithfully, remembering that the Savior’s purpose is greater than our discomfort.
