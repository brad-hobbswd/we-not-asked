CHAPTER FOURTEEN He Never Told Them to Leave The Gospels contain encounters in which people approached Jesus carrying circumstances that made others uncomfortable. Some were considered ceremonially unclean, some carried painful histories, and others became the subjects of religious debate. Their circumstances differed, yet the accounts reveal a Savior who did not allow the opinions of observers to determine whether He would engage the people before Him. Jesus did not approach people carelessly or abandon the purpose of His ministry to accommodate every demand placed upon Him. He taught truth, called people to repentance, confronted hypocrisy, and made clear the seriousness of following God. His ministry nevertheless demonstrated that people accustomed to being dismissed could encounter Him personally. The man who asked for cleansing Mark 1:40–45 (AMPC) records a man with leprosy approaching Jesus and asking to be made clean. His condition carried significant social and religious consequences. The law provided instructions concerning ceremonial cleanliness, and people experiencing such conditions could face isolation and restrictions that affected daily life. Jesus responded with compassion, stretched out His hand, touched him, and declared that He was willing. The man was cleansed. Jesus did not respond by retreating in disgust, make the man feel ashamed for seeking help, or treat the man’s condition as a reason to withhold compassion. The account also reminds us that the instructions concerning ceremonial cleanliness were not meaningless. Jesus directed the man to follow the requirements associated with his cleansing and present himself according to the law. His response was compassionate without disregarding the responsibilities described in Scripture. The encounter should not be reduced to a simplistic claim that every boundary in biblical law was irrelevant. The significance lies in the way Jesus responded to a suffering person who approached Him in faith. The church encounters people carrying circumstances that may require responsible care, appropriate boundaries, or practical considerations. Such responsibilities should never become excuses for humiliating people or treating them as though their humanity has diminished. The man came asking for cleansing. Jesus responded to the man rather than allowing the stigma surrounding his condition to determine the encounter. The church must recognize when people are being treated according to the discomfort surrounding their circumstances rather than the dignity of their humanity. The woman who came in tears Luke 7:36–50 (AMPC) describes a woman known in the city as a sinner who approached Jesus while He was dining at a Pharisee’s home. She stood behind Him, weeping, washed His feet with her tears, wiped them with her hair, kissed His feet, and anointed them with perfume. The Pharisee observing the encounter questioned Jesus’ understanding of the woman. He reasoned that a prophet should recognize the character of someone touching Him. Jesus responded with a parable about two debtors who had been forgiven debts of different amounts. Neither could repay the creditor, and both were forgiven. Jesus used the illustration to explain the woman’s response and the relationship between forgiveness and love. He then addressed the woman directly, declaring that her sins were forgiven and that her faith had saved her. Forgiveness remained central to the encounter. The woman was not dismissed because of her reputation, and her approach was not treated as inappropriate simply because others knew her history. The passage does not require the church to ignore sin. Jesus addressed forgiveness, and the narrative makes clear that the woman’s encounter involved the reality of sin and the mercy of Christ. Nevertheless, the account challenges the assumption that someone known for sinful conduct should be treated as though approaching Jesus were inappropriate. The church must ask whether we sometimes become more concerned about the appearance of a person approaching Christ than about the possibility of that person encountering mercy. We must also consider whether our reactions reveal that we have forgotten how deeply we ourselves depend upon forgiveness. The woman approached Jesus knowing she needed mercy; the religious observer evaluated her from a position that appeared confident in his own assessment. The man who was healed and rejected John 9:1–41 (AMPC) records Jesus healing a man blind from birth. The disciples initially approached the man’s condition through a question concerning sin, asking whether his blindness resulted from his own sin or the sin of his parents. Jesus rejected that assumption and redirected the conversation toward the works of God being manifested. The healing did not produce universal celebration. The circumstances became controversial because the healing occurred on the Sabbath. Religious observers questioned Jesus’ conduct, and the man who had been healed was repeatedly interrogated. His testimony was simple and grounded in his experience: he had been blind, and now he could see. The religious leaders continued questioning him. Their examination became increasingly hostile, and the man was eventually cast out. He had experienced physical healing, endured religious interrogation, and been expelled from the community. Jesus sought him out and revealed Himself more fully. The religious authorities focused upon the circumstances surrounding the healing. Jesus focused upon the man. The encounter reveals how a religious system can become so focused upon preserving its interpretations that the person experiencing the consequences becomes secondary. Someone may become the subject of a debate about doctrine, tradition, policy, identity, conduct, or religious interpretation. The discussion may involve sincere convictions, and participants may believe the stakes are important. The church should never allow theological disagreements to erase the responsibility to recognize the dignity, humanity, and spiritual needs of the people involved. Jesus sought the man after his expulsion. The detail matters. The person who had been questioned and rejected was not forgotten by Christ. His encounter with Jesus continued beyond the conflict with religious observers. The church should consider whether our attention remains upon people when a conversation becomes difficult, when a decision is made, or when someone no longer fits the expectations of a community. The person before us These encounters were not identical. The man with leprosy sought cleansing. The woman approached Jesus in sorrow and expressed love. The man born blind experienced healing and then faced religious interrogation. Jesus responded to each according to the circumstances before Him. His words and actions were not interchangeable, yet His ministry remained grounded in truth, authority, compassion, and the purpose of God. The church should resist the temptation to turn every unfamiliar encounter into a demonstration of its own authority. People should not have to navigate unnecessary suspicion merely to ask questions, hear Scripture, receive prayer, or seek spiritual guidance. We do not need to approve every decision someone has made to treat the person with compassion. Nor must we abandon biblical convictions to listen carefully, offer responsible guidance, or recognize the humanity of someone whose circumstances differ from our own. Religious observers often became confident in evaluating the people approaching Jesus. They knew distinctions, customs, and expectations, and believed certain circumstances required particular responses. The difficulty arose when their understanding became the lens through which they evaluated His ministry. They questioned His conduct toward people whose circumstances they believed made the encounters inappropriate. Their objections did not always reveal a desire to protect people. Sometimes those objections revealed a desire to preserve their expectations concerning how holiness should appear. We may believe we are protecting biblical truth while failing to recognize the way personal discomfort has shaped our reactions. We may believe we are preserving holiness while treating people in ways that contradict the mercy, humility, and compassion Scripture teaches. We should ask whether our responses help people understand Christ, whether our conduct reflects His compassion, whether our judgments are grounded in Scripture, and whether our actions are consistent with the purpose of the gospel. The religious observer should never become so confident in evaluating the people approaching Jesus that the observer forgets the responsibility to follow Him. Jesus remained faithful to the purpose for which He came. He preached the kingdom of God, called people to repentance, taught His disciples, confronted sin, and prepared to give His life. The church sometimes treats these responsibilities as though they compete with compassion. A congregation may believe maintaining biblical convictions requires a guarded, suspicious, or dismissive attitude toward people whose circumstances are unfamiliar. Jesus remained faithful to His purpose while engaging people personally. His ministry demonstrates that biblical truth does not require the absence of compassion, and compassion does not require the abandonment of biblical truth. We are called to teach Scripture faithfully, care for people sincerely, recognize our own need for grace, and direct people toward Jesus. The responsibility is serious. The invitation is sacred. The people seeking Him deserve to encounter a church that understands the difference between representing Christ and attempting to control the circumstances surrounding His ministry. The Gospels reveal a Savior who continually encountered people beyond the expectations of religious observers. The encounters were not identical, and their outcomes were not identical. The common thread was His willingness to engage people personally and reveal the purpose of His ministry. The church must never become so preoccupied with the people it considers difficult that it forgets the people Jesus repeatedly received. We must remember the Savior whose compassion was not limited to those who had already established a favorable reputation, who taught with authority while remaining accessible to those seeking Him, who corrected religious observers when their assumptions interfered with the purpose of God, and who remained faithful to truth while demonstrating compassion toward people. Our responsibility is not to recreate the expectations of the crowds who questioned His conduct. Our responsibility is to follow the Savior whose ministry revealed the heart of God. Jesus never told those seeking His presence to leave merely because their circumstances made religious observers uncomfortable. Our calling is to follow Him, represent Him faithfully, proclaim His truth, and demonstrate His compassion. Our responsibility is to make certain our conduct reflects the Savior who extends the invitation.
