CHAPTER FOURTEEN He Never Told Them to Leave The Gospels contain encounter after encounter in which people approached Jesus carrying circumstances that made others uncomfortable. Some were known for their reputations, some were considered ceremonially unclean, some carried physical suffering, and others lived within divisions that shaped how people treated them. The reactions surrounding Jesus often reveal a tension between those who believed they understood holiness and the Savior who demonstrated holiness through His own actions. Jesus did not approach people carelessly or abandon the purpose of His ministry to accommodate every demand placed upon Him. He taught truth, called people to repentance, confronted hypocrisy, and made clear the seriousness of following God. The opinions of those watching, however, did not determine whom He would engage. His ministry consistently revealed that people accustomed to being dismissed could encounter Him personally. Matthew at the tax collector’s booth Tax collectors were despised among many Jewish people because of their association with Roman taxation and the abuses often connected with the system. Their profession carried a reputation that made them socially and religiously suspect. Matthew 9:9–13 (AMPC) records Jesus calling Matthew while he was sitting at the tax collector’s booth. Jesus said, “Follow Me,” and Matthew rose and followed Him. The encounter did not end with the calling of a disciple. Matthew later hosted a meal where Jesus sat with tax collectors and sinners. Religious observers questioned the company Jesus kept, asking why He ate with people they regarded as unacceptable. Jesus answered through the image of a physician, explaining that those who are well do not need a physician in the same way those who are sick do. He declared that He had come to call sinners rather than those who considered themselves righteous. Jesus was not associating with people merely to create controversy. He was engaging people who needed spiritual restoration. His presence among them communicated that the opportunity to respond to God’s call was not reserved for those who had already established a favorable reputation. He knew the people with whom He was sitting. His willingness to receive them was not the result of ignorance concerning their circumstances. The church must consider what happens when a person’s reputation becomes more influential than the possibility of redemption. Some people are known by the worst thing they have done; others are defined by circumstances, choices, struggles, identities, or opinions formed by those around them. A reputation may contain information, yet a reputation cannot replace the responsibility to see a person as a human being. Jesus called Matthew while Matthew was still sitting at the tax collector’s booth. The invitation preceded the transformation of Matthew’s entire life. The church should never assume that someone must first become acceptable to religious observers before an invitation to follow Jesus can be extended. The Samaritan woman who asked questions John 4:7–26 (AMPC) records Jesus’ conversation with a Samaritan woman at a well. The setting carried religious and cultural significance. Jews and Samaritans shared a complicated history involving differences in religious practice, claims concerning worship, and longstanding hostility. Jesus crossed those divisions by engaging the woman in conversation. She recognized the significance of the interaction and questioned why a Jewish man would ask a Samaritan woman for water. The conversation developed beyond the immediate request and addressed living water, worship, and the identity of the Messiah. She raised the question of where worship should take place, referring to differences between Samaritan and Jewish traditions. Jesus explained that true worshipers would worship the Father in spirit and truth, redirecting the conversation toward the nature of worship rather than merely the location. The encounter also included a personal conversation concerning her life. Jesus demonstrated knowledge of her circumstances, and the woman recognized that she was speaking with someone who understood more than an ordinary traveler. She eventually returned to her community and spoke about the encounter. Her testimony encouraged others to come and hear Jesus for themselves. The significance of this account extends beyond the fact that Jesus spoke with someone from a group commonly viewed with suspicion. He engaged a woman who asked questions, challenged assumptions, and participated in meaningful theological conversation. The conversation was not superficial. Jesus addressed worship, spiritual understanding, and the reality of her life. The church must be careful not to assume unfamiliar people are incapable of understanding spiritual truth. Someone may ask questions that challenge our assumptions while genuinely seeking God. Someone may arrive carrying a history that requires patience, thoughtful instruction, and compassion. A conversation should not be terminated merely because the person asking questions does not fit the expectations of the one answering. Jesus demonstrated that spiritual conversations can move beyond barriers people assume must remain in place. Patient engagement can help someone understand truth without requiring that person to arrive with every answer already settled. The man who asked for cleansing Mark 1:40–45 (AMPC) records a man with leprosy approaching Jesus and asking to be made clean. His condition carried significant social and religious consequences. The law provided instructions concerning ceremonial cleanliness, and people experiencing such conditions could face isolation and restrictions that affected daily life. Jesus responded with compassion, stretched out His hand, touched him, and declared that He was willing. The man was cleansed. Jesus did not respond by retreating in disgust, make the man feel ashamed for seeking help, or treat the man’s condition as a reason to withhold compassion. The account also reminds us that the instructions concerning ceremonial cleanliness were not meaningless. Jesus directed the man to follow the requirements associated with his cleansing and present himself according to the law. His response was compassionate without disregarding the responsibilities described in Scripture. The encounter should not be reduced to a simplistic claim that every boundary in biblical law was irrelevant. The significance lies in the way Jesus responded to a suffering person who approached Him in faith. The church encounters people carrying circumstances that may require responsible care, appropriate boundaries, or practical considerations. Such responsibilities should never become excuses for humiliating people or treating them as though their humanity has diminished. The man came asking for cleansing. Jesus responded to the man rather than allowing the stigma surrounding his condition to determine the encounter. The church must recognize when people are being treated according to the discomfort surrounding their circumstances rather than the dignity of their humanity. The woman who came in tears Luke 7:36–50 (AMPC) describes a woman known in the city as a sinner who approached Jesus while He was dining at a Pharisee’s home. She stood behind Him, weeping, washed His feet with her tears, wiped them with her hair, kissed His feet, and anointed them with perfume. The Pharisee observing the encounter questioned Jesus’ understanding of the woman. He reasoned that a prophet should recognize the character of someone touching Him. Jesus responded with a parable about two debtors who had been forgiven debts of different amounts. Neither could repay the creditor, and both were forgiven. Jesus used the illustration to explain the woman’s response and the relationship between forgiveness and love. He then addressed the woman directly, declaring that her sins were forgiven and that her faith had saved her. Forgiveness remained central to the encounter. The woman was not dismissed because of her reputation, and her approach was not treated as inappropriate simply because others knew her history. The passage does not require the church to ignore sin. Jesus addressed forgiveness, and the narrative makes clear that the woman’s encounter involved the reality of sin and the mercy of Christ. Nevertheless, the account challenges the assumption that someone known for sinful conduct should be treated as though approaching Jesus were inappropriate. The church must ask whether we sometimes become more concerned about the appearance of a person approaching Christ than about the possibility of that person encountering mercy. We must also consider whether our reactions reveal that we have forgotten how deeply we ourselves depend upon forgiveness. The woman approached Jesus knowing she needed mercy; the religious observer evaluated her from a position that appeared confident in his own assessment. The man who was healed and rejected John 9:1–41 (AMPC) records Jesus healing a man blind from birth. The disciples initially approached the man’s condition through a question concerning sin, asking whether his blindness resulted from his own sin or the sin of his parents. Jesus rejected that assumption and redirected the conversation toward the works of God being manifested. The healing did not produce universal celebration. The circumstances became controversial because the healing occurred on the Sabbath. Religious observers questioned Jesus’ conduct, and the man who had been healed was repeatedly interrogated. His testimony was simple and grounded in his experience: he had been blind, and now he could see. The religious leaders continued questioning him. Their examination became increasingly hostile, and the man was eventually cast out. He had experienced physical healing, endured religious interrogation, and been expelled from the community. Jesus sought him out and revealed Himself more fully. The religious authorities focused upon the circumstances surrounding the healing. Jesus focused upon the man. The encounter reveals how a religious system can become so focused upon preserving its interpretations that the person experiencing the consequences becomes secondary. Someone may become the subject of a debate about doctrine, tradition, policy, identity, conduct, or religious interpretation. The discussion may involve sincere convictions, and participants may believe the stakes are important. The church should never allow theological disagreements to erase the responsibility to recognize the dignity, humanity, and spiritual needs of the people involved. The person before us Some came cautiously. Some approached publicly. Some sought help through intermediaries. Some cried out from a distance. Others entered spaces where their presence was questioned. The circumstances differed, yet these encounters consistently reveal Jesus’ willingness to engage people personally. Jesus did not respond to every situation identically. He corrected some people, encouraged others, confronted hypocrisy, answered questions, and called people toward repentance. His responses were governed by His purpose, not the comfort of observers. The church must resist the temptation to turn every unfamiliar encounter into a demonstration of its own authority. People should not have to navigate unnecessary suspicion merely to ask questions, hear Scripture, receive prayer, or seek spiritual guidance. We must remember that the people approaching Jesus were not always confident. Some were afraid, ashamed, desperate, or uncertain whether they would be received. A person who approaches a church should encounter an environment where Scripture can be taught faithfully, questions can be addressed responsibly, prayer can be offered sincerely, and human dignity remains intact. The church does not need to approve every decision someone has made to treat the person with compassion. Nor must we abandon biblical convictions to listen carefully, offer responsible guidance, or recognize the humanity of someone whose circumstances differ from our own. Religious observers often became confident in evaluating the people approaching Jesus. They knew the distinctions, customs, and expectations, and they believed certain circumstances required particular responses. The difficulty arose when their understanding became the lens through which they evaluated His ministry. They questioned His association with tax collectors, criticized His interactions with sinners, and challenged His conduct toward people whose circumstances they believed made the encounters inappropriate. Their objections did not always reveal a desire to protect people. Sometimes those objections revealed a desire to preserve their expectations concerning how holiness should appear. We may believe we are protecting biblical truth while failing to recognize the way personal discomfort has shaped our reactions. We may believe we are preserving holiness while treating people in ways that contradict the mercy, humility, and compassion Scripture teaches. We should ask whether our responses help people understand Christ, whether our conduct reflects His compassion, whether our judgments are grounded in Scripture, and whether our actions are consistent with the purpose of the gospel. The religious observer should never become so confident in evaluating the people approaching Jesus that the observer forgets the responsibility to follow Him. Jesus remained faithful to the purpose for which He came. He preached the kingdom of God, called people to repentance, taught His disciples, confronted sin, and prepared to give His life. The church sometimes treats these responsibilities as though they compete with compassion. A congregation may believe maintaining biblical convictions requires a guarded, suspicious, or dismissive attitude toward people whose circumstances are unfamiliar. Jesus remained faithful to His purpose while engaging people personally. His ministry demonstrates that biblical truth does not require the absence of compassion, and compassion does not require the abandonment of biblical truth. We are called to teach Scripture faithfully, care for people sincerely, recognize our own need for grace, and direct people toward Jesus. The responsibility is serious. The invitation is sacred. The people seeking Him deserve to encounter a church that understands the difference between representing Christ and attempting to control the circumstances surrounding His ministry. The Gospels reveal a Savior who continually encountered people beyond the expectations of religious observers. The encounters were not identical, and their outcomes were not identical. The common thread was His willingness to engage people personally and reveal the purpose of His ministry. The church must never become so preoccupied with the people it considers difficult that it forgets the people Jesus repeatedly received. We must remember the Savior whose compassion was not limited to those who had already established a favorable reputation, who taught with authority while remaining accessible to those seeking Him, who corrected religious observers when their assumptions interfered with the purpose of God, and who remained faithful to truth while demonstrating compassion toward people. Our responsibility is not to recreate the expectations of the crowds who questioned His conduct. Our responsibility is to follow the Savior whose ministry revealed the heart of God. Jesus never told those seeking His presence to leave merely because their circumstances made religious observers uncomfortable. Our calling is to follow Him, represent Him faithfully, proclaim His truth, and demonstrate His compassion. Our responsibility is to make certain our conduct reflects the Savior who extends the invitation.
