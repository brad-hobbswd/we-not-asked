CHAPTER TWO When We Mistook Proximity for Authority Walking closely with someone does not make us the authority over that person. The distinction is easy to recognize in ordinary relationships, yet spiritual responsibility can blur the boundary. Familiarity may create confidence; confidence can become assumption; and assumption may convince us that our position grants permission to make decisions beyond the responsibility we were given. The disciples knew Jesus personally. They had heard His teaching, witnessed His miracles, traveled with Him, and observed the way He responded to people seeking help. Their relationship was real, and their calling was significant. Still, they did not possess authority over every encounter He allowed. Following Him meant learning His ways and carrying out His instructions, not managing His mission according to their own expectations. That distinction matters wherever people are entrusted with leadership, teaching, influence, or care. Responsibility is meaningful, but responsibility is not ownership. A pastor, parent, teacher, ministry leader, or mentor may influence the lives of others without possessing unrestricted authority over their convictions, decisions, or spiritual journey. The greater the influence, the greater the need for humility and accountability. The danger of assuming more than was entrusted Scripture presents authority as a responsibility exercised under God, not an opportunity to elevate personal preference. Those who lead remain accountable to the One who entrusted the work to them. The purpose of leadership is not to secure unquestioned control, but to serve faithfully within the boundaries established by God. Influence carries consequences. A leader’s interpretation may shape a congregation’s understanding of Scripture. A family member’s convictions may influence how someone understands God and their own spiritual life. A teacher’s words may become the foundation upon which students build beliefs. Decisions made within a religious community can affect whether someone feels encouraged to seek the Lord or discouraged from returning. Such influence should never be treated casually. Yet the problem is not authority itself. Scripture provides instruction concerning leadership, discipline, teaching, pastoral care, and the responsibilities believers share. The problem begins when the purpose of authority is distorted, when those entrusted to care for others begin acting as though the responsibility grants ownership over matters Scripture has not placed under their control. The disciples’ response to the children illustrates that confusion. First Peter 5:2–3 instructs elders to shepherd the flock willingly and not domineer over those entrusted to their care. Matthew 20:25–28 likewise contrasts worldly authority with the servant leadership Jesus requires. These passages help clarify the proper purpose of influence: care and service, not control. They were present with Jesus, but their presence did not authorize them to regulate His welcome. Their position among His followers could not make their assumption correct. Familiarity can conceal the need to learn Repeated exposure to the words of Jesus does not guarantee that we understand their implications. A person may know familiar passages while overlooking their context, or use the language of faith without recognizing that conduct has departed from the character of Christ. The disciples’ correction reminds us that misunderstanding can exist within sincere devotion. Familiarity may also cause us to believe that we can anticipate what the Lord would do without returning to His instruction. We remember past decisions, develop confidence in our instincts, and begin to act as though experience itself has become a sufficient guide. Experience can help us recognize patterns, but it remains imperfect. A circumstance that resembles something from the past may require a different response. A person we believe we understand may be carrying a need we have never heard. Familiarity should make us attentive, not presumptuous. This can happen in family life as readily as in ministry. Parents carry real responsibilities for their children, and teachers are entrusted with the safety and learning of students. Pastors and ministry leaders have responsibilities to care for congregations. Those roles are meaningful, yet the people affected remain human beings with dignity, questions, and a spiritual life that cannot be owned by the person exercising responsibility. A role should clarify what service is required, not expand into authority without limits. When responsibility is confused with ownership, people may begin expecting unquestioned agreement. Questions are interpreted as disrespect, disagreement becomes disloyalty, and a request for explanation is treated as a challenge to the position itself. Such reactions can discourage honest communication. A person may comply outwardly while becoming afraid to speak truthfully, and fear can conceal problems that responsible leadership should want to understand. The church must not equate quietness with spiritual maturity or unquestioning compliance with healthy discipleship. Scripture calls believers to humility and order, but also to wisdom, discernment, truthful speech, and accountability. A person may raise a concern respectfully and still deserve a serious response. Leaders should be able to explain the purpose of a decision and consider whether their approach needs correction. The need to learn does not disappear with years of service. Long experience, biblical knowledge, leadership positions, or inherited practices may give a person confidence, but confidence must remain subject to examination. When a familiar belief is challenged, the response should not automatically be defensiveness. We should return to Scripture and consider whether the belief has been understood faithfully. Religious communities can find this difficult. A conviction passed down through generations may become so closely connected with identity that examining the interpretation feels like rejecting the people who taught it. Yet respect for those who came before us does not require treating every conclusion as beyond review. Loyalty to Christ calls us to examine the Word honestly, including the beliefs we have held for a long time. The disciples were not corrected because they had ceased following Jesus. They were corrected while following Him. Their example leaves room for humility: we can be sincere, committed, and still need our understanding brought into alignment with the Lord. Shepherding is care, not possession The biblical image of shepherding describes care, guidance, protection, and service. A shepherd must exercise discernment, but discernment is not a license to control. The manner in which responsibility is carried should reflect the character of the Shepherd. Jesus demonstrated leadership through compassion, instruction, confrontation, and attention to people. He did not ignore truth or abandon the demands of discipleship. Neither did He treat the people who came to Him as interruptions to be dismissed. His ministry shows that truth and care belong together. The church must distinguish guiding people according to Scripture from making human approval the center of their spiritual experience. Teaching involves explaining the Word, encouraging obedience, and helping people grow. Correction should serve the purpose of restoration, not domination. People should be able to ask honest questions and disclose struggles without being treated as though vulnerability itself were a failure of faith. Control can produce fear. People may become reluctant to speak honestly, uncertain whether questions will be welcomed, or convinced that the safest response is to hide their struggles. Such an atmosphere does not make discipleship stronger. Faithful leadership must create room for truth to be spoken and received with accountability, patience, and dignity. Authority remains accountable Every responsibility within the church remains beneath the authority of Jesus Christ. A title, history of service, family inheritance, denominational standing, or personal influence cannot establish authority equal to His. Those entrusted with leadership must be willing to have their decisions and conduct examined according to Scripture. This accountability does not diminish leadership. It protects the purpose of leadership. Authority becomes trustworthy when the person exercising it recognizes the limits of the responsibility, welcomes appropriate examination, and refuses to elevate personal judgment beyond the Word. We can become familiar with Christian language, institutions, and practices while failing to recognize the heart of Christ in a particular circumstance. Familiarity should produce humility rather than presumption. Responsibility should make us more careful about the effect of our decisions, not more convinced that our decisions are beyond question. The disciples’ correction remains a warning for those who carry influence. They were near Jesus, yet their understanding was incomplete. Their position did not protect them from correction, and the Lord’s response showed that their assumptions could not remain in control. Proximity is not ownership The people bringing children sought an encounter with Jesus. The disciples introduced resistance, and the Lord corrected them. The episode reveals the limits of their role, but the larger lesson for this chapter concerns the confidence that can arise from proximity. Being close to a ministry, knowing its language, or carrying responsibility within it does not mean we possess authority over everything connected with the work. Those who serve must remember that the people entrusted to their care are not possessions. Their spiritual growth is not a measure of personal control, and their questions do not automatically represent a threat. Our responsibility is to guide faithfully, teach carefully, serve compassionately, and remain accountable to Christ. The disciples were followers. Jesus was the Lord. Their calling was to learn from Him and carry out His instructions, including when His correction exposed an assumption they had not recognized. Spiritual responsibility must never become a reason to claim authority the Lord has not given. Walking closely with Jesus calls us to greater humility, not a greater sense of ownership.
