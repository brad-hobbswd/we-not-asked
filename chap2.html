PART I · THE AUTHORITY WE ASSUMED

When We Mistook Proximity for Authority

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 2 of 17Reading edition

Being close to someone does not make us the authority over them. Most of us understand that in ordinary relationships, yet spiritual responsibility can blur the line. Familiarity builds confidence. Confidence can become assumption. Before long, we may begin making decisions as though our position gives us permission to speak for God beyond what He has entrusted to us.

The disciples knew Jesus. They had traveled with Him, heard His teaching, and seen His works. Their calling was meaningful, but following Jesus meant learning His ways. They could not use their closeness as permission to manage His mission according to their own expectations.

The same can happen in a church. People who have served for years know the history, remember past decisions, and may have sacrificed deeply. Their experience deserves respect, yet no amount of service makes a person's interpretation beyond examination. A tradition may be valuable; the reason behind a practice still needs to be understood.

Authority in Scripture is a responsibility exercised under God. Peter tells elders to shepherd willingly and not domineer over those entrusted to their care (1 Peter 5:2–3). Jesus contrasts worldly power with the servant leadership He expects (Matthew 20:25–28). Leadership remains necessary, but the purpose is care, not control.

One way control becomes visible is in how questions are handled. A sincere request for explanation may be called disrespect. A disagreement may be treated as disloyalty. Someone may be told that asking for clarity proves a lack of faith. Such responses can silence people without resolving the concern.

Not every challenge is right, and leaders sometimes have to make decisions others will not like. Still, a question deserves to be considered on its merits. A leader can explain the reasoning, return to Scripture, seek counsel, or admit that a decision needs reconsideration. Position should never be used as a substitute for truth.

Respect and discernment belong together. Believers are called to humility, but also to examine teaching and remain accountable to the Word. Quietness does not always mean understanding, and unquestioning agreement is not the only evidence of maturity. Healthy spiritual relationships help people grow in discernment rather than become dependent on another person's approval.

Those entrusted with leadership should remember why they were given responsibility. Pastors shepherd. Teachers help people learn. Parents guide children toward maturity. Mentors encourage growth. None of these roles gives a person ownership over another's conscience or relationship with God.

The disciples had a calling, but Jesus remained the authority. Their closeness did not excuse their mistake. We are safest when our confidence remains submitted to Christ, and when the people entrusted to our care can see that we are willing to be corrected by the same Word we teach.
