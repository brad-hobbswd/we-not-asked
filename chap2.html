CHAPTER TWO When We Mistook Proximity for Authority There is a distinction between walking closely with someone and possessing authority over the decisions that belong to that person. The distinction may appear obvious when considered outside the context of religious responsibility, yet the boundaries can become less clear when people are entrusted with leadership, teaching, influence, or the care of others. Familiarity can create confidence, confidence can develop into assumption, and assumption can gradually convince a person that their position grants permission to make decisions beyond the responsibilities they were actually given. The disciples walked with Jesus. They heard His teaching, witnessed His miracles, traveled with Him, and observed the way He responded to people who came seeking help. Their relationship with Him was not distant or superficial. They had been chosen to follow Him, learn from Him, and participate in the work of His ministry. Yet their proximity did not mean they understood every aspect of His heart, nor did their position among His followers give them authority over every encounter He permitted. The distinction becomes especially important when examining the ways spiritual authority is exercised. A person may be entrusted with responsibility within a congregation, family, ministry, or institution without possessing unrestricted authority over the people affected by that responsibility. The presence of leadership does not eliminate the need for humility, accountability, discernment, or examination of Scripture. Positions of influence require greater care precisely because the decisions made by those holding such positions can affect the faith, dignity, relationships, and spiritual lives of others. Jesus did not establish a ministry in which proximity to Him became permission to control Him. The disciples were followers, not owners of His mission. Their responsibility was to learn His ways and carry out His instructions. When their conduct contradicted His direction, their position did not protect them from correction. The danger of assuming more than was entrusted Throughout Scripture, responsibility is accompanied by accountability. The authority entrusted to God’s servants is never presented as an opportunity to elevate personal preference above His will. Leadership is a stewardship, requiring the person exercising responsibility to remain answerable to the One who entrusted that responsibility. This principle becomes particularly significant within the church because spiritual influence can carry consequences that extend beyond immediate circumstances. A leader’s words may shape how a congregation understands Scripture. A family member’s convictions may influence the way another person understands God. A teacher’s interpretation may become the foundation upon which students build their beliefs. A decision made within a religious community may determine whether someone feels encouraged to seek the Lord or discouraged from returning. Influence is therefore not a small responsibility. The greater the influence, the greater the need to recognize its limitations. The problem is not authority itself. Scripture provides instruction concerning leadership, discipline, teaching, pastoral care, and the responsibilities of believers toward one another. The problem arises when the purpose of authority becomes distorted, when a person begins exercising influence as though the responsibility entrusted to them grants ownership over the decisions, convictions, or spiritual journey of another person beyond what Scripture establishes. The disciples’ response to the children demonstrates the danger of that assumption. They attempted to regulate an encounter with Jesus, yet the encounter belonged to Jesus. They were present, but their presence did not make them the authority over His invitation. Familiarity does not guarantee understanding People can become familiar with the words of Jesus while remaining unfamiliar with the implications of those words. Repeated exposure to Scripture does not automatically produce an accurate understanding of its meaning. Hearing a passage frequently does not guarantee that its context has been carefully considered, and knowing the language of faith does not necessarily mean that our conduct reflects the character of Christ. The disciples’ correction reminds us that misunderstanding can exist within sincere faith. Their actions were corrected not because they had abandoned Jesus, but because their response did not reflect what He was teaching them. They were still learning the relationship between the kingdom He proclaimed and the expectations they carried into His ministry. Such learning requires humility. The believer must remain willing to encounter Scripture in ways that challenge familiar assumptions. A passage may reveal that a practice has been misunderstood, that an attitude has become inconsistent with Christ’s teaching, or that a decision was made without sufficient consideration of the responsibility God actually entrusted. Spiritual growth requires the willingness to receive such correction rather than treating every challenge as an attack upon faith. The danger of familiarity becomes especially pronounced when people have been surrounded by the same interpretations for many years. The beliefs of a family, congregation, denomination, or religious community may become so deeply embedded that questioning them feels disloyal. Yet loyalty to Christ cannot require the abandonment of careful biblical examination. Our understanding must remain subject to His Word. The difference between shepherding and controlling The language of shepherding carries a responsibility of care. A shepherd tends, protects, guides, and serves the flock. Such responsibility requires discernment, patience, and a willingness to consider the needs of those entrusted to one’s care. Shepherding does not become faithful merely through the exercise of authority; the manner in which authority is exercised must also reflect the character of the Shepherd. Jesus provided the clearest example of leadership through His own ministry. He demonstrated compassion toward people, gave instruction, confronted wrongdoing, and remained attentive to those who came seeking Him. His ministry was neither careless concerning truth nor indifferent toward human suffering. He did not abandon His purpose, yet He consistently revealed that the purpose of His ministry could not be separated from the people He came to reach. The church must therefore distinguish between guiding people according to Scripture and attempting to control their access to Christ. Teaching discipleship involves communicating the demands of following Jesus, helping people understand the Word, and encouraging faithful obedience. Such responsibility cannot be reduced to determining whether another person is personally acceptable to us. The work of ministry requires a posture that directs people toward the Lord rather than making human approval the center of their spiritual experience. When control replaces care, the consequences may be deeply damaging. People can become afraid to ask questions, reluctant to disclose struggles, or convinced that honesty will result in rejection. Relationships within the church may become governed by fear of disapproval rather than the pursuit of truth and spiritual growth. Those experiencing such circumstances may struggle to distinguish the character of Jesus from the conduct of those who claim to represent Him. The responsibility of leadership is not to create an environment in which people are protected from every uncomfortable truth. The responsibility is to communicate truth faithfully while recognizing that people are human beings requiring compassion, patience, dignity, and spiritual care. Correction should serve restoration and growth, not personal domination. The authority of Christ remains greater Every responsibility within the church must remain beneath the authority of Jesus Christ. No title, position, history of service, family inheritance, denominational standing, or personal influence can establish authority equal to His. Those entrusted with leadership remain servants of the One who called them, and their decisions must be examined according to the Scriptures rather than accepted solely because of the position from which they were made. This does not diminish the importance of leadership. It establishes the proper foundation for leadership. Authority becomes trustworthy when the person exercising it recognizes accountability to Christ, welcomes appropriate examination, and refuses to elevate personal judgment beyond the boundaries of God’s Word. The disciples’ response to the children offers a sobering reminder that even those closest to Jesus could misunderstand the responsibility they carried. Their proximity was real, but their understanding was incomplete. Their correction was necessary, and the response of Jesus revealed the authority that had been missing from their decision. The church must remain attentive to that distinction. We can be close to the language, practices, and institutions of Christianity while failing to understand the heart of Christ in a particular circumstance. Our familiarity should produce greater humility, not greater presumption. The more responsibility we carry, the more carefully we must examine whether our actions are consistent with the commission we received. Authority cannot replace the invitation The people bringing children to Jesus were seeking an encounter with Him. The disciples introduced resistance, but Jesus removed the obstruction. His response established the boundary that their actions had crossed. They were not authorized to prevent those children from approaching Him, and their decision could not override His invitation. The same principle requires examination wherever human authority influences the way people encounter Christ. A leader’s interpretation, a family’s expectation, or a congregation’s customary practice must never be treated as though such influence grants ownership over the Savior’s invitation. Our responsibility is to remain faithful to the Word, communicate the gospel, and guide people toward the Lord. We cannot make ourselves the final authority over whom He receives. The issue is not whether every person who approaches Jesus will remain unchanged. The gospel calls people into a life of faith, repentance, obedience, and transformation. The issue is whether we understand our responsibility to proclaim that invitation faithfully rather than obstructing people before they have the opportunity to respond. Those who follow Christ must remain willing to examine the limits of their own authority. We must recognize that leadership is a responsibility of service, that influence carries accountability, and that proximity to Jesus does not grant permission to assume His place. The disciples were near enough to Jesus to hear His correction. Their experience reminds us that no position within the church removes the need for humility before the Lord. We remain followers, servants, witnesses, and messengers. Christ remains the Savior. Our authority is entrusted. His authority is absolute. Walking beside Jesus never gave us permission to stand between Him and someone seeking His presence.
