PART I · THE AUTHORITY WE ASSUMED

When We Mistook Proximity for Authority

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 2 of 17Reading edition

Walking closely with someone does not make us the authority over that person. The distinction is easy to recognize in ordinary relationships, yet spiritual responsibility can blur the boundary. Familiarity may create confidence; confidence can become assumption; and assumption may convince us that our position grants permission to make decisions beyond the responsibility we were given.

The disciples knew Jesus personally. They had heard His teaching, witnessed His miracles, traveled with Him, and observed the way He responded to people seeking help. Their relationship was real, and their calling was significant. Yet being close to the Lord did not mean they could determine His intentions without listening to Him. Following Him meant learning His ways and carrying out His instructions, not managing His mission according to their own expectations.

Proximity can create an illusion of authority

People who spend years in a ministry may begin to feel that familiarity gives them a special claim over its direction. They know how things have been done, remember earlier decisions, understand the personalities involved, and may have sacrificed greatly to support the work. Their experience can be valuable, but experience does not make every conclusion correct.

The same confusion can occur in families. Parents carry real responsibilities for children, and those responsibilities include guidance, protection, and instruction. As children mature, however, care must not become ownership of every decision, question, or conviction. The role of a parent is to prepare a child to live responsibly, not to make the child's entire life an extension of the parent's preferences.

Teachers, mentors, ministry leaders, and pastors also hold responsibilities that matter. They influence understanding, help people develop, provide direction, and sometimes must address difficult circumstances. The legitimacy of those roles does not make their authority unlimited. Responsibility should clarify what service is required, not expand into control over matters that belong to God or the individual conscience.

The danger of assuming more than was entrusted

Scripture presents authority as a responsibility exercised under God. Those who lead are accountable to the One who entrusted the work to them. Leadership is not intended to secure unquestioned control; its purpose is to serve faithfully within the boundaries established by God.

First Peter 5:2–3 instructs elders to shepherd willingly and not domineer over those entrusted to their care. Matthew 20:25–28 contrasts the authority exercised by worldly rulers with the servant leadership Jesus requires. These passages do not erase leadership or the need for direction. They clarify the spirit and purpose in which leadership is to be carried.

Influence carries consequences. A leader’s interpretation may shape a congregation’s understanding of Scripture. A family member’s convictions may influence how someone understands God and their own spiritual life. A teacher’s words may become the foundation upon which students build beliefs. Decisions made within a religious community can affect whether someone feels encouraged to seek the Lord or discouraged from returning.

Such influence should never be treated casually. The problem is not authority itself. The problem begins when the purpose of authority is distorted, when those entrusted to care for others begin acting as though their responsibility grants ownership over matters Scripture has not placed under their control.

When questions become threats

A culture of control often reveals itself through the way questions are received. A request for clarification may be interpreted as disrespect. A disagreement may be labeled disloyalty. A person seeking to understand the reason behind a decision may be told that questioning leadership demonstrates a lack of faith. Such responses can discourage honest communication, even when the person raising the concern is sincere and respectful.

The church must not equate quietness with spiritual maturity or unquestioning compliance with healthy discipleship. Scripture calls believers to humility and order, but also to wisdom, discernment, truthful speech, and accountability. A person may raise a concern respectfully and still deserve a serious response. Leaders should be able to explain the purpose of a decision and consider whether their approach needs correction.

Questions are not automatically rebellion. They can expose confusion, reveal a need for teaching, identify an inconsistency, or help a community understand whether a practice is grounded in Scripture. A leader who receives questions with patience may strengthen trust and clarify the teaching. A leader who responds with intimidation may silence the concern without resolving the underlying matter.

This does not mean every challenge is accurate or every decision must be changed. Leadership sometimes requires difficult answers, boundaries, and decisions that not everyone will welcome. The responsibility is to respond with integrity, explain what can be explained, consider the concern honestly, and avoid using position as a substitute for reasoning or biblical authority.

Familiarity must remain teachable

Long experience, biblical knowledge, leadership positions, or inherited practices may give a person confidence, but confidence must remain subject to examination. When a familiar belief is challenged, the response should not automatically be defensiveness. We should return to Scripture and consider whether the belief has been understood faithfully.

Religious communities can find this difficult. A conviction passed down through generations may become so closely connected with identity that examining the interpretation feels like rejecting the people who taught it. Yet respect for those who came before us does not require treating every conclusion as beyond review. Loyalty to Christ calls us to examine the Word honestly, including the beliefs we have held for a long time.

Sincere devotion does not eliminate the need to learn. A person can be committed, active, and knowledgeable while still needing correction. That recognition should not threaten faith. The principle reminds us that the Word remains authoritative and our understanding remains accountable to Scripture.

Shepherding is care, not possession

The biblical image of shepherding describes care, guidance, protection, and service. A shepherd must exercise discernment, but discernment is not a license to control. The manner in which responsibility is carried should reflect the character of the Shepherd.

Jesus demonstrated leadership through compassion, instruction, confrontation, and attention to people. He did not ignore truth or abandon the demands of discipleship. Neither did He treat the people who came to Him as interruptions to be dismissed. His ministry shows that truth and care belong together.

The church must distinguish guiding people according to Scripture from making human approval the center of their spiritual experience. Teaching involves explaining the Word, encouraging obedience, and helping people grow. Correction should serve the purpose of restoration, not domination. People should be able to ask honest questions and disclose struggles without being treated as though vulnerability itself were a failure of faith.

Control can produce fear. People may become reluctant to speak honestly, uncertain whether questions will be welcomed, or convinced that the safest response is to hide their struggles. Such an atmosphere does not make discipleship stronger. Faithful leadership must create room for truth to be spoken and received with accountability, patience, and dignity.

Authority remains accountable

Every responsibility within the church remains beneath the authority of Jesus Christ. A title, history of service, family inheritance, denominational standing, or personal influence cannot establish authority equal to His. Those entrusted with leadership must be willing to have their decisions and conduct examined according to Scripture.

Accountability does not diminish leadership. Such accountability protects the purpose of leadership. Authority becomes trustworthy when the person exercising it recognizes the limits of the responsibility, welcomes appropriate examination, and refuses to elevate personal judgment beyond the Word. Leaders should be willing to seek counsel, admit uncertainty, correct errors, and acknowledge when a decision has caused harm.

The same principle applies to those who follow. Respect for leadership is not permission to surrender discernment. Believers are responsible to study Scripture, grow in understanding, and speak truth with humility. Healthy spiritual relationships should encourage maturity rather than dependence upon a person's approval.

The boundary between service and control

Responsibility is most faithfully exercised when we remember why it was entrusted to us. A ministry leader is called to serve the mission, not protect personal status. A teacher is called to help students learn, not demand that every question affirm the teacher's authority. A pastor is called to shepherd, not make the congregation dependent upon personal approval. A parent is called to nurture and guide, not confuse care with possession.

The people affected by our responsibilities remain human beings with dignity, conscience, questions, and a relationship with God that cannot be owned by another person. Their spiritual growth is not a measure of our control. Our task is to teach carefully, guide faithfully, provide appropriate protection, and remain accountable for the way we exercise influence.

The disciples’ experience illustrates the danger of allowing closeness to become confidence without submission. Their calling was significant, but the Lord remained the authority. They needed to learn that their role was to follow, not assume that proximity gave them permission to decide beyond what He had entrusted.

That distinction becomes clear in the encounter itself. The next chapter examines what Mark records, without requiring us to speculate about the disciples’ motives. Their action was wrong, and Jesus’ correction was clear. We must be willing to recognize when our assumptions have become an obstacle.
