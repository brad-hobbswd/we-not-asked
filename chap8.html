PART II · THE PEOPLE WE DECIDED AGAINST

Sinners Judging Sinners

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 8 of 17Reading edition

Judgment becomes dangerous when the person delivering it forgets their own need for mercy. A believer can become so focused on another person’s failures that personal examination fades. The sins of others become subjects of correction and conversation, while the condition of one’s own heart receives little attention. Scripture confronts this tendency because religious confidence can create an illusion of righteousness.

The problem is not recognizing sin. The church is called to discernment, correction, accountability, and holiness. The problem begins when discernment becomes superiority, correction becomes humiliation, and accountability is demanded from others while personally resisted. God’s Word was never intended to elevate us above people whose struggles differ from our own.

The beam and the speck

Jesus addresses this danger in Matthew 7:1–5 (AMPC). He describes a person who notices a speck in another’s eye while overlooking a beam in their own. The image exposes the absurdity of attempting to correct someone while refusing to confront personal condition. The problem is hypocrisy, not simply inaccurate judgment.

Jesus instructs the person to remove the beam before attempting to remove the speck. His teaching does not eliminate the responsibility to help another person. Jesus establishes the posture and order of correction: self-examination, repentance, and humility must precede the attempt to address another’s spiritual condition.

A person may approach correction confidently while lacking compassion. Their words may contain Scripture, yet their manner may reveal impatience, pride, resentment, or the desire to establish superiority. Biblical language does not automatically make every response faithful to Christ.

We may also treat our own failures as complicated, influenced by history, wounds, weakness, and the process of growth, while viewing another person’s struggles with far less patience. Such inconsistency should cause us to examine whether the standards we apply are truly biblical or whether some people are receiving scrutiny we would resist ourselves.

The righteousness built on comparison

In Luke 18:9–14 (AMPC), Jesus tells a parable to those who trusted in their own righteousness and looked down on others. The Pharisee’s prayer describes his standing in comparison with people he considers morally inferior. The tax collector acknowledges his need for mercy. Jesus declares the tax collector justified rather than the Pharisee.

The contrast exposes a danger within religious practice. Worship, observance, and knowledge can coexist with a heart that depends upon comparison rather than grace. When righteousness is measured by the perceived failures of others, we may lose sight of the mercy upon which our own relationship with God depends.

The tax collector does not establish his standing by listing others’ shortcomings. He acknowledges his need. Every believer must remain aware of that dependence. The church cannot faithfully proclaim grace while cultivating an atmosphere in which people compete to appear more righteous than one another.

This does not mean sin has no consequences or that repentance is unnecessary. The principle requires us to recognize sin honestly without using another person’s failure to avoid personal accountability.

The patience of God

Paul’s words in Romans 2:1–4 (AMPC) challenge those who judge others while practicing similar things. He reminds readers that God judges according to truth and that His kindness, forbearance, and patience lead people toward repentance. Recognizing wrongdoing in another person does not place the observer beyond examination.

God’s patience is not permission to remain in sin, and mercy does not eliminate accountability. His kindness creates an opportunity for repentance. Those who have experienced that kindness should be cautious about responding to another person’s need with contempt or impatience.

Within congregations, some people receive private counsel through struggles that never become public knowledge, while others face circumstances visible to the community. Visibility should not determine the mercy a person receives, nor should privacy be mistaken for greater faithfulness. God sees beyond appearances, and His Word calls every believer to examine the heart.

Judgment can also take place through gossip, repeated speculation about motives, or exclusion based on assumptions. A conversation may begin with concern and drift into conjecture. A question becomes an assumption, then a story repeated until opinions form without anyone speaking directly to the person involved. Such conduct can damage reputations while a congregation continues claiming to value compassion.

Believers are called to guard their speech. Words should encourage, instruct, correct, comfort, and build up. Conversations concerning another person’s spiritual condition require wisdom and a sincere pursuit of truth rather than the satisfaction of curiosity.

A concern may be shared with someone who has responsibility to help, yet become gossip when repeated without a purpose. The relevant questions are who needs to know, why the information is being shared, whether the person is being treated fairly, and whether the discussion can lead toward responsible action. Curiosity does not become spiritual concern simply through the use of religious language.

A person who offers correction should be willing to consider whether the conversation is necessary, whether the concern is based on reliable information, and whether the person being addressed has been given a fair opportunity to respond. Such questions do not weaken accountability. They protect accountability from becoming an expression of personal frustration or a means of establishing superiority. Truth should be pursued carefully, especially when the consequences of a judgment may affect someone's relationships or place within a community.

The responsibility to examine ourselves belongs to the whole congregation, not only to those who hold formal leadership. Members can contribute to an atmosphere of humility by refusing to repeat unverified stories, resisting partiality, speaking directly when appropriate, and being willing to acknowledge when their own words have caused harm. Leaders should model the same practices and make clear that accountability is not reserved for people with less influence.

Correction must seek restoration

Galatians 6:1 (AMPC) instructs believers that when someone is overtaken in a fault, those who are spiritual should restore that person in a spirit of meekness while considering themselves. The objective is restoration, and humility is the required posture.

Correction is not an opportunity to establish dominance. The person offering help must recognize their own vulnerability and dependence upon God. They should consider whether their words are truthful, whether the timing is wise, whether their motives are sincere, and whether their conduct reflects the humility Scripture requires. The goal is spiritual growth, not winning an argument.

Accountability belongs to the entire body of Christ. Leaders, teachers, members, and families all remain accountable. Spiritual authority must never become protection against examination. A person who demands repentance from others while refusing correction has misunderstood Christian leadership.

Selective attention weakens the witness

Judgment becomes especially visible when a congregation focuses on the sins associated with people outside its community while overlooking destructive conduct within its own relationships. Pride, partiality, gossip, unforgiveness, arrogance, and misuse of authority may receive little attention when protected by reputation or position.

Such selective attention does not strengthen the gospel. The church’s credibility suffers when members appear more concerned about the failures of others than their own obedience to Christ. The world does not need believers who claim moral superiority. The world needs believers whose lives demonstrate the transforming power of grace.

This is not a call to abandon biblical convictions. Scripture remains the authority for Christian faith and practice. The church must teach faithfully and recognize the seriousness of sin, while carrying those responsibilities with the humility Scripture requires. Truth must never become a tool for self-exaltation.

The cross leaves no room for spiritual arrogance. Every believer comes through grace and depends upon the mercy of Jesus Christ. Every believer needs forgiveness, guidance, correction, and transformation. Knowledge, influence, and reputation do not elevate anyone above that need.

The same Word must examine us

Before we identify the speck in another person’s eye, we must acknowledge the beam that may obstruct our own vision. Self-examination is not a reason to avoid helping others. Self-examination prepares us to approach them with humility, patience, and care.

The church must not allow the sins it recognizes in others to distract from the sins God is calling it to confront within itself. The same Scripture used to address another person’s life must remain open to the person holding the Bible. The same call to repentance applies to the one delivering the message, and the same mercy proclaimed to the congregation must shape the way the congregation treats people.

Our histories, struggles, and circumstances differ, but our need for Christ remains universal. No believer has earned the privilege of approaching God through personal superiority. We all stand in need of the mercy that makes salvation possible.

Truth is not diminished by humility, correction is not weakened by compassion, and holiness is not compromised by recognizing another person’s humanity. The ministry of Jesus calls us to examine personal conduct as carefully as we examine the conduct of others.

When we recognize failure in another person, the mercy that has sustained our own lives should shape our words, decisions, and treatment of them. The church is entrusted with proclaiming salvation, pursuing holiness, and demonstrating the love of Christ. Examining another person must never become a substitute for allowing God to examine us. Such examination should lead us toward humility and renewed hope, not a verdict that another person is beyond the reach of mercy.
