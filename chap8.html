Chapter Eight Sinners Judging Sinners Judgment becomes especially dangerous when the person delivering the judgment forgets their own need for mercy. A believer may become so focused on identifying the failures of another person that personal examination gradually disappears. The sins of others become subjects of conversation, correction, and condemnation, while the condition of one’s own heart receives far less attention. Scripture repeatedly confronts this tendency because religious confidence can create an illusion of righteousness that leaves a person blind to their own spiritual need. The problem is not that believers recognize sin. Scripture calls the church to discernment, correction, accountability, and holiness. The problem arises when discernment becomes an excuse for superiority, correction becomes humiliation, and accountability becomes something demanded of others while personally resisted. God’s Word was never intended to give people permission to elevate themselves above those whose struggles differ from their own. Jesus addressed this danger directly in Matthew 7:1–5. His teaching concerning judgment does not end with the instruction against judging. He continues by describing the person who notices a speck in another’s eye while overlooking a beam in their own. The illustration exposes the absurdity of attempting to correct someone while remaining unwilling to confront the condition of one’s own heart. The imagery is deliberately uncomfortable. Jesus does not describe a person who lacks the ability to recognize a problem. He describes someone whose attention has become so concentrated upon another person’s fault that their own condition has escaped honest examination. The problem is not merely inaccurate judgment. The problem is hypocrisy. Jesus concludes by instructing the person to remove the beam from their own eye before attempting to remove the speck from another’s. His teaching does not eliminate the responsibility to help someone. Rather, He establishes the order in which correction must occur. Personal repentance, humility, and self-examination must precede the attempt to address another person’s spiritual condition. The church must take that order seriously. A person who has never examined their own heart may approach correction with confidence but without compassion. Their words may contain Scripture, yet their spirit may reveal impatience, pride, resentment, or a desire to establish superiority. The presence of biblical language does not automatically make every response faithful to the character of Christ. The temptation to judge often becomes strongest when another person’s struggle appears different from our own. We may recognize our own failures as complicated, influenced by circumstances, wounds, history, weakness, or the process of growth. The failures of others, however, may be viewed with less patience. Their circumstances may receive little consideration, and their mistakes may be interpreted as evidence of a character we have already decided to condemn. Such inconsistency reveals how easily personal experience can influence the application of Scripture. The church must be willing to ask whether the standards being applied are genuinely biblical or whether certain people are receiving a level of scrutiny that others would never accept for themselves. Jesus’ teaching concerning the Pharisee and the tax collector in Luke 18:9–14 offers another examination of this attitude. He addressed those who trusted in their own righteousness and looked down upon others. The two men approached the temple to pray, yet their prayers revealed different understandings of their standing before God. The Pharisee expressed gratitude while describing himself in comparison with other people. His prayer centered upon the distance he believed separated him from those he considered morally inferior. The tax collector, standing apart, acknowledged his need for mercy. Jesus declared that the tax collector went home justified rather than the Pharisee. The contrast reveals a spiritual danger that can exist within religious practice. A person may participate in worship, observe religious expectations, and maintain an outward appearance of devotion while developing a heart that depends upon comparison rather than grace. The moment righteousness becomes measured by the perceived failures of others, the individual may lose sight of the mercy upon which their own relationship with God depends. The tax collector did not arrive with a list of other people’s shortcomings. He did not attempt to establish his standing by identifying those whose lives appeared worse. His prayer acknowledged his own need. That posture is essential to the Christian life because every believer must continually recognize their dependence upon God. The church cannot faithfully proclaim grace while cultivating an atmosphere in which people compete to appear more righteous than one another. Spiritual maturity does not require a person to pretend that sin has no consequences. Spiritual maturity requires the honesty to recognize sin without using another person’s failure as a means of avoiding personal accountability. Paul’s words in Romans 2:1–4 further challenge those who judge others while practicing similar things. He reminds his readers that God’s judgment is according to truth and that His kindness, forbearance, and patience lead people toward repentance. The passage confronts the assumption that recognizing wrongdoing in another person places the observer beyond examination. The kindness of God deserves careful consideration. Divine patience is not permission to remain in sin, nor does mercy eliminate accountability. God’s kindness creates an opportunity for repentance. The believer who has experienced that kindness should be cautious about responding to another person’s need with contempt, impatience, or a desire for punishment. The church must never forget how much patience has been extended to those within its own walls. Many believers have experienced seasons of spiritual confusion, failure, restoration, and growth. Some have received counsel privately. Others have been supported through circumstances that never became public knowledge. The congregation may celebrate the testimony of a person whose struggles were known only to a few, while remaining unwilling to extend similar compassion to someone whose circumstances are more visible. Visibility should not determine the level of mercy a person receives. A struggle that becomes public does not automatically become more deserving of condemnation than a struggle kept private. God’s knowledge extends beyond appearances, and His Word calls every believer to examine the heart. The church must also recognize that judgment can occur through more than spoken condemnation. Gossip may become a form of judgment. Repeated speculation concerning someone’s motives may become a form of judgment. Excluding a person from meaningful relationships because of assumptions may become a form of judgment. A careless comment can communicate a verdict long before a person has been given the opportunity to explain their circumstances. These behaviors often develop gradually. A conversation begins with concern, then moves toward speculation. A question becomes an assumption. An assumption becomes a repeated story. Over time, the person being discussed may find that others have formed opinions without ever approaching them directly. The church may continue speaking about compassion while practicing a culture in which reputations are damaged through conversations that never lead toward restoration. Scripture calls believers to guard their speech. The responsibility extends beyond avoiding obvious lies. Words should be used to encourage, instruct, correct, comfort, and build up. Conversations concerning another person’s spiritual condition should be handled with seriousness, wisdom, and a willingness to pursue truth rather than merely satisfy curiosity. Correction is a responsibility that requires humility. Galatians 6:1 instructs believers that when someone is overtaken in a fault, those who are spiritual should restore such a person in a spirit of meekness while considering themselves, lest they also be tempted. The instruction identifies restoration as the objective and humility as the posture. The passage does not describe correction as an opportunity to establish dominance. Restoration requires an understanding that the person being helped remains a human being whose spiritual well-being matters. The one offering correction must recognize their own vulnerability and dependence upon God. A believer who approaches someone with the intention of restoring them should be concerned about more than winning an argument. They should consider whether their words are truthful, whether their timing is wise, whether their motives are sincere, and whether their conduct reflects the humility Scripture requires. The goal should be spiritual growth, not personal satisfaction. The church often speaks about accountability, yet accountability must be understood as a responsibility shared by the body of Christ. Leaders must remain accountable. Teachers must remain accountable. Members must remain accountable. Families must remain accountable. No one should be permitted to use spiritual authority as protection against examination. A person who demands repentance from others while refusing correction themselves has misunderstood the nature of Christian leadership. Authority within the church is not a declaration of personal perfection. Leadership requires greater responsibility, greater humility, and a willingness to receive correction when necessary. The danger of judging others becomes especially visible when a congregation discusses people whose lives differ from the expectations of its members. Conversations may focus on particular sins while neglecting the biblical responsibilities concerning pride, partiality, mercy, patience, and love. The church may carefully examine the behavior of someone outside its community while overlooking destructive conduct within its own relationships. Such selective attention does not strengthen the gospel. The church’s credibility is damaged when its members appear more concerned about the failures of others than about their own obedience to Christ. The world does not need believers who claim moral superiority. The world needs believers who faithfully demonstrate the transforming power of grace. This is not a call to abandon biblical convictions. Scripture remains the authority for Christian faith and practice. The church must teach the Word faithfully and recognize the seriousness of sin. Those responsibilities must be carried with the same humility Scripture demands from those who teach. Truth should never become a tool for self-exaltation. The cross leaves no room for spiritual arrogance. Every believer comes to God through grace. Every believer depends upon the mercy of Jesus Christ. Every believer needs forgiveness, guidance, correction, and transformation. The ground beneath the cross does not become higher for those who possess more biblical knowledge, greater influence, or a more respected reputation. The church should therefore approach conversations concerning sin with a deep awareness of its own dependence upon God. Such awareness does not weaken the message of repentance. It strengthens the message by reminding believers that repentance is not a burden imposed upon an inferior group of people. Repentance is a necessary response to God for every person who desires to walk faithfully with Him. The person receiving correction should not be treated as though the one offering correction has never needed the same grace. The person teaching should not assume that knowledge has removed the need for personal examination. The person exercising authority should remember that authority does not exempt them from the judgment of God. A church that practices this kind of humility can address difficult matters without creating an atmosphere of superiority. Members can speak honestly, ask questions, receive correction, and pursue growth without turning every disagreement into a declaration of someone’s worth. Such a community remains committed to truth while recognizing that every person within the community needs the transforming work of Christ. The words of Jesus in Matthew 7 remain a necessary examination for every believer. Before identifying the speck in another person’s eye, we must be willing to acknowledge the beam that may obstruct our own vision. That examination is not a reason to avoid helping others. It is a reason to approach them with greater humility, patience, and care. The church must never allow the sins it recognizes in others to become a distraction from the sins God is calling it to confront within itself. The same Scripture used to address another person’s life must remain open to the person holding the Bible. The same call to repentance must remain applicable to the one delivering the message. The same mercy proclaimed to the congregation must shape the way the congregation treats people beyond its walls. We are all dependent upon grace. Our histories differ, our struggles differ, and our circumstances differ, yet our need for Christ remains universal. No believer has earned the privilege of approaching God through personal superiority. Every believer stands in need of the mercy that makes salvation possible. The church must remember that truth is not diminished by humility, correction is not weakened by compassion, and holiness is not compromised by recognizing the humanity of another person. The ministry of Jesus calls believers toward a faith that examines personal conduct as carefully as it examines the conduct of others. A congregation that learns to approach people through humility rather than superiority can become a place where correction leads toward restoration and where accountability is accompanied by compassion. Such a church understands that the gospel is not a declaration of personal achievement. The gospel is the announcement of what God has done through Jesus Christ for people who need Him. The next time we recognize a failure in another person, we must remember the mercy that has sustained our own lives. That remembrance should shape the words we speak, the decisions we make, and the way we treat those whose circumstances challenge our understanding. The church has been entrusted with the message of salvation. Our responsibility is to proclaim that message faithfully, live according to Scripture, pursue holiness, and demonstrate the love of Christ. The work of examining another person’s life must never become a substitute for the work of allowing God to examine our own.
