PART II · THE PEOPLE WE DECIDED AGAINST

Sinners Judging Sinners

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 8 of 17Reading edition

Jesus' warning about the speck and the beam in Matthew 7:1–51 is familiar, perhaps familiar enough that we forget how uncomfortable the picture is. Someone is trying to correct another person's vision while refusing to notice the obstruction in their own. The problem is not that correction has no place. The problem is the posture of the person offering it.

We know how easy comparison can become. We may treat our own failures as complicated, shaped by circumstances and weakness, while describing someone else's struggle with a much simpler judgment. We can be patient with ourselves and impatient with others. That difference deserves attention.

In Luke 18:9–14,2 Jesus tells of a Pharisee who measures his righteousness against others and a tax collector who asks for mercy. The tax collector does not build his prayer around someone else's failure. He recognizes his need for God. The parable reminds us that spiritual confidence can become misplaced when we depend on comparison.

Recognizing sin remains part of Christian faithfulness. Scripture calls us to discern, correct, and pursue holiness. Yet Galatians 6:13 says restoration should be approached with meekness, while those helping should consider themselves. The person offering correction is not standing outside the need for grace.

That is why gossip and speculation are so damaging. A concern can begin with a desire to help and drift into a story repeated for curiosity. Before sharing something about another person, we should ask who needs to know, what purpose the conversation serves, and whether the facts are reliable. Religious concern does not make unnecessary exposure harmless.

The church needs the courage to examine its own conduct as carefully as it examines the conduct of others. Our standards should not change according to someone's influence, reputation, or proximity to us. The same Word that corrects our neighbor must remain open to correcting us.

Humility does not mean avoiding difficult conversations. Humility means approaching them aware that we, too, need repentance, patience, and mercy. When we remember that, correction can become an act of care rather than a way to prove ourselves right.

1. Matthew 7:1–5 warns against judging others while refusing to examine our own faults, using the image of a speck and a beam.

2. Luke 18:9–14 contrasts a self-righteous prayer with the humble plea for mercy from a tax collector, showing that justification is not gained through comparison.

3. Galatians 6:1 calls believers to restore someone caught in wrongdoing with gentleness while remaining mindful of their own vulnerability.
