CHAPTER SIXTEEN We Were Never the Gatekeepers There is a difference between opening a door and claiming ownership of the doorway. There is a difference between guiding someone toward a destination and deciding whether that person is permitted to arrive. There is a difference between serving under authority and assuming authority that was never entrusted to us. The church has been entrusted with an extraordinary responsibility. We are called to preach the gospel, teach the Word of God, make disciples, encourage believers, care for people, and faithfully represent Jesus Christ. Those responsibilities require spiritual discernment, leadership, accountability, and order. Yet responsibility can become distorted when people begin to believe that their position gives them control over the spiritual destiny of others. A congregation may establish membership requirements. A ministry may maintain appropriate boundaries. Leaders may address misconduct, protect people, provide correction, and exercise responsibilities according to Scripture. Such matters require thoughtful and responsible leadership. The difficulty arises when the authority necessary to serve becomes an assumed authority to determine who may approach Jesus, who deserves mercy, who is worthy of hearing the gospel, or whose spiritual journey may be dismissed. Christ remains the Shepherd In John 10:11–18 (AMPC), Jesus described Himself as the Good Shepherd and also declared Himself to be the door for the sheep. His teaching addressed the relationship between Himself, those who belong to Him, and the protection and life He provides. Jesus did not describe Himself as one person among many who could determine access to the sheep. He presented Himself as the One through whom the sheep enter and receive life. His identity and authority stand at the center of the passage. Spiritual leaders may serve, teach, guide, correct, and protect, yet none of those responsibilities changes the identity of the Savior. When the church begins to behave as though the invitation to Jesus requires its personal approval, the distinction between service and ownership becomes blurred. A believer may help someone understand Scripture. A pastor may provide spiritual guidance. A congregation may offer fellowship and discipleship. Those responsibilities can be meaningful and necessary. The authority to determine the eternal destiny of a human soul, however, does not belong to a religious observer. The church must never confuse the responsibility of carrying the gospel with the authority of owning the invitation. The keys do not make us owners Matthew 16:18–19 (AMPC) contains Jesus’ words to Peter concerning the keys of the kingdom of heaven and the authority associated with binding and loosing. The passage has been interpreted and understood in different ways across Christian traditions, and the church should approach such discussions with care rather than assuming every question has been resolved by a personal conclusion. The passage clearly places spiritual responsibility within the context of Christ’s authority and the revelation concerning His identity. Jesus had asked His disciples who they believed He was. Peter confessed that Jesus was the Christ, the Son of the living God. Jesus responded to that confession and spoke concerning the foundation upon which He would build His church. The authority discussed within the passage must be understood in relation to the identity, purpose, and authority of Jesus rather than treated as permission for human beings to exercise unlimited control over others. The responsibility of leadership should produce humility, accountability, and careful stewardship. Leaders must recognize that the people they serve belong to God. Their responsibilities are serious precisely because they are entrusted with something that is not their personal possession. The church must be careful not to turn passages concerning spiritual responsibility into instruments of personal domination. The keys of the kingdom should never become an excuse for treating people as though the mercy of God were subject to the preferences of a religious leader. Whatever authority is exercised within the church must remain accountable to Christ, consistent with Scripture, and directed toward the purposes of God. The Savior remains the One who calls Hebrews 4:14–16 (AMPC) encourages believers to hold firmly to their confession and approach the throne of grace with confidence because of Jesus Christ, the great High Priest. The passage presents Jesus as the One who understands human weakness and provides the foundation for approaching God. The invitation is to receive mercy and find grace for help in time of need. The language is powerful because the access described does not depend upon the perfection of the person approaching. The church should never communicate in a manner that causes people to believe access to God depends upon the approval of a human being who has placed themselves in the position of spiritual gatekeeper. People may need guidance, teaching, pastoral care, accountability, or encouragement from other believers. Those responsibilities should direct people toward Christ rather than create the impression that Christ cannot receive them without the permission of a congregation. A person who needs prayer should not be treated as though their circumstances make prayer inappropriate. The responsibility of believers is to help people understand the Savior through whom grace is available. Stewards, not owners The church is entrusted with the gospel, Scripture, the responsibility of making disciples, and the responsibility of caring for people. A steward cannot simply do whatever is personally preferred and claim that the authority of the owner supports the decision. The steward must act according to the purpose of the trust. When leaders establish policies, make decisions, correct people, or address difficult circumstances, they should consider whether their actions faithfully serve the purpose for which spiritual responsibility was entrusted. The purpose of spiritual leadership is not to create an environment where people become dependent upon the approval of a particular individual. The purpose is to help people grow toward maturity in Christ. A leader who understands stewardship will recognize that the people being served are not personal possessions. Such a leader will resist the temptation to control the spiritual lives of others beyond the responsibilities established by Scripture. The church must never forget the difference between being entrusted with responsibility and being granted ownership. We may want to know who belongs, who understands, who is trustworthy, who is sincere, and who will respond appropriately to instruction. Institutions require organization, and leadership requires discernment. However, the desire for certainty can become excessive when people begin attempting to control matters that belong to God. We may conclude that someone has no meaningful relationship with God because their spiritual journey does not resemble ours. We may believe that our ability to recognize certain outward characteristics gives us authority to determine the condition of someone’s heart. Such assumptions can become dangerous when treated as though they possess the authority of Scripture. Human beings observe outward circumstances and make judgments based upon limited understanding. We may recognize conduct that requires correction, evaluate circumstances responsibly, and teach biblical principles. We must nevertheless remain aware of the limits of our understanding. Romans 14 addresses disagreements among believers concerning matters of conscience, practices, and the way people evaluated one another. Paul emphasized that believers should not assume the position of ultimate judgment over another person’s relationship with God. The principle is not an excuse for abandoning biblical teaching. The surrounding discussion concerns the responsibility of believers to avoid contempt, judgmental attitudes, and unnecessary division over matters addressed within the passage. Paul’s instruction challenges the church to recognize that spiritual accountability ultimately belongs to God. We must be careful not to treat personal certainty as divine authority or assume that our ability to observe someone gives us the right to pronounce a final verdict concerning that person’s soul. The church should teach faithfully while recognizing that God alone possesses complete knowledge of the human heart. Boundaries and barriers Churches must protect children and vulnerable people. Ministries must establish responsible practices. Leaders must address misconduct. Congregations must maintain order. Some situations require restrictions, intervention, accountability, or decisions that can be difficult. The existence of such responsibilities does not contradict the invitation of Christ. A boundary established to protect someone from harm serves a different purpose from a barrier established to keep someone away because their presence makes others uncomfortable. A responsible boundary should have a legitimate purpose, be applied thoughtfully, and remain consistent with the principles of Scripture. It should not become an excuse for unnecessary humiliation or discrimination, nor should it disguise personal reluctance as spiritual authority. The church should never assume maintaining order requires contempt. Protecting people does not require dehumanizing others. Exercising authority does not require abandoning humility. The distinction between a necessary boundary and an unnecessary barrier should be examined carefully rather than dismissed through broad assumptions. The Spirit guides toward truth Jesus spoke about the work of the Holy Spirit in John 16:13–15 (AMPC). He described the Spirit’s ministry in relation to truth, conviction, and the revelation of what was to come. A person may be moved by a message, recognize a need for repentance, begin asking questions about faith, or experience a desire to seek God more deeply. The church’s responsibility is to faithfully serve those opportunities rather than attempt to control every response. Manipulation may produce outward compliance, yet outward compliance does not necessarily represent the inward transformation the gospel describes. People should not be pressured into making declarations merely to satisfy the expectations of others, humiliated into responses presented as evidence of spiritual sincerity, or treated as though the church’s ability to control their behavior determines the work of God within them. The church must faithfully proclaim the message while recognizing that spiritual transformation belongs to God. We can carry the gospel, pray for people, teach Scripture, encourage obedience, and serve with compassion. The responsibility is significant, but the responsibility does not make us owners of the spiritual lives of others. Authority must remain accountable The misuse of spiritual authority can become especially damaging when leaders begin to treat disagreement as disloyalty. A person who questions a decision may be accused of rebellion. Someone who expresses concern may be described as divisive. A person who asks for clarification may be treated as though they are challenging the authority of God. Leadership should never require the abandonment of personal conscience or the refusal to examine decisions. The church must recognize that spiritual leaders remain human beings who need accountability, wisdom, correction, and humility. Jesus addressed the nature of leadership in Matthew 20:25–28 (AMPC), contrasting the way worldly rulers exercise authority with the responsibility of His disciples to serve. He taught that greatness among His followers should be expressed through service and that the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve and give His life. The church must remember that spiritual authority is intended to serve people rather than establish personal ownership over them. A leader who understands the teaching of Jesus should be willing to listen, acknowledge mistakes, and exercise authority with humility. The authority exercised within the church must remain consistent with the example and teaching of Christ. The church must not confuse these responsibilities with the authority to determine the final limits of God’s mercy. The Bible contains accounts of people whose lives were transformed in ways religious observers did not expect. Zacchaeus responded to the encounter with Jesus through repentance and restitution. The thief on the cross received a promise from Jesus in circumstances that could not be reduced to the expectations of religious observers. These accounts remind us that the work of God cannot be limited by our assumptions concerning the people He receives. The church should remain humble before the One who knows the heart, understands every circumstance, and possesses authority over salvation. The commission belongs to Christ The Great Commission in Matthew 28:18–20 (AMPC) begins with the authority of Jesus. He declared that all authority in heaven and on earth had been given to Him. He then commissioned His disciples to go, make disciples, baptize, and teach people to observe His commandments. The church is not the source of the authority behind the commission. The church serves under the authority of Christ. We should not ask how to preserve personal control over the people entrusted to our care, determine which people are worthy of hearing the gospel, or establish ourselves as the final authority over another person’s spiritual journey. We should ask how to teach the commandments of Jesus while representing His character. Those responsibilities require courage, wisdom, conviction, compassion, humility, and accountability. We were never given permission to treat people as though their spiritual destiny belonged to our approval. The church must faithfully carry the gospel while remembering that the gospel belongs to Christ. The people who desire to know Jesus must not be made to believe the invitation belongs to someone else. Our responsibility is to faithfully carry His message and never presume that the invitation belongs to us.
