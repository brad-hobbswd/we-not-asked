CHAPTER SIXTEEN We Were Never the Gatekeepers Gatekeeping can begin with a responsibility that is legitimate. Someone must organize a ministry, protect vulnerable people, teach the congregation, maintain appropriate procedures, and address conduct that affects the community. Those responsibilities require judgment. The problem arises when judgment becomes personal control, when a decision is made without a clear purpose, or when the people affected are treated as though their dignity depends upon the approval of those making the decision. The church must distinguish between stewardship and ownership. A steward manages what has been entrusted for a purpose and remains accountable for the manner in which the responsibility is carried. A gatekeeper who assumes personal ownership may begin to treat access, participation, attention, or belonging as privileges distributed according to preference. The difference should be visible in the reasons behind decisions, the way decisions are communicated, and the willingness of leaders to receive correction. When responsibility becomes control A congregation may establish membership requirements, ministry expectations, safeguarding practices, and procedures for addressing misconduct. Such responsibilities are not inherently oppressive. They may protect people, support accountability, and help a ministry function responsibly. The concern arises when a decision is based primarily on personal discomfort, an unexamined assumption, a desire to preserve status, or an expectation that people must agree without understanding. A policy may sound official while reflecting a preference that has never been examined through Scripture. A leader may call a decision necessary without explaining the actual responsibility it serves. Those affected by decisions should not be treated as though asking for clarification is automatically disrespectful. A responsible leader should be able to explain the purpose of a requirement, consider whether the requirement is being applied consistently, and recognize when a practice needs reconsideration. Authority should be exercised to serve the community, not to protect a leader from questions. The people are not possessions The church is responsible to care for people, but people are not the property of a congregation or ministry. Their spiritual growth cannot be claimed as personal achievement, and their choices should not become a measure of a leader’s control. This distinction matters when someone leaves a ministry, changes congregations, asks questions, disagrees with a decision, or seeks help outside the circle of familiar leadership. Such choices may require conversation, clarification, or pastoral care. They do not automatically establish disloyalty or rebellion. A healthy church recognizes that relationships are not maintained through pressure. People may need to make decisions concerning their families, spiritual care, safety, conscience, and circumstances. Leaders can offer guidance without treating every decision as an opportunity to demonstrate authority. The responsibility of ministry is to help people grow toward maturity in Christ. Mature discipleship should not create dependence upon a particular leader's approval. It should equip people to understand Scripture, exercise discernment, serve faithfully, and remain accountable to God. Conscience and disagreement Romans 14 addresses disagreements among believers concerning matters of conscience and practices. Paul warns against contempt and judgment between people who are responding differently within the matters he discusses. His instruction reminds the church that personal conviction must not become an excuse for despising another believer. The passage does not erase biblical teaching or suggest that every matter is morally neutral. Its context concerns the way believers treat one another when disagreements arise over particular practices. The principle is that God remains the ultimate judge, and Christians should not turn personal conclusions into a reason for contempt or unnecessary division. A congregation should take care when a matter involves conscience, interpretation, or a decision that Scripture does not explicitly settle in the way the community assumes. Leaders should distinguish what the Word clearly requires from the practices established by the congregation. That distinction allows teaching to remain honest and decisions to be explained without claiming divine authority for every preference. A person may need instruction, and a leader may need to make a decision. The manner of communication should still respect the person affected. Clarity is better than intimidation. Explanation is better than unexplained exclusion. Responsible conversation can address disagreement without turning the relationship into a contest. Boundaries that protect, barriers that exclude Churches must protect children and vulnerable people. Ministries must establish responsible practices. Leaders must address misconduct. Congregations must maintain order. Some situations require restrictions, intervention, accountability, or decisions that can be difficult. The existence of such responsibilities does not contradict the invitation of Christ. A boundary established to protect someone from harm serves a different purpose from a barrier established to keep someone away because their presence makes others uncomfortable. A responsible boundary should have a legitimate purpose, be applied thoughtfully, and remain consistent with Scripture. It should not become an excuse for unnecessary humiliation, nor should personal reluctance be disguised as spiritual authority. The church should never assume maintaining order requires contempt. Protecting people does not require dehumanizing others. Exercising authority does not require abandoning humility. The distinction between a necessary boundary and an unnecessary barrier should be examined carefully rather than dismissed through broad assumptions. When a decision affects participation, access, or responsibility, leaders should consider whether the reason is clear, whether the response is proportionate, whether the same standard is applied consistently, and whether the person is given an appropriate opportunity to understand and respond. Such examination does not guarantee agreement. It demonstrates that the decision is being treated as a serious responsibility rather than a personal preference. The language of stewardship Stewardship should produce humility rather than entitlement. A person entrusted with responsibility manages what belongs to another and remains accountable for how that responsibility is carried. The more responsibility a person receives, the more carefully that person should consider whether decisions serve the purpose for which the responsibility was entrusted. A ministry leader may organize service and establish reasonable procedures, yet those procedures remain accountable to the purpose of ministry and the principles of Scripture. A pastor may provide guidance, but pastoral care should not make people dependent upon personal approval. A teacher is responsible for helping learners understand, not using questions to establish the teacher's authority. The church must also be cautious about using the phrase “God told me” to end a conversation that requires biblical examination. Spiritual impressions may be sincerely held, yet sincerity alone does not make an impression infallible. The written Word remains the standard by which teaching, decisions, and conduct are tested. A claim of divine direction should never become a means of shielding personal preferences from reasonable questions or accountability. Responsible communication People should not be left to discover a decision through embarrassment, gossip, or public speculation. When a matter affects someone directly, leaders should communicate as clearly and privately as circumstances permit. The explanation should identify the concern, the responsibility involved, and the steps available where appropriate. Privacy matters. Information should be shared only with those who need it for a legitimate purpose. A person's circumstances should not become public material simply because a decision has been made. The church must resist the temptation to explain its actions through unnecessary disclosure or to protect its reputation by exposing someone else's life. Communication should also allow for questions. A person may not agree with a decision, but they should not be treated as though disagreement automatically reveals a rebellious heart. Leaders should listen, consider the concern, and be willing to acknowledge when a decision has been poorly handled or needs correction. When the decision is wrong No leader or congregation is beyond error. A decision may be made from incomplete information, a misunderstanding of Scripture, an inconsistent standard, or a preference that has been mistaken for necessity. The church must be willing to recognize such possibilities. Accountability should not be treated as a threat to spiritual authority. It protects the people affected and helps ensure that authority remains directed toward service. Leaders should be able to seek counsel, review decisions, admit mistakes, and make appropriate changes. When harm has occurred, repair may require more than a private apology. The response should be proportionate to the nature of the harm. Public embarrassment may require public correction. Mishandled information may require steps to protect privacy. Decisions that affected participation may need to be reviewed. Those affected should not be pressured to restore trust before meaningful accountability has taken place. The purpose is not to weaken leadership or create suspicion toward every decision. The purpose is to ensure that leadership reflects the character of Christ and remains accountable to the Word. Serving rather than controlling Jesus addressed the nature of leadership in Matthew 20:25–28 (AMPC), contrasting the way worldly rulers exercise authority with the responsibility of His disciples to serve. He taught that greatness among His followers should be expressed through service and that the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve and give His life. The teaching gives the church a standard for examining the way authority is exercised. A leader should not need to humiliate someone to demonstrate responsibility. A ministry should not need to silence questions to preserve order. A congregation should not need to pressure people to remain in a relationship to prove unity. Service requires attention to the people affected by decisions. It requires the willingness to explain, listen, protect, correct, and sometimes reconsider. Authority becomes trustworthy when those who exercise it recognize that they are accountable to Christ and that the people they serve have dignity that does not depend upon their approval. We were never the gatekeepers The church has been entrusted with teaching Scripture, proclaiming the gospel, making disciples, providing care, and exercising responsible leadership. Those responsibilities matter. They must be carried with discernment, integrity, humility, and accountability. We must not turn stewardship into ownership, boundaries into unnecessary barriers, or leadership into control. We must not treat people as though access to spiritual care depends upon satisfying personal preferences. We must not assume that the ability to make a decision grants permission to make every decision without examination. When people seek Jesus, our responsibility is to faithfully carry His message and serve them according to Scripture. When a legitimate boundary is necessary, we should explain and exercise it with care. When a barrier exists only because of our discomfort, pride, or unexamined assumptions, we should be willing to remove it. The church is not the Savior. We are servants entrusted with the message, the ministry, and the care of people. Our authority is limited, our decisions remain accountable, and our calling is to help people follow Christ rather than claim ownership over their spiritual journey. A church that understands stewardship will serve people without claiming ownership of their spiritual journey. The invitation belongs to Christ, and the responsibility of His servants is to carry it faithfully.
