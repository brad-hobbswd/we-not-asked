Chapter 16 We Were Never the Gatekeepers There is a difference between opening a door and claiming ownership of the doorway. There is a difference between guiding someone toward a destination and deciding whether that person is permitted to arrive. There is a difference between serving under authority and assuming authority that was never given. The church has been entrusted with an extraordinary responsibility. We are called to preach the gospel, teach the Word of God, make disciples, encourage believers, care for people, and faithfully represent Jesus Christ. Those responsibilities require spiritual maturity, discernment, accountability, and a willingness to serve. Yet responsibility can become distorted when people begin to believe that their position gives them control over the spiritual destiny of others. A congregation may establish membership requirements. A ministry may maintain appropriate boundaries. Leaders may address misconduct, protect people, provide correction, and exercise responsibilities according to Scripture. Such matters require thoughtful and faithful leadership. The difficulty arises when the authority necessary to serve becomes an assumed authority to determine who may approach Jesus, who deserves mercy, who is worthy of hearing the gospel, or whose spiritual journey may be dismissed. The church was never the owner of salvation. Jesus Christ is the Savior. The invitation to come to Him belongs to Him. The authority of the church must always remain accountable to the One who commissioned it. The Door Belongs to Christ In John 10, Jesus described Himself as the Good Shepherd and also declared Himself to be the door for the sheep. His teaching addressed the relationship between Himself, those who belong to Him, and the protection and life He provides. The imagery is significant. Jesus did not describe Himself as one person among many who could determine access to the sheep. He presented Himself as the one through whom the sheep enter and receive life. His identity and authority stand at the center of the passage. The church does not replace Christ. Pastors do not replace Christ. Religious institutions do not replace Christ. Spiritual leaders may serve, teach, guide, correct, and protect, yet none of those responsibilities changes the identity of the Savior. When the church begins to behave as though the invitation to Jesus requires its personal approval, the distinction between service and ownership becomes blurred. A believer may help someone understand Scripture. A pastor may provide spiritual guidance. A congregation may offer fellowship and discipleship. Those responsibilities can be meaningful and necessary. However, the authority to save belongs to Jesus. The authority to forgive sins ultimately rests with God. The authority to determine the eternal destiny of a human soul does not belong to a religious observer. The church must never confuse the responsibility of carrying the gospel with the authority of owning the invitation. The Keys Were Never an Invitation to Control People Matthew 16 contains Jesus’ words to Peter concerning the keys of the kingdom of heaven and the authority associated with binding and loosing. The passage has been interpreted and understood in different ways across Christian traditions, and the church should approach the text carefully rather than making personal assumptions its final authority. The passage clearly places spiritual responsibility within the context of Christ’s authority and the revelation concerning His identity. Jesus had asked His disciples who they believed He was. Peter confessed that Jesus was the Christ, the Son of the living God. Jesus responded to that confession and spoke concerning the foundation upon which He would build His church. The passage does not present the church as independent of Christ. The church belongs to Him. The authority discussed within the passage must be understood in relation to the identity, purpose, and authority of Jesus rather than treated as permission for human beings to exercise unlimited control over others. Religious authority is never a license for pride. The responsibility of leadership should produce humility, accountability, and careful stewardship. Leaders must recognize that the people they serve belong to God. Their responsibilities are serious precisely because they are entrusted with something that does not belong to them. The church must be careful not to turn passages concerning spiritual responsibility into instruments of personal domination. The keys of the kingdom should never become an excuse for treating people as though the mercy of God were subject to the preferences of a religious leader. Whatever authority is exercised within the church must remain accountable to Christ, consistent with Scripture, and directed toward the purposes of God. Access to God Is Not Owned by a Congregation Hebrews 4:14–16 encourages believers to hold firmly to their confession and approach the throne of grace with confidence because of Jesus Christ, the great High Priest. The passage presents Jesus as the one who understands human weakness and provides the foundation for approaching God. The invitation is to receive mercy and find grace for help in time of need. The language is powerful because the access described does not depend upon the perfection of the person approaching. The passage acknowledges weakness. The passage acknowledges the need for mercy. The passage acknowledges the need for grace. The believer approaches God through the ministry of Jesus Christ. The church should never communicate in a manner that causes people to believe that access to God depends upon the approval of a human being who has placed themselves in the position of spiritual gatekeeper. People may need guidance. They may need teaching. They may need pastoral care. They may need accountability. They may need encouragement from other believers. Those responsibilities should direct people toward Christ rather than create the impression that Christ cannot receive them without the permission of the congregation. A person who has questions should be encouraged to seek understanding. A person who recognizes sin should be directed toward repentance. A person who is grieving should be offered compassion. A person who needs prayer should not be treated as though their circumstances make prayer inappropriate. A person who desires to know Jesus should be encouraged to pursue Him. The church must remember that the throne described in Hebrews is a throne of grace. The responsibility of believers is to help people understand the Savior through whom that grace is available. The Church Is a Steward, Not an Owner A steward is entrusted with responsibility over something belonging to another. The distinction matters. The church is entrusted with the gospel. The church is entrusted with Scripture. The church is entrusted with the responsibility of making disciples and caring for people. The church is entrusted with the responsibility of teaching faithfully and living in a manner consistent with the message it proclaims. Stewardship requires accountability. A steward cannot simply do whatever is personally preferred and claim that the authority of the owner supports the decision. The steward must act according to the purpose of the trust. The church must examine its conduct according to the same principle. When leaders establish policies, make decisions, correct people, or address difficult circumstances, they should consider whether their actions faithfully serve the purpose for which spiritual responsibility was entrusted to them. Are we helping people understand Scripture? Are we protecting people from genuine harm? Are we encouraging repentance and spiritual growth? Are we making disciples? Are we serving the people entrusted to our care? Are we representing Jesus faithfully? These questions should govern the exercise of authority. The purpose of spiritual leadership is not to create an environment where people become dependent upon the approval of a particular individual. The purpose is to help people grow toward maturity in Christ. A leader who understands stewardship will recognize the limits of personal authority. A leader who understands stewardship will remain accountable. A leader who understands stewardship will recognize that the people being served are not personal possessions. A leader who understands stewardship will resist the temptation to control the spiritual lives of others beyond the responsibilities established by Scripture. The church must never forget the difference between being entrusted with responsibility and being granted ownership. When We Attempt to Control What Belongs to God Human beings naturally desire certainty. We want to know who belongs, who understands, who is trustworthy, who is sincere, and who will respond appropriately to instruction. Institutions require organization, and leadership requires discernment. However, the desire for certainty can become excessive when people begin attempting to control matters that belong to God. We may attempt to determine the sincerity of another person’s faith through assumptions. We may conclude that someone has no meaningful relationship with God because their spiritual journey does not resemble ours. We may treat unfamiliar circumstances as evidence of spiritual failure. We may believe that our ability to recognize certain outward characteristics gives us authority to determine the condition of someone’s heart. Such assumptions can become dangerous when they are treated as though they possess the authority of Scripture. God knows the heart. Human beings observe outward circumstances and make judgments based upon limited understanding. We may recognize conduct that requires correction. We may evaluate circumstances responsibly. We may teach biblical principles. Yet we must remain aware of the limits of our knowledge. Romans 14 addresses disagreements among believers concerning matters of conscience, practices, and the way people evaluated one another. Paul emphasized that believers should not assume the position of ultimate judgment over another person’s relationship with God. The chapter reminds readers that each person stands accountable before the Lord. The principle is not an excuse for abandoning biblical teaching. The surrounding discussion concerns the responsibility of believers to avoid contempt, judgmental attitudes, and unnecessary division over matters addressed within the passage. Paul’s instruction challenges the church to recognize that spiritual accountability ultimately belongs to God. We must be careful not to claim knowledge that God has not given us. We must be careful not to treat personal certainty as though personal certainty were divine authority. We must be careful not to assume that our ability to observe someone gives us the right to pronounce the final verdict concerning that person’s soul. The church should teach faithfully while recognizing that God alone possesses complete knowledge of the human heart. The Difference Between a Boundary and a Barrier Boundaries can serve necessary purposes. Churches must protect children and vulnerable people. Ministries must establish responsible practices. Leaders must address misconduct. Congregations must maintain order. Some situations require restrictions, intervention, accountability, or decisions that cannot be avoided. The existence of boundaries does not automatically represent gatekeeping. A boundary established to protect someone from harm serves a different purpose from a barrier established to keep someone away because their presence makes others uncomfortable. The distinction requires discernment. A responsible boundary should have a legitimate purpose, be applied thoughtfully, and remain consistent with the principles of Scripture. It should not become an excuse for unnecessary humiliation or discrimination. It should not be used to disguise personal resentment, fear, or prejudice as spiritual authority. The church must examine the reasons behind its decisions. A policy may be necessary. A restriction may be justified. A correction may be appropriate. A leadership decision may require serious consideration. However, the people affected by those decisions remain worthy of dignity. The church should never assume that maintaining order requires contempt. Protecting people does not require dehumanizing others. Exercising authority does not require the abandonment of humility. The distinction between a necessary boundary and an unnecessary barrier should be examined carefully rather than dismissed through broad assumptions. The responsibility of leadership is to serve the purposes of God faithfully. The Church Cannot Replace the Work of the Holy Spirit Jesus spoke about the work of the Holy Spirit in John 16. He described the Spirit’s ministry in relation to truth, conviction, and the revelation of what was to come. The work of the Holy Spirit is not something the church can manufacture through manipulation. Believers can preach. Believers can teach. Believers can pray. Believers can encourage. Believers can provide opportunities for people to hear and respond to the gospel. The church cannot control the inward work of God through human pressure. A person may be moved by a message. Someone may recognize a need for repentance. Someone may begin asking questions about faith. Someone may experience a desire to seek God more deeply. The church’s responsibility is to faithfully serve those opportunities rather than attempt to control every response. Manipulation may produce outward compliance, yet outward compliance does not necessarily represent the inward transformation the gospel describes. People should not be pressured into making declarations merely to satisfy the expectations of others. People should not be humiliated into responses that are presented as evidence of spiritual sincerity. People should not be treated as though the church’s ability to control their behavior determines the work of God within them. The church must faithfully proclaim the message while recognizing that spiritual transformation belongs to God. This recognition should produce humility. We are not the Holy Spirit. We cannot manufacture conviction. We cannot control the heart. We cannot substitute human pressure for the work of God. We can faithfully carry the gospel, pray for people, teach Scripture, encourage obedience, and serve with compassion. The responsibility is significant, but the responsibility does not make us owners of the spiritual lives of others. When Authority Becomes Personal Ownership The misuse of spiritual authority can become especially damaging when leaders begin to treat disagreement as disloyalty. A person who questions a decision may be accused of rebellion. Someone who expresses concern may be described as divisive. A person who asks for clarification may be treated as though they are challenging the authority of God. Such responses can create an environment where people are afraid to speak honestly. Leadership should never require the abandonment of personal conscience or the refusal to examine decisions. The church must recognize that spiritual leaders remain human beings who need accountability, wisdom, correction, and humility. The authority of a position does not make every decision correct. The presence of a title does not eliminate the responsibility to listen. The responsibility to lead does not remove the obligation to serve. Jesus addressed the nature of leadership in Matthew 20:25–28, contrasting the way worldly rulers exercise authority with the responsibility of His disciples to serve. He taught that greatness among His followers should be expressed through service and that the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve and give His life. The passage challenges leadership that seeks power for personal elevation. The church must remember that spiritual authority is intended to serve people rather than establish personal ownership over them. A leader who understands the teaching of Jesus should be willing to listen, acknowledge mistakes, and exercise authority with humility. People should not be made to feel that questioning a leader is equivalent to questioning God. The church belongs to Jesus. The people belong to God. The authority exercised within the church must remain consistent with the example and teaching of Christ. No Human Being Is the Final Judge of Another’s Mercy The church can recognize wrongdoing. The church can teach repentance. The church can exercise appropriate accountability. The church can address serious circumstances responsibly. The church can encourage people toward faithful living. However, the church must not confuse these responsibilities with the authority to determine the final limits of God’s mercy. The Bible contains accounts of people whose lives were transformed in ways that religious observers did not expect. Paul persecuted the church before becoming a servant of Christ. Peter denied Jesus and later received restoration. The Samaritan woman encountered Jesus and became a witness within her community. Matthew left the tax collector’s booth and followed Christ. Zacchaeus responded to the encounter with Jesus through repentance and restitution. The thief on the cross received a promise from Jesus in circumstances that could not be reduced to the expectations of religious observers. These accounts remind us that the work of God cannot be limited by our assumptions concerning the people He receives. We must remain careful when speaking about the spiritual condition of others. We may teach what Scripture reveals. We may address conduct. We may encourage repentance. We may offer guidance. We may recognize the need for accountability. We must never presume that our personal verdict possesses the authority of God. The church should remain humble before the One who knows the heart, understands every circumstance, and possesses authority over salvation. The Responsibility to Open What We Have Been Entrusted to Carry The Great Commission in Matthew 28:18–20 begins with the authority of Jesus. He declared that all authority in heaven and on earth had been given to Him. He then commissioned His disciples to go, make disciples, baptize, and teach people to observe His commandments. The order matters. The authority belongs to Jesus. The commission comes from Jesus. The responsibility of the disciples is to carry out what Jesus commanded. The church is not the source of the authority behind the commission. The church serves under the authority of Christ. This should shape the way we approach ministry. We should not ask how to preserve personal control over the people entrusted to our care. We should ask how to faithfully fulfill the commission. We should not ask how to determine which people are worthy of hearing the gospel. We should ask how to proclaim the gospel faithfully. We should not ask how to protect our preferences from unfamiliar circumstances. We should ask how to teach the commandments of Jesus while representing His character. We should not ask how to establish ourselves as the final authority over another person’s spiritual journey. We should ask how to help people become disciples of Christ. The church has a mission. The mission is not to own people. The mission is to proclaim Jesus. We Must Return to Our Assignment The church has been given a sacred responsibility. We are called to preach the gospel. We are called to teach Scripture. We are called to make disciples. We are called to baptize. We are called to care for people. We are called to serve. We are called to love our neighbors. We are called to remain faithful to Jesus. Those responsibilities require courage, wisdom, conviction, compassion, humility, and accountability. They also require us to recognize what was never entrusted to us. We were never given ownership of the invitation. We were never given authority to determine the limits of God’s mercy. We were never given permission to replace the Savior. We were never given permission to treat people as though their spiritual destiny belonged to our approval. The church must faithfully carry the gospel while remembering that the gospel belongs to Christ. The people who seek Him must be directed toward Him. The people who need correction must be taught responsibly. The people who need restoration must be approached with humility. The people who have been wounded must be treated with compassion. The people who have questions must be given opportunities to seek understanding. The people who desire to know Jesus must not be made to believe that the invitation belongs to someone else. We were entrusted with the message. We were entrusted with the mission. We were entrusted with the responsibility of representing Christ. We were never entrusted with ownership of the doorway. Jesus remains the Savior. Jesus remains the authority. Jesus remains the One who invites. Our responsibility is to faithfully carry His message and never presume that the invitation belongs to us. We were never the gatekeepers. We were called to be witnesses.
