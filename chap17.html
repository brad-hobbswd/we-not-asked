PART IV · RETURNING TO THE HEART OF JESUS

Forbid Them Not

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 17 of 17Reading edition

The words Jesus spoke in Mark 10:14 deserve to be heard again without rushing past the scene that gave them meaning. People were bringing children to Him, hoping He would touch them. The disciples responded by rebuking those who brought them. Jesus became indignant and said, “Let the little children come to Me, and do not forbid or hinder them” (Mark 10:13–16). He then received the children, took them into His arms, laid His hands upon them, and blessed them.

The account gives us more than a correction to remember. The scene shows what happened when the followers of Jesus placed their judgment between Him and people seeking His attention. The disciples attempted to stop the approach. Jesus interrupted their interruption. The children were not left waiting for the disciples to reconsider; Jesus called them near and welcomed them.

The original passage concerns children, and we must not turn the account into a claim that it directly settles every modern question of doctrine, church practice, or discipleship. The narrative nevertheless presents a principle that remains worthy of examination: the followers of Jesus must not hinder those whom He is willing to receive. The passage asks us to consider the difference between serving the mission of Christ and acting as though our own judgment determines who may approach Him.

The people who brought the children were seeking something from Jesus. Mark does not tell us that they came to challenge the disciples, interrupt a teaching session, or demand recognition. They brought the children so that Jesus might touch them. Their action was directed toward Him.

The disciples rebuked them. The text does not provide a detailed explanation of the disciples’ reasoning, and we should resist filling that silence with certainty. Perhaps they believed the moment was inappropriate, perhaps they were trying to preserve order, or perhaps they had misunderstood the importance of the request. Mark gives us enough to recognize the result without inventing a motive: the approach was being discouraged.

This matters because churches can become so occupied with evaluating the people who arrive that we forget to ask what brought them. A person may come seeking prayer, understanding, hope, Scripture, community, or simply a place to begin asking questions about God. Their circumstances may be complicated, and the church may have responsibilities that require wisdom. Yet our first response should not be to assume that the person is an interruption or a problem to be managed.

Listening does not require abandoning discernment. Listening requires allowing a person to be heard before conclusions are formed. A respectful conversation can reveal what someone is seeking, what they understand, what questions remain, and what kind of care may be appropriate. The church cannot minister well to people whose stories it refuses to hear.

Mark records Jesus’ response with unusual directness. He was indignant at what the disciples had done. His reaction makes clear that their interference was not a harmless matter of preference. He corrected them because their conduct opposed what He wanted in that moment.

The disciples may have believed their actions served Jesus, but the passage shows that their assumption was mistaken. A decision made in His name was not necessarily consistent with His will. The correction did not depend upon whether they had intended to cause harm. Their behavior had hindered the children, and Jesus addressed the behavior.

The church should take that distinction seriously. We often defend conduct by describing the intention behind it. Intention matters, but intention cannot be the only measure of faithfulness. A person may intend to protect, teach, preserve order, or uphold conviction and still respond in a way that needs correction. When the effect of our conduct becomes clear, we must be willing to examine whether the method served the purpose of Christ.

Correction is not an enemy of discipleship. The disciples themselves needed correction, and Jesus did not allow their position to exempt them from hearing it. A church that expects humility from those it teaches must also practice humility in its leadership, its relationships, and its decisions.

Jesus did not merely tell the disciples to stop rebuking the people. He actively welcomed the children. He called them to Himself, received them, held them, and blessed them. The action is important because the correction was not only about removing a barrier; the encounter continued through the welcome Jesus gave.

A congregation may learn to avoid openly discouraging people while still communicating distance through its ordinary behavior. A greeting may be withheld. A person may be discussed rather than engaged. Questions may be treated as evidence of a hidden motive. Someone may be allowed to attend while being made to feel that their presence is a burden. The absence of a formal rejection does not necessarily mean that people have been welcomed with care.

The example of Jesus calls for more than passive tolerance. His followers must be prepared to teach, listen, pray, serve, and make room for genuine encounters with the gospel. Welcome does not mean every question has already been resolved, nor does hospitality require the church to conceal its convictions. The welcome should be honest, and the teaching should be faithful. Neither should be used as an excuse for treating people without dignity.

Mark’s account continues with Jesus teaching that whoever does not receive the kingdom of God like a child will not enter it. The passage turns the disciples’ assumptions in another direction. Those who considered themselves responsible for deciding which approach was appropriate were reminded that receiving the kingdom requires humility.

The child in the scene is not presented as a symbol of perfection or as someone without need. The children are being brought to Jesus because they need His touch and blessing. Their dependence is part of the setting. The disciples, meanwhile, were acting as though their judgment should govern the encounter. Jesus’ teaching challenges the confidence that can cause a follower to forget dependence upon God.

This is a necessary correction for people who have served in church for many years. Knowledge, experience, responsibility, and leadership can be valuable gifts, but none removes our need for grace. We remain learners. We remain accountable. We remain people whose understanding may need to be corrected by the Word and by the Lord we claim to follow.

Humility also changes how we treat someone whose questions expose our uncertainty. We do not need to pretend that every answer is immediate. We can admit when we need to study, seek counsel, or return to Scripture. A thoughtful response is more faithful than an unexamined reaction delivered with certainty.

The passage should lead us to examine our response before we defend it. Do we listen before forming conclusions? Do we recognize when assumptions have shaped our treatment of someone? Are we willing to admit that a decision or reaction may need correction?

The answer is not demonstrated by the language we use about compassion. Faithfulness is seen through patience when a conversation is difficult, humility when an apology is needed, careful speech when truth must be communicated, and willingness to change when correction is warranted.

The people who come seeking Jesus are not assignments to complete according to our timetable. Discipleship involves teaching, repentance, obedience, and growth, yet spiritual formation is not accomplished through pressure or control. We can share truth, offer prayer, and provide counsel without demanding private disclosure or making a person's worth depend upon our approval.

Our responsibility is to be faithful in what we offer. We proclaim Christ, teach Scripture, demonstrate love, and serve. We do not control the response. The Savior is the One who calls, convicts, forgives, transforms, and leads.

The disciples were told not to hinder the children. The command was given to those who were close enough to Jesus to believe they understood what should happen. That detail should keep us from treating the passage as a criticism directed only toward people outside the church. The correction belongs to followers of Christ.

We may need to reconsider the way we have spoken about someone, a decision we defended, an assumption we repeated, or a practice we allowed to continue. We may need to listen to someone whose experience we dismissed, seek forgiveness, or make a change that others will notice. Repentance is not simply acknowledging that a passage is important. Repentance means allowing the Word to redirect our conduct.

We cannot undo every painful encounter or guarantee that every person will trust the church again. We can refuse to defend our mistakes as though our intentions made them right. We can become more careful with words, more responsible with authority, more willing to hear concerns, and more consistent in the dignity we extend to people.

Jesus’ instruction remains within its original account: allow the children to come, and do not hinder them. The church must read the passage honestly, teach it within its context, and allow the correction to examine our conduct.

When people seek Jesus, our responsibility is not to replace His authority with our own. We are to proclaim the gospel faithfully, teach His Word, serve with humility, and help people understand the call of discipleship. Where a legitimate responsibility requires a boundary, we should exercise that responsibility with fairness and care. Where personal preference has become an unnecessary obstacle, we should be willing to remove it.

The invitation belongs to Christ. Our work is to carry the message, not claim ownership of the people who hear it.

We were asked to follow Him, proclaim Him, teach His Word, make disciples, love our neighbors, and serve.

We were not asked to stand in His way.

Forbid them not.
