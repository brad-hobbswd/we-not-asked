CHAPTER SEVENTEEN Forbid Them Not There are moments in Scripture when a few words carry a responsibility that reaches far beyond the immediate circumstances in which they were spoken. Jesus’ instruction in Mark 10:14 (AMPC) is one of those moments. When people brought children to Him, the disciples attempted to prevent the encounter. Their response may have reflected assumptions about importance, access, priorities, or the appropriateness of interrupting the ministry of Jesus. Whatever influenced their actions, the outcome was the same: people who wanted to come to Jesus were being hindered by the very individuals walking beside Him. Jesus became indignant. He told His disciples to allow the children to come to Him and not forbid them. He received them, took them into His arms, laid His hands upon them, and blessed them. The disciples had misunderstood their responsibility. Their position beside Jesus did not give them permission to prevent people from approaching Him. Their actions required correction, and Jesus made certain the people they had attempted to hinder were received. The original encounter concerned children, and its meaning must remain grounded in that setting. The principle nevertheless calls every generation of believers to examine our assumptions when someone approaches whose circumstances differ from our own, our traditions when they become more influential than the purposes of God, and our authority when we begin acting as though the invitation belongs to us. The disciples were not strangers to Jesus. They had walked with Him, heard His teaching, witnessed His ministry, and been called to participate in the work He was doing. Their familiarity with Jesus did not prevent them from misunderstanding His heart. Spiritual familiarity does not guarantee spiritual maturity. Years of church attendance do not eliminate the possibility of misunderstanding Scripture. Ministry involvement does not make someone incapable of acting contrary to the purpose of Christ. Leadership does not remove the need for correction. The church needs the same willingness to receive correction when our conduct becomes an obstacle to the people we are called to serve. We cannot assume our intentions are sufficient evidence of faithfulness, nor assume our decisions accurately represent the heart of Jesus simply because we made them within a religious environment. We must remain willing to hear His words when they challenge the way we have been conducting ourselves. The people we have misunderstood Throughout this book, we have considered people judged according to reputations, circumstances, identities, histories, questions, or differences. We have considered people made uncomfortable within religious communities, people who experienced spiritual wounds, people who stopped attending, and people whose sincere desire to seek Jesus was complicated by the conduct of those who claimed to represent Him. The person whose life differs from ours is not merely a subject for theological discussion. The person who asks difficult questions is not merely an interruption to our preferred conversation. The person carrying a painful history is not merely an example of what we believe needs correcting. The person who has been rejected is not merely an opportunity to demonstrate our convictions. Every person who seeks to understand the gospel deserves to hear the message faithfully. Every person who needs correction deserves responsible teaching rather than unnecessary humiliation. Every person who desires to approach Jesus must be directed toward the Savior rather than made to believe that the invitation belongs to someone else. The church must learn to see people before seeing the assumptions we have attached to them. Jesus commissioned His disciples to make disciples, baptize, and teach people to observe His commandments. The mission was clear, but the mission was never an invitation to control every person who encountered the message. Faithfulness requires conviction. The church must remain faithful to biblical teaching and not become careless concerning doctrine, repentance, obedience, holiness, or spiritual responsibility. The gospel is not ours to rewrite according to cultural pressure, personal preference, or the desire to avoid difficult conversations. Faithfulness also requires humility. We must recognize the difference between teaching what Scripture says and demanding that every person conform to personal assumptions, between necessary boundaries and unnecessary barriers, and between correction intended to restore and conduct intended to humiliate. The church cannot faithfully proclaim the invitation of Jesus while behaving as though access to Jesus belongs to us. The responsibility of discipleship is serious, but the responsibility does not make us owners of the people we serve. The experiences we must acknowledge Some people may have been waiting for a church to recognize that their experiences mattered. Some may have needed someone to listen without immediately assigning motives. Others may have needed acknowledgment that spiritual authority can be misused, or the opportunity to ask questions without being treated as though questions represented rebellion. Some may have needed to hear that their humanity had not disappeared beneath the opinions of others. The church cannot assume every person has experienced these circumstances. We should not invent stories or assign wounds to people without knowing their experiences. We must, however, acknowledge that such experiences exist and that our conduct can contribute to them. The willingness to recognize that someone else’s experience may differ from our interpretation is an important beginning. Changing our conduct when Scripture reveals that we have been wrong is another. A church unwilling to examine itself cannot faithfully claim that every decision reflects the heart of Christ. We must remain willing to recognize that people we once misunderstood may have been trying to tell us something we needed to hear. Jesus received people whose presence challenged the expectations of religious observers. He confronted religious leaders who placed burdens upon others while failing to recognize their own responsibilities. His ministry repeatedly demonstrated that the opinions of those observing Him did not determine the purpose of His mission. Our discomfort cannot become the final authority over someone else’s opportunity to encounter Jesus. Our positions cannot become excuses for disregarding the humanity of those we serve. The church must faithfully carry the message without allowing our own limitations to become unnecessary obstacles. Truth and compassion must remain together The church must remain faithful to Scripture. We must teach the truth of God’s Word, recognize the seriousness of sin, and proclaim repentance, salvation, obedience, and the transforming work of Jesus Christ. The Bible also commands love, mercy, humility, patience, forgiveness, restoration, and compassion. We cannot claim to defend truth while misrepresenting truth through our conduct. We cannot claim to defend righteousness while refusing to examine our own failures. We cannot claim to defend the gospel while treating people in ways that contradict the message we proclaim. The church should be able to teach firmly, love sincerely, correct responsibly, and listen humbly. These responsibilities belong together. Truth does not require contempt. Conviction does not require humiliation. Correction does not require cruelty. Holiness does not require dehumanization. Faithfulness does not require pride. When we become the obstacle There are moments when the greatest obstacle in a particular encounter may not be the person seeking Jesus. The obstacle may be our refusal to acknowledge that we have misunderstood something. Defending decisions may feel easier than examining them. Explaining why someone else was wrong may feel easier than considering whether our own conduct contributed to the difficulty. Protecting an institution’s reputation may feel easier than acknowledging the experiences of people who have been hurt. The church cannot faithfully follow Jesus while refusing to receive correction from Jesus. When our traditions exceed the authority of Scripture, we must be willing to reconsider them. When our decisions fail to reflect compassion, we must be willing to change them. When our treatment of people contradicts the commandments of Christ, we must be willing to repent. Someone may be carrying a painful history and hoping to find a place where the love of Christ is genuine. The church must be ready to receive people without assuming their arrival will be uncomplicated. We must remember that the Savior we proclaim is the same Savior who received people whose presence challenged the expectations of religious observers. The church must not become so occupied with determining who is acceptable that we forget the people who are coming. We must not become so focused upon protecting our preferences that we overlook opportunities to serve, or so confident in our own understanding that we refuse to learn. The people still coming Somewhere, someone is beginning to ask questions about God. Someone is reading Scripture for the first time, considering attending church, praying after years of silence, wondering whether forgiveness is possible, carrying a painful history, trying to understand discipleship, or longing for hope. The church must be ready to receive people without assuming their arrival will be uncomplicated. We must be ready to teach, listen, pray, serve, answer questions, recognize that people need time, correct responsibly, acknowledge failures, and love people who do not resemble us. Our responsibility is to carry the invitation faithfully. We cannot force another person’s response, and we should not manipulate a spiritual journey to satisfy our expectations. We can faithfully proclaim the gospel, explain Scripture, demonstrate compassion, and remain available to serve. A final examination Have we allowed traditions to shape our judgments more strongly than Scripture? Have we recognized the dignity of people whose circumstances we did not understand? Have we considered whether our conduct has made the journey toward Jesus more difficult for someone? The church must continually return to the teachings and example of Jesus. We must remain willing to recognize when our conduct has departed from His heart, repent, and grow. The disciples were corrected when they attempted to prevent children from approaching Jesus. He confronted their misunderstanding and made certain the children were received. The church must learn to respond in the same manner when Christ reveals that our conduct has become an obstruction. Forbid them not Jesus’ words remain: “Let the little children come to Me, and do not forbid or hinder them” (Mark 10:14, AMPC). The invitation belongs to Christ. The people seeking Him are not ours to control. The message is not ours to manipulate. The authority is not ours to possess, and the mission is not ours to redefine. The church must remain a place where the gospel is proclaimed faithfully, Scripture is taught responsibly, people are treated with dignity, correction serves restoration, leaders remain accountable, questions can be addressed, and the love of Christ is demonstrated through the conduct of His people. The invitation is not ours to own. The Savior is not ours to control. The mercy of God is not ours to limit. Those who need grace must hear the gospel of grace. Those who need truth must receive faithful teaching. Those who need compassion must encounter compassion. We must faithfully carry what Christ entrusted. We were asked to follow Him, proclaim Him, teach His Word, make disciples, love our neighbors, serve, and carry the gospel. We were not asked to stand in His way. Forbid them not.
