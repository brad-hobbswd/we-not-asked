Chapter 17 Forbid Them Not There are moments in Scripture when a few words carry a responsibility that reaches far beyond the immediate circumstances in which they were spoken. Jesus’ instruction in Mark 10:14 is one of those moments. When people brought children to Him, the disciples attempted to prevent the encounter. Their response may have reflected assumptions about importance, access, priorities, or the appropriateness of interrupting the ministry of Jesus. Whatever influenced their actions, the outcome was the same. People who wanted to come to Jesus were being hindered by the very individuals who were walking beside Him. Jesus responded with indignation. He told His disciples to allow the children to come to Him and not forbid them. He received them, took them into His arms, laid His hands upon them, and blessed them. The instruction was unmistakable. The disciples had misunderstood their responsibility. Their position beside Jesus did not give them permission to prevent people from approaching Him. Their actions required correction, and Jesus made certain the people they had attempted to hinder were received. The moment reveals something important about the heart of Christ and the responsibility of those who claim to follow Him. People were coming to Jesus. The disciples were standing in their way. Jesus told the disciples to stop. The church must never lose the significance of that encounter. The Words That Should Examine Us “Forbid them not” is more than a statement concerning one particular group of children. The words reveal a principle concerning the responsibility of those who serve Christ. We must examine our conduct when people are seeking Him. We must examine our assumptions when someone approaches whose circumstances differ from our own. We must examine our attitudes when a person’s presence makes us uncomfortable. We must examine our traditions when they become more influential than the purposes of God. We must examine our authority when we begin acting as though the invitation belongs to us. The disciples were not strangers to Jesus. They had walked with Him. They had heard His teaching. They had witnessed His ministry. They had been called to follow Him and participate in the work He was doing. Their familiarity with Jesus did not prevent them from misunderstanding His heart. That truth should encourage humility among believers. Spiritual familiarity does not guarantee spiritual maturity. Years of church attendance do not eliminate the possibility of misunderstanding Scripture. Ministry involvement does not make someone incapable of acting contrary to the purpose of Christ. Leadership does not remove the need for correction. The disciples needed Jesus to confront their actions. The church needs the same willingness to receive correction when our conduct becomes an obstacle to the people we are called to serve. We cannot assume that our intentions are sufficient evidence of our faithfulness. We cannot assume that our traditions are beyond examination. We cannot assume that our decisions accurately represent the heart of Jesus simply because we made them within a religious environment. We must remain willing to hear the words of Christ when those words challenge the way we have been conducting ourselves. The People We Were Supposed to Receive Throughout this book, we have considered people who have been judged according to their reputations, circumstances, identities, histories, questions, or differences. We have considered people who have been made uncomfortable within religious communities. We have considered people who have experienced spiritual wounds. We have considered people who stopped attending. We have considered people whose sincere desire to seek Jesus was complicated by the conduct of those who claimed to represent Him. The circumstances differ. The responsibility remains. People are not abstractions. The person whose life differs from ours is not merely a subject for theological discussion. The person who asks difficult questions is not merely an interruption to our preferred conversation. The person carrying a painful history is not merely an example of what we believe needs correcting. The person who has been rejected is not merely an opportunity to demonstrate our convictions. Every person is a human being created by God. Every person deserves to be treated with dignity. Every person who seeks to understand the gospel deserves to hear the message faithfully. Every person who needs correction deserves responsible teaching rather than unnecessary humiliation. Every person who has been wounded deserves compassion. Every person who desires to approach Jesus must be directed toward the Savior rather than made to believe that the invitation belongs to someone else. The church must learn to see people before seeing the assumptions we have attached to them. The Church Must Not Confuse Its Mission Jesus commissioned His disciples to go and make disciples. He instructed them to baptize and teach people to observe His commandments. The mission was clear. The church was called to carry the gospel. The church was called to teach. The church was called to serve. The church was called to make disciples. The church was called to represent Christ. The mission was never an invitation to control every person who encountered the message. The church must remain faithful to biblical teaching. We must not become careless concerning doctrine, repentance, obedience, holiness, or spiritual responsibility. The gospel is not ours to rewrite according to cultural pressure, personal preference, or the desire to avoid difficult conversations. Faithfulness requires conviction. Faithfulness also requires humility. We must recognize the difference between teaching what Scripture says and demanding that every person conform to our personal assumptions. We must recognize the difference between necessary boundaries and unnecessary barriers. We must recognize the difference between correction intended to restore and conduct intended to humiliate. The church cannot faithfully proclaim the invitation of Jesus while behaving as though access to Jesus belongs to us. The responsibility of discipleship is serious, but the responsibility does not make us owners of the people we serve. We are witnesses. We are servants. We are stewards. We are disciples. We are people who continue to depend upon the mercy of God. The People We Have Misunderstood Some people may have been waiting for a church to recognize that their experiences mattered. Some may have been waiting for someone to listen without immediately assigning motives. Some may have been waiting for a conversation that did not begin with suspicion. Some may have been waiting for a sincere apology. Some may have been waiting for someone to acknowledge that spiritual authority can be misused. Some may have been waiting for the opportunity to ask questions without being treated as though asking questions represented rebellion. Some may have been waiting to hear that their humanity had not disappeared beneath the opinions of others. The church cannot assume that every person has experienced these circumstances. We should not invent stories or assign wounds to people without knowing their experiences. However, we must be willing to acknowledge that such experiences exist and that our conduct can contribute to them. The willingness to listen is an important beginning. The willingness to acknowledge wrongdoing is an important beginning. The willingness to examine our decisions is an important beginning. The willingness to recognize that someone else’s experience may differ from our interpretation is an important beginning. The willingness to change our conduct when Scripture reveals that we have been wrong is an important beginning. A church that is unwilling to examine itself cannot faithfully claim that every decision reflects the heart of Christ. We must be willing to learn. We must be willing to repent. We must be willing to make things right wherever possible. We must be willing to recognize that the people we once misunderstood may have been trying to tell us something we needed to hear. The Invitation Is Greater Than Our Discomfort Jesus received people whose presence challenged the expectations of religious observers. He spoke with the Samaritan woman. He called Matthew. He entered the home of Zacchaeus. He received the woman who anointed His feet. He touched the man suffering from leprosy. He sought the man who had been healed of blindness. He taught people who asked questions. He confronted religious leaders who placed burdens upon others while failing to recognize their own responsibilities. His ministry repeatedly demonstrated that the opinions of those observing Him did not determine the purpose of His mission. The church must learn from His example. Our discomfort cannot become the final authority over someone else’s opportunity to encounter Jesus. Our preferences cannot become universal commandments. Our assumptions cannot become the measure of another person’s worth. Our traditions cannot become more authoritative than the Word of God. Our positions cannot become excuses for disregarding the humanity of those we serve. The invitation remains greater than our discomfort. Jesus remains greater than our assumptions. The gospel remains greater than our personal preferences. The church must faithfully carry the message without allowing our own limitations to become unnecessary obstacles. Truth Must Remain Truth, and Love Must Remain Love Some may misunderstand this book as a call to abandon biblical convictions. That is not the purpose. The church must remain faithful to Scripture. We must teach the truth of God’s Word. We must recognize the seriousness of sin. We must proclaim repentance, salvation, obedience, and the transforming work of Jesus Christ. Those responsibilities remain. However, the church must also recognize that the Bible commands love, mercy, humility, patience, forgiveness, restoration, and compassion. We cannot selectively emphasize one responsibility while neglecting another. We cannot claim to defend holiness while disregarding the character of Christ. We cannot claim to defend truth while misrepresenting the truth through our conduct. We cannot claim to defend righteousness while refusing to examine our own failures. We cannot claim to defend the gospel while treating people in ways that contradict the message we proclaim. The church must allow the entirety of Scripture to govern its conduct. Truth does not require contempt. Conviction does not require humiliation. Correction does not require cruelty. Holiness does not require dehumanization. Faithfulness does not require pride. The church should be able to teach firmly, love sincerely, correct responsibly, and listen humbly. These responsibilities belong together. We Must Stop Standing in the Way There are moments when the church must recognize that the greatest obstacle in a particular encounter may not be the person seeking Jesus. The obstacle may be our assumptions. The obstacle may be our traditions. The obstacle may be our unwillingness to listen. The obstacle may be our fear of unfamiliar circumstances. The obstacle may be our misuse of authority. The obstacle may be our pride. The obstacle may be our refusal to acknowledge that we have misunderstood something. Such recognition requires courage. It is easier to defend our decisions than to examine them. It is easier to explain why someone else was wrong than to consider whether our own conduct contributed to the difficulty. It is easier to protect the reputation of an institution than to acknowledge the experiences of people who have been hurt. However, the church cannot faithfully follow Jesus while refusing to receive correction from Jesus. When our conduct becomes an obstacle, we must be willing to remove the obstacle. When our words wound unnecessarily, we must be willing to apologize. When our traditions exceed the authority of Scripture, we must be willing to reconsider them. When our decisions fail to reflect compassion, we must be willing to change them. When our treatment of people contradicts the commandments of Christ, we must be willing to repent. The responsibility is not to protect our pride. The responsibility is to faithfully represent Jesus. The People Still Coming Somewhere, someone is beginning to ask questions about God. Someone is reading Scripture for the first time. Someone is considering attending a church. Someone is praying after years of silence. Someone is wondering whether forgiveness is possible. Someone is carrying a painful history and hoping to find a place where the love of Christ is genuine. Someone is trying to understand what following Jesus means. Someone is longing for hope. Someone is coming. The church must be ready to receive people without assuming that their arrival will be uncomplicated. We must be ready to teach. We must be ready to listen. We must be ready to pray. We must be ready to serve. We must be ready to answer questions. We must be ready to recognize that people may need time to understand. We must be ready to correct responsibly. We must be ready to acknowledge our own failures. We must be ready to love people who do not resemble us. We must be ready to remember that the Savior we proclaim is the same Savior who received people whose presence challenged the expectations of religious observers. The church must not become so occupied with determining who is acceptable that we forget the people who are coming. We must not become so focused upon protecting our preferences that we overlook the opportunity to serve. We must not become so confident in our own understanding that we refuse to learn. The people are coming. Our responsibility is to faithfully carry the invitation. A Final Examination Before we close this book, the question must become personal. How have we responded to people who were different from us? Have we listened carefully? Have we made assumptions? Have we treated our preferences as though they were commandments? Have we allowed our traditions to shape our judgments more strongly than Scripture? Have we used spiritual authority responsibly? Have we corrected people with humility? Have we offered compassion when someone needed help? Have we recognized the dignity of people whose circumstances we did not understand? Have we made room for honest questions? Have we acknowledged our mistakes? Have we considered whether our conduct has made the journey toward Jesus more difficult for someone? These questions are not intended to produce shame without purpose. They are intended to produce examination. The church must continually return to the teachings and example of Jesus. We must remain willing to recognize when our conduct has departed from His heart. We must remain willing to repent and grow. The disciples were corrected when they attempted to prevent children from approaching Jesus. The correction was not the end of their discipleship. The correction was part of their discipleship. Jesus confronted their misunderstanding and made certain the children were received. The church must learn to respond in the same manner when Christ reveals that our conduct has become an obstruction. Forbid Them Not The words of Jesus remain. “Let the little children come to me, and do not forbid or hinder them.” The instruction reminds us that the invitation belongs to Christ. The people seeking Him are not ours to control. The message is not ours to manipulate. The authority is not ours to possess. The mission is not ours to redefine. We were called to follow Jesus. We were called to proclaim the gospel. We were called to make disciples. We were called to teach His Word. We were called to love our neighbors. We were called to serve. We were called to demonstrate compassion. We were called to remain faithful. We were never called to stand between the Savior and the people seeking Him. The church must remain a place where the gospel is proclaimed faithfully, where Scripture is taught responsibly, where people are treated with dignity, where correction serves restoration, where leaders remain accountable, where questions can be addressed, and where the love of Christ is demonstrated through the conduct of His people. The invitation is not ours to own. The Savior is not ours to control. The mercy of God is not ours to limit. The people who seek Jesus must be directed toward Jesus. The people who need grace must hear the gospel of grace. The people who need truth must receive faithful teaching. The people who need compassion must encounter compassion. The people who need restoration must be treated with humility and care. The church must faithfully carry what Christ entrusted to us. We were asked to follow Him. We were asked to proclaim Him. We were asked to teach His Word. We were asked to make disciples. We were asked to love our neighbors. We were asked to serve. We were asked to carry the gospel. We were not asked to stand in His way. Forbid them not. Amen.
