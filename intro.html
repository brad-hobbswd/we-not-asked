INTRODUCTION We Were Not Asked There are moments in Scripture when the words of Jesus leave no room for misunderstanding, yet the people standing closest to Him fail to recognize what He is revealing. The account of children being brought to Jesus in Mark 10:13–16 is one of those moments. Parents and others brought their children, hoping Jesus would touch and bless them. The disciples responded by rebuking those who brought them, apparently believing that the children should not interrupt the Master’s time or occupy a place among the matters demanding His attention. Their response may have seemed reasonable to them, perhaps even protective, but Jesus saw something in their actions that required immediate correction. He was indignant. The reaction of Jesus deserves careful attention. He did not quietly allow the disciples to continue making decisions about who could approach Him. He called the children to Himself, instructed the disciples to stop hindering them, and declared that the kingdom of God belongs to those who receive such things as children. The disciples had placed themselves between people seeking Jesus and Jesus Himself. Their proximity to Him had not given them authority to control His invitation, and their understanding of ministry had not aligned with His heart. Jesus said, “Let the little children come to Me, and do not forbid or hinder them” (Mark 10:14, AMPC). Those words were spoken within a particular moment, concerning children who were being prevented from approaching Christ. The rebuke, however, reveals something that every generation of believers must examine: the danger of becoming an obstacle to those whom the Lord is calling. The church has been entrusted with the message of reconciliation, the proclamation of the gospel, and the commission to make disciples of all nations. We have been called to teach, serve, love, proclaim truth, and bear witness to the saving power of Jesus Christ. None of those responsibilities gives us permission to appoint ourselves as the final authority over who may approach Him. The invitation belongs to Christ. The mission belongs to Christ. The people who respond belong before Christ, where their lives, hearts, questions, convictions, wounds, and needs can be brought into His presence. Somewhere along the way, however, people can become so accustomed to the structures surrounding their faith that they begin confusing those structures with the faith itself. Traditions may be inherited without careful examination. Interpretations may be repeated without returning to the context of Scripture. Positions held by respected leaders may become unquestionable, while the words and actions of Jesus receive less attention than the opinions formed around them. Familiarity can create a sense of certainty that has never been tested through honest biblical examination. Authority carries responsibility, but responsibility can become distorted when the person exercising authority forgets the purpose for which such responsibility was entrusted. A shepherd is called to care for the flock, not to claim ownership over the Shepherd’s invitation. A teacher is called to help people understand the Word, not to replace the Word with personal preference. A family may pass along convictions with sincere intentions, yet sincerity alone cannot establish whether every inherited belief accurately reflects the heart and mission of Christ. Every generation must remain willing to examine what has been received, comparing belief, practice, and interpretation with the Scriptures. The question is not whether tradition matters, whether leadership deserves respect, or whether biblical truth should be taken seriously. Those questions deserve thoughtful consideration. The deeper question concerns what happens when authority and tradition begin functioning beyond the responsibilities Christ entrusted to His followers. What happens when the desire to protect a familiar understanding of faith becomes stronger than the willingness to examine whether our conduct resembles Jesus? What happens when people become more concerned with determining who should be welcomed than with faithfully carrying the message that Jesus saves? Those questions become especially important when we consider the people who have been made to feel that approaching Jesus requires the approval of someone else. A person’s history may become the subject of conversation before anyone has listened to the person’s heart. Past failures may be treated as permanent qualifications for rejection. Present struggles may become reasons for distance rather than opportunities for compassion and ministry. Sexual orientation may become the defining subject of a person’s treatment, overshadowing the humanity, dignity, spiritual longing, and need for Christ shared by every soul. Religious judgment can leave people believing that the church has already decided their place before they have had the opportunity to encounter the Savior. Such treatment deserves examination, not because Scripture should be set aside to make people comfortable, but because Scripture must never be used carelessly to justify attitudes and actions that have not been examined in the light of Christ. The call to holiness cannot become permission for cruelty. Conviction cannot excuse contempt. Biblical instruction cannot be separated from the responsibility to love our neighbors. The gospel cannot be faithfully represented through conduct that drives people away from the very Savior we claim to proclaim. Every person stands in need of salvation. Every person has sinned. Every person requires the mercy of God. No one approaches Christ with a résumé of personal righteousness sufficient to establish superiority over another. The ground beneath the cross does not elevate one sinner above another. We come needing grace, and the grace that reached us was never ours to ration according to our comfort, familiarity, or approval. Galatians 3:28 reminds believers that distinctions which divide humanity do not establish superiority in Christ. The passage speaks directly to the unity of those who belong to Him, revealing the significance of a shared identity in Christ that transcends the divisions through which human beings have historically measured one another. The message of the gospel calls us away from boasting in distinctions and toward recognizing our common dependence upon the Lord. Such truth should make us cautious whenever we begin treating another person as though our own standing before God gives us permission to obstruct their approach to Him. The Great Commission also requires us to reconsider the posture with which we encounter people. Jesus instructed His followers to go into all the world, make disciples, baptize, and teach. The commission was not given as permission to narrow the reach of the gospel according to personal preference. The message was to be carried beyond familiar boundaries, toward people whose lives, cultures, circumstances, and histories would not always resemble those of the messengers. The church cannot faithfully carry the commission while becoming preoccupied with preserving distance from the very people whom the commission calls us to reach. This book is not written to excuse sin, dismiss Scripture, diminish holiness, or replace biblical examination with personal sentiment. Nor is the purpose to condemn every person serving in authority or every family that has faithfully preserved its convictions. The purpose is to bring our assumptions, attitudes, interpretations, and conduct before the words of Jesus and allow His teaching to examine us. A sincere believer can misunderstand a passage. A respected leader can make a decision that causes harm. A family can pass down a belief without recognizing the weight such a belief carries for another person’s life. A congregation can become so accustomed to a particular way of thinking that the possibility of having misunderstood the heart of Christ becomes difficult to consider. The willingness to examine ourselves is not a threat to genuine faith. Such willingness is part of faithfulness. We must be willing to return to Scripture, read carefully, consider context, recognize the difference between what the Word declares and what we have added through interpretation, and remain humble enough to receive correction. The disciples themselves were corrected by Jesus. Their correction was not recorded to demonstrate that they were beyond hope, but to reveal that even those walking closely with Christ needed their understanding and conduct brought into alignment with His heart. The most uncomfortable possibility may be that some people have been hindered from approaching Jesus by those who sincerely believe they are protecting Him. The disciples were not strangers to Christ. They had walked with Him, listened to Him, witnessed His ministry, and received instruction concerning the kingdom. Yet, in this moment, their actions contradicted the welcome Jesus was extending. Their failure reminds us that spiritual familiarity does not guarantee spiritual understanding, and religious responsibility does not make a person incapable of obstructing the work of God. We must therefore ask ourselves questions that cannot be answered through the examination of someone else’s conduct alone. Have our words discouraged someone who was seeking God? Have our attitudes communicated rejection before we offered understanding? Have we made personal discomfort appear to be biblical authority? Have we inherited positions without carefully studying the Scriptures upon which those positions rest? Have we become so certain of our own judgments that the possibility of Christ correcting us has been overlooked? These questions are not intended to produce shame without hope. They are invitations to honest repentance and renewed faithfulness. The same Jesus who corrected His disciples continued teaching them. The same Lord who rebuked obstruction called people into His presence. His correction reveals His concern for those who were being hindered and His expectation that those following Him would learn to reflect His heart more faithfully. The words “We Were Not Asked” are not a declaration that biblical truth is unimportant or that Christian responsibility has disappeared. They are a reminder that authority has boundaries, that the commission has a purpose, and that the invitation of Christ does not belong to us. We were not appointed to decide whom Jesus may receive. We were commissioned to make Him known. The pages that follow will examine the assumptions that can develop around spiritual authority, the traditions that may go unexamined, the people who have been judged or rejected, the wounds created through religious interference, and the responsibility of believers to return to the heart of Christ. The examination will not remain directed toward leaders, institutions, families, or groups of people. Each reader must also consider the ways personal attitudes and actions may have contributed to the very obstruction being challenged. The question at the center of this book is simple, but the implications reach deeply into our understanding of ministry, discipleship, compassion, responsibility, and the gospel itself: What happens when people who were never given authority over the invitation begin controlling access to the One who extended it? Jesus answered the disciples when they attempted to hinder those coming to Him. His words remain before us, calling every generation to reconsider whom we discourage, whom we reject, whom we judge, and whom we may unknowingly prevent from approaching the Savior. We were asked to follow Him. We were asked to proclaim Him. We were asked to love our neighbors. We were asked to carry the gospel. We were not asked to stand in His way.
