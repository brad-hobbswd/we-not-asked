Introduction

We Were Not Asked · Bradley Hobbs

The scene in Mark 10 is easy to picture. Parents and other people are bringing children to Jesus, hoping He will bless them. The disciples step in and rebuke the people. Jesus is displeased, calls the children near, and tells His followers not to hinder them. A small moment becomes a revealing one: the people closest to Jesus have misunderstood what their place is.

Mark does not explain why the disciples reacted as they did. We do not need to invent their motives to learn from what happened. They tried to stop an approach Jesus welcomed, and He corrected them. Their mistake raises a question that reaches beyond that day. Can sincere followers become an obstacle while believing they are helping?

Churches have real responsibilities. We teach Scripture, make disciples, care for people, address harmful conduct, and help believers grow in holiness. Those responsibilities matter. The trouble begins when we treat our responsibility as though we own the invitation Jesus extends. We can share the gospel; we cannot take Christ’s place.

This book is not an argument for abandoning biblical conviction or pretending every decision is simple. Some situations require boundaries, careful discernment, and accountability. Those matters deserve more than quick conclusions. The question throughout these pages is whether we are responding from Scripture and love, or whether preference, fear, reputation, or assumptions have quietly become our authority.

People sometimes arrive at church carrying a story they have never told. Others have tried to explain and felt dismissed. We cannot know a person's whole life from a first conversation, a rumor, a label, or a visible struggle. Listening will not answer every theological question, yet listening can help us stop treating people as conclusions before we know them.

Rather than repeat one point in different language, each chapter considers a different part of the responsibility entrusted to us: authority, history, assumptions, judgment, restoration, religious wounds, witness, interrupted encounters, absence, truth, and stewardship. The movement is toward honest self-examination, not toward condemning every church or leader.

I hope these pages sound less like someone standing above the reader and more like a conversation we need to have together. I have questions to examine, too. None of us is beyond correction, and familiarity with Scripture does not guarantee that every response reflects the heart of Jesus.

The title is a reminder of a boundary. We were asked to follow Christ, proclaim His gospel, teach His Word, and serve people. We were not asked to control whom He receives. The invitation belongs to Him. Our task is to carry it faithfully.
