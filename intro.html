INTRODUCTION We Were Not Asked The account of children being brought to Jesus in Mark 10:13–16 presents a moment in which those closest to Him misunderstood what their responsibility required. People brought children so Jesus might touch and bless them. The disciples rebuked those bringing the children, apparently believing that the approach should be discouraged. Jesus responded with indignation, called the children to Himself, and instructed His followers not to hinder them. His words were direct: “Let the little children come to Me, and do not forbid or hinder them” (Mark 10:14, AMPC). The original encounter concerned children, and the passage must be read within that setting. Yet the correction also reveals a principle worthy of careful examination: those who follow Jesus must not allow their own assumptions to obstruct people whom He is receiving. The disciples were not strangers to Christ. They had walked with Him, heard His teaching, and witnessed His ministry. Their closeness, however, did not prevent them from acting contrary to His desire. They assumed a responsibility that Jesus had not given them, and their decision required correction. The church has been entrusted with proclaiming the gospel, making disciples, teaching Scripture, serving others, and bearing witness to the saving power of Jesus Christ. Those responsibilities are serious. They do not make us owners of His invitation or authorize us to place personal approval between another person and the Savior. We are messengers of the gospel, not the source of grace; servants of Christ, not substitutes for His authority. Religious familiarity can make assumptions difficult to recognize. Traditions may be inherited without careful examination, interpretations repeated without returning to their context, and the opinions of respected leaders treated as beyond question. Over time, the distinction between what Scripture teaches and what we have added through custom or preference can become blurred. Faithfulness requires us to examine what we believe and how we behave in the light of the Word. This examination matters when people are made to feel that approaching Jesus requires someone else’s approval. A person’s history may be discussed before anyone has listened. Past failures may be treated as permanent grounds for rejection. Present struggles may lead to distance rather than compassionate ministry. Sexual orientation may become the defining subject of a person’s treatment, overshadowing the dignity, spiritual longing, and need for Christ shared by every human being. Such experiences deserve honest consideration, not so Scripture can be set aside, but so Scripture is not used carelessly to defend conduct that fails to reflect Jesus. The call to holiness cannot excuse cruelty, and conviction cannot justify contempt. Biblical instruction belongs alongside the commands to love our neighbors, show mercy, act humbly, and pursue restoration. Truth must be taught faithfully, while the manner in which we carry truth must also be examined. The gospel is not represented well when our conduct contradicts the Savior we proclaim. Every person stands in need of God’s mercy. None approaches Christ with a record of personal righteousness sufficient to establish superiority over another. We come needing grace, and the grace that reached us was never ours to ration according to comfort, familiarity, or approval. Galatians 3:28 speaks of the unity of those who belong to Christ and challenges distinctions used to establish superiority among believers. The passage does not erase every distinction or settle every question of practice; rather, it directs attention to our shared standing in Christ and our dependence upon Him. Such truth should make us cautious about treating another person as though our own standing gives us permission to obstruct their approach to the Lord. The Great Commission calls the church beyond familiar boundaries. Jesus commanded His followers to make disciples of all nations, baptize, and teach people to observe His commandments. The commission includes teaching and discipleship, yet begins with carrying the gospel. We cannot faithfully fulfill that mission while becoming preoccupied with keeping distance from people whom we are called to reach. This book is not written to excuse sin, dismiss Scripture, diminish holiness, or replace biblical examination with sentiment. Nor is the purpose to condemn every leader or family that has sincerely preserved its convictions. The purpose is to bring our assumptions, attitudes, interpretations, and conduct before the teaching of Jesus. Believers can misunderstand Scripture; respected leaders can make harmful decisions; families can pass along convictions without recognizing their effect; and congregations can become so accustomed to a particular interpretation that reconsideration feels threatening. Self-examination is not a threat to genuine faith. A willingness to return to Scripture, consider context, distinguish the Word from our conclusions, and receive correction is part of faithfulness. The disciples themselves were corrected by Jesus. Their correction did not end their discipleship; rather, the encounter exposed a misunderstanding that needed to change. The uncomfortable possibility is that people may have been hindered from approaching Jesus by those who sincerely believed they were protecting the faith. The disciples’ failure reminds us that spiritual familiarity does not guarantee understanding, and religious responsibility does not make anyone incapable of obstructing the work of God. The questions that follow must therefore become personal. Have our words discouraged someone seeking God? Have our attitudes communicated rejection before we offered understanding? Have we mistaken discomfort for biblical authority? Have we inherited positions without carefully examining the Scriptures behind them? Have we become so confident in our judgment that we no longer expect Christ to correct us? These questions are not meant to produce shame without hope. They call us toward repentance, renewed faithfulness, and a more honest reflection of Christ. The same Jesus who corrected His disciples continued teaching them. His correction revealed concern for those being hindered and an expectation that His followers would learn to represent Him more faithfully. The title, We Were Not Asked, is not a declaration that biblical truth is unimportant or Christian responsibility has disappeared. It reminds us that authority has boundaries and that the invitation belongs to Christ. We were not appointed to decide whom Jesus may receive. We were commissioned to make Him known. The chapters ahead examine spiritual authority, inherited assumptions, the people judged or rejected, the wounds created through religious interference, and the responsibility of believers to return to the heart of Christ. The examination must not remain directed toward leaders, institutions, families, or groups of people. Every reader must consider how personal attitudes and actions may have contributed to the obstruction being challenged. The question at the center of this book is both simple and searching: What happens when people who were never given authority over the invitation begin controlling access to the One who extended it? Jesus corrected the disciples when they hindered those coming to Him. His words continue to call us to reconsider whom we discourage, whom we judge, and whether our conduct makes the journey toward the Savior unnecessarily difficult. We were asked to follow Him, proclaim Him, love our neighbors, and carry the gospel. We were not asked to stand in His way.
