INTRODUCTION We Were Not Asked The account of children being brought to Jesus in Mark 10:13–16 presents a moment when those closest to Him misunderstood their responsibility. People brought children so Jesus might touch and bless them. The disciples rebuked those bringing the children, apparently believing the approach should be discouraged. Jesus responded with indignation, called the children to Himself, and instructed His followers not to hinder them. His words were direct: “Let the little children come to Me, and do not forbid or hinder them” (Mark 10:14, AMPC). The original encounter concerned children, and the passage must be read within that setting. The correction nevertheless raises a question for every generation of believers: can people who sincerely follow Jesus misunderstand their responsibility and become an obstacle to someone seeking Him? The disciples were not strangers to Christ. They had walked with Him, heard His teaching, and witnessed His ministry. Their closeness did not prevent them from acting contrary to His desire. They assumed a responsibility that Jesus had not given them, and their decision required correction. Their example reminds us that religious familiarity, years of service, and sincere intentions do not make our judgments infallible. The church has been entrusted with proclaiming the gospel, making disciples, teaching Scripture, serving others, and bearing witness to the saving power of Jesus Christ. Those responsibilities are serious and cannot be separated from repentance, obedience, holiness, and spiritual growth. Yet responsibility does not make us owners of Christ’s invitation. We are messengers of the gospel, not the source of grace; servants of Christ, not substitutes for His authority. This book examines what can happen when assumptions, traditions, personal preferences, or interpretations begin to function as though they carry the authority of Scripture. A person's history may be discussed before anyone has listened. Past failures may be treated as permanent grounds for rejection. Questions may be mistaken for rebellion. Differences may become reasons for distance rather than opportunities for thoughtful ministry. Sexual orientation may become the defining subject of a person's treatment, overshadowing the dignity, spiritual longing, and need for Christ shared by every human being. Such experiences deserve honest consideration. The purpose is not to set Scripture aside, excuse sin, diminish holiness, or condemn every leader and family that has sincerely preserved convictions. The purpose is to examine whether our beliefs and conduct faithfully reflect the Word we claim to uphold. The call to holiness cannot excuse cruelty, and conviction cannot justify contempt. Biblical instruction belongs alongside the commands to love our neighbors, show mercy, act humbly, and pursue restoration. The distinction between the invitation and discipleship must remain clear. Jesus calls people to Himself, and He calls those who follow Him into repentance, obedience, and transformation. The church must proclaim that message without weakening its meaning. At the same time, believers must not confuse the demands of following Christ with additional obstacles created through pride, fear, preference, or assumptions. We must teach faithfully and examine ourselves honestly. Listening is part of that examination. Some people carry experiences they have never shared publicly. Others have attempted to explain what happened and felt unheard. We cannot presume to know every story, nor should we make someone's private life the subject of unnecessary scrutiny. Listening does not settle every theological question. Listening helps us understand whether our conduct has contributed to pain, confusion, or unnecessary distance, and gives correction a more honest foundation. The chapters move from the authority we assumed, toward the people affected by our decisions, through the consequences of interference, and finally toward the responsibility of returning to the heart of Jesus. Each chapter considers a different aspect of the question. The movement is not intended to establish that every concern is identical or every response requires the same solution. Scripture remains the standard, and our conclusions must be examined rather than merely repeated. The questions that follow are personal. Have our words discouraged someone seeking God? Have our attitudes communicated rejection before we offered understanding? Have we mistaken discomfort for biblical authority? Have we inherited positions without carefully examining the Scriptures behind them? Have we become so confident in our judgment that we no longer expect Christ to correct us? These questions are not meant to produce shame without hope. They call us toward repentance, renewed faithfulness, and a more honest reflection of Christ. The same Jesus who corrected His disciples continued teaching them. His correction revealed concern for those being hindered and an expectation that His followers would learn to represent Him more faithfully. The title, We Were Not Asked, is a reminder that authority has boundaries and that the invitation belongs to Christ. We were not appointed to decide whom Jesus may receive. We were commissioned to make Him known. The central question is simple, yet searching: what happens when people who were never given authority over the invitation begin controlling access to the One who extended it? The pages that follow invite us to examine that question honestly, under the authority of Scripture and the example of Jesus.
