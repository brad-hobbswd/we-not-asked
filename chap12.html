CHAPTER TWELVE What Happens When the Invitation Is Interrupted? An invitation carries the possibility of a life changing encounter. A person who has been searching for hope may finally hear a message that reaches the deepest part of the heart. Someone burdened by shame may begin to believe mercy remains available. A person who has spent years feeling unseen may discover that God has not forgotten them. Such moments are precious, and the responsibility of those carrying the gospel should never be taken lightly. An interruption does not always appear as a formal prohibition. A question may be dismissed, a person treated as inconvenient, a circumstance judged before it is understood, or a sincere search met with suspicion. The invitation remains present in Scripture, yet the experience surrounding the invitation may become difficult enough that someone begins to wonder whether the message is meant for them. We cannot know the outcome of every encounter that becomes difficult. A person may continue seeking God elsewhere, return later, or choose not to pursue the conversation. The person who caused the interruption may never know what the moment meant to someone else. The difference between what seemed like a brief interaction to one person and what became a defining experience for another should make us careful. The responsibility of the church extends beyond words spoken from a pulpit. The gospel is carried through conversations, relationships, hospitality, patience, teaching, and the manner in which believers respond to those who are seeking. A message about the love of Jesus can be spoken accurately while conduct surrounding the message communicates something contradictory. The danger lies in allowing personal discomfort to become spiritual authority. Philip was willing to serve the encounter Acts 8:26–39 (AMPC) presents a remarkable account of a man traveling from Jerusalem who was reading the prophet Isaiah. The Ethiopian eunuch possessed influence and responsibility, yet the passage he was reading remained difficult to understand. He was searching for meaning, and his questions were sincere. Philip was directed toward the man's journey. When he approached, he did not assume the man was incapable of understanding Scripture. He asked whether the man understood what he was reading, and the man invited Philip to explain. The encounter developed through the Word. Philip began with the passage the man was reading and proclaimed Jesus. The conversation did not require the seeker to arrive with every answer already settled. His questions became the starting point for instruction, and the instruction led toward understanding. The eunuch eventually asked about baptism. Philip responded to the request, and the man was baptized. Acts 8 provides a picture of someone seeking understanding and a believer willing to meet him along the way. Philip did not control the man's journey or abandon the responsibility of teaching. He was present, attentive, and faithful to the direction given. People rarely arrive with identical backgrounds, knowledge, experiences, or questions. Some have spent years studying Scripture. Others know only a few passages. Some carry painful experiences with religion. Others have never attended a church. Some understand the language of faith, while others struggle to know how to ask what they need to ask. A sincere question should not become a reason to humiliate someone. A search for understanding should not be met with the assumption that the person is dangerous, dishonest, or spiritually inferior. Confusion is not the same as rebellion. A person asking for clarification may be standing at the beginning of understanding rather than demonstrating an unwillingness to learn. Philip did not have to possess every answer to serve the moment faithfully. He needed to be willing to explain what he understood and direct the conversation toward Jesus. His role was neither to take ownership of the man's conscience nor to abandon the responsibility of teaching. The Cost of an Unnecessary Barrier Luke 19:1–10 (AMPC) tells of Zacchaeus, a chief tax collector and wealthy man whose profession carried a reputation that made him despised among the people. When Jesus entered Jericho, Zacchaeus wanted to see Him. His position and stature created a practical difficulty, so he climbed a tree to see Jesus as He passed. Jesus stopped, looked up, and called Zacchaeus by name. He told him to come down because He intended to stay at his house. The decision was personal and deliberate. Jesus did not merely acknowledge Zacchaeus from a distance. He entered the man's home and created an opportunity for an encounter that would have lasting consequences. The crowd responded with murmuring. Their objection was directed toward Jesus' decision to enter the home of someone they regarded as a sinner. The crowd saw Zacchaeus through his reputation. Jesus initiated an encounter with the person standing before Him. The encounter produced a response in Zacchaeus. He declared his intention to give to the poor and make restitution for those he had wronged. Jesus announced that salvation had come to the house and affirmed that the Son of Man had come to seek and save the lost. The crowd's murmuring did not prevent Jesus from entering the house, yet the account reveals how quickly people can criticize the very action through which mercy reaches someone. Religious observers sometimes become so focused on the perceived unworthiness of another person that they struggle to recognize the significance of Christ drawing near. The gospel does not ask believers to pretend wrongdoing has never occurred. Zacchaeus demonstrated repentance through a willingness to address the harm he had caused. The encounter involved transformation and responsibility, yet the process began with Jesus extending a personal invitation to relationship. That distinction matters when considering people who arrive carrying histories, questions, reputations, or circumstances that have already been judged by others. A church may become so preoccupied with deciding whether someone is acceptable that the congregation never creates an environment where honest spiritual conversation can occur. The people seeking Jesus should not have to convince every observer that they deserve the opportunity to seek Him. A reputation may explain why people hold an opinion. A reputation does not give the crowd authority to determine whether Jesus may engage a person. Believers should be careful not to confuse discernment with a verdict pronounced before the person has been heard. When the Message Is Heard Through the Conduct of the Messenger Paul wrote to the Corinthians about the responsibility of believers to live as representatives of Christ. In 2 Corinthians 5:20 (AMPC), he described them as ambassadors for Christ, carrying a message of reconciliation. An ambassador does not become the owner of the message. The responsibility belongs to the one who commissioned the ambassador. Faithfulness requires the representative to communicate accurately while remembering the authority from which the message comes. Personal preferences cannot replace the commission, and conduct should not contradict the character of the One represented. The church carries the message of reconciliation. Believers are entrusted with proclaiming the death and resurrection of Jesus, explaining the gospel, teaching His commandments, and encouraging people toward a life of discipleship. The manner of that representation matters. Someone who encounters a believer may not immediately separate the believer's conduct from the believer's claims about Jesus. Patience may help a person recognize patience as a characteristic of the faith being presented. Compassion may help them recognize the mercy described in Scripture. Contempt may make the message difficult to reconcile with the treatment received. The conduct of every believer does not perfectly define Christ. Jesus remains the standard by which the church must examine itself. The failures of His followers do not erase His character or change His truth. Nevertheless, the church cannot dismiss the consequences of its own conduct simply by telling wounded people to look beyond those who hurt them. A person entrusted with speaking on behalf of Christ should be willing to consider how words, tone, timing, and conduct affect the encounter. Truth may require a difficult conversation. Faithfulness does not require cruelty, impatience, humiliation, or a display of power. The messenger may need to revisit a conversation, acknowledge an error, apologize, and seek to repair harm. Such action does not weaken the message. Humility demonstrates that the messenger remains accountable to Christ. When believers interrupt a sincere search through unnecessary humiliation, suspicion, or exclusion, the interruption may affect the way the message is heard. The person may remember the correction more clearly than the Scripture offered afterward. They may remember being treated as a problem rather than being taught. We must therefore examine whether our conduct helps people understand the gospel or obscures the message we intended to carry. When we make the journey harder than Jesus made it There is a difference between teaching someone the cost of discipleship and creating additional burdens through personal preferences. Jesus spoke clearly about following Him. Discipleship involves surrender, obedience, sacrifice, humility, and a willingness to carry the cross. The church has a responsibility to teach those truths without weakening the message. However, the difficulty of discipleship should never become an excuse for adding human obstacles that Scripture does not require. Jesus addressed this concern in Matthew 23:1–4 (AMPC), confronting religious leaders who placed heavy burdens upon others while failing to embody the mercy, justice, and faithfulness God required. His criticism exposed a religious system in which burdens were imposed without the character and responsibility necessary to serve people properly. The church must examine the burdens placed upon those who seek fellowship and understanding. Are people being taught Scripture, or required to satisfy personal expectations before anyone will listen? Are boundaries established to protect people and support responsible ministry, or used to protect the comfort of those who hold authority? Are questions answered through patient teaching, or silenced through intimidation? Are people directed toward Christ, or made dependent upon approval from individuals who have placed themselves between the seeker and the Savior? The answers require humility. Some boundaries are necessary. Some situations require accountability. Some people need careful pastoral guidance. Biblical teaching must remain faithful, and the church must not confuse compassion with the abandonment of responsibility. Nevertheless, unnecessary burdens can obscure the purpose of discipleship. A person should be able to seek understanding without being treated as though every question represents an attack. A person should be able to learn without being humiliated for the knowledge they have not yet acquired. Teaching requires patience, explanation, relationship, and a willingness to recognize that growth takes time. Removing what does not belong in the way The church cannot remove every difficulty a person will encounter while following Christ. The path of discipleship contains challenges that require faith, courage, perseverance, and obedience. Believers must teach those realities honestly. The church can, however, examine the obstacles created through its own conduct. We can reconsider the way we speak to people whose backgrounds are unfamiliar. We can examine whether traditions have become more important than the principles they were intended to serve. We can recognize the difference between biblical conviction and personal preference. We can refuse to treat a person's questions as an inconvenience. We can create opportunities for honest conversation, responsible teaching, and spiritual growth. We can also recognize that people may carry wounds we do not immediately see. A person who appears distant may have learned to protect themselves from rejection. Someone who seems angry may be struggling to express pain. Someone who hesitates to trust spiritual leaders may have experienced authority used in ways that damaged confidence. Careful listening may reveal what judgment would have missed. Removing an unnecessary barrier does not mean removing the cross from the message. We must stop confusing the burden Christ calls people to carry with burdens we place upon them through pride, fear, impatience, or unexamined preference. The distinction deserves careful consideration, especially when the people affected have little power to challenge the person making the decision. The invitation should remain clear enough that people can recognize the voice of Christ through the message being presented. The encounter we must not interrupt Every believer should consider the possibility that someone may be standing near the beginning of a spiritual journey while we are standing near the beginning of an assumption. The person seeking Jesus may not look familiar. Their questions may not sound like our questions. Their history may differ from ours. Their understanding may be incomplete. Their life may contain circumstances that require careful teaching, responsible accountability, and time. None of those realities gives us permission to treat the person as though the invitation belongs to us. The Ethiopian eunuch needed someone willing to explain Scripture. Zacchaeus needed an encounter with Jesus that reached beyond the crowd's opinion. The people described throughout the Gospels needed someone willing to recognize that the presence of Christ was not reserved for those whom religious observers considered respectable. The church must remain willing to serve the encounter rather than interrupt it. We should teach faithfully, love sincerely, correct responsibly, listen patiently, examine our conduct honestly, and recognize the dignity of people who arrive carrying questions and experiences we may never fully understand. The gospel belongs to Jesus. The invitation belongs to Jesus. The authority to save belongs to Jesus. Our responsibility is to carry the message faithfully, live in a manner worthy of the calling, and refuse to place unnecessary obstacles before those who are seeking Him. Some interruptions may never be recognized by the people who caused them. Some opportunities may pass without anyone understanding what could have happened. The church cannot rewrite every encounter, yet it can learn from Scripture and become more faithful in the encounters still ahead. The invitation remains. We were called to proclaim Christ, teach His Word, love our neighbors, and make disciples. We were never called to interrupt the invitation He extends.
