Chapter 12 What Happens When the Invitation Is Interrupted? An invitation carries the possibility of a life changing encounter. A person who has been searching for hope may finally hear a message that reaches the deepest part of their heart. Someone burdened by shame may begin to believe that mercy remains available. A person who has spent years feeling unseen may discover that God has not forgotten them. Such moments are precious, and the responsibility of those carrying the gospel should never be taken lightly. The interruption of an invitation can happen quietly. No public confrontation may occur. Nobody may raise their voice, announce a prohibition, or formally tell someone to leave. A person’s questions may simply be dismissed, their presence treated as inconvenient, their circumstances judged before their story is understood, or their sincere desire to seek God met with suspicion rather than encouragement. The invitation remains present in Scripture, yet the human experience surrounding the invitation becomes difficult enough that someone begins to wonder whether the invitation was truly meant for them. The consequences of such interruptions are not always visible to those responsible. A person may walk away without explaining what happened. They may never return to the congregation where the interaction occurred. They may continue believing in God while losing confidence in the people who claimed to represent Him. Someone else may begin searching for answers in another place, carrying unanswered questions and the memory of a spiritual encounter that never reached the fullness of what could have been. The person who caused the interruption may never know the outcome. They may remember a conversation as a brief correction, a necessary boundary, or a defense of what they believed was right. The person receiving the correction may remember something entirely different. What felt ordinary to one person may have become a defining moment for another. This is why the responsibility of the church extends beyond the words spoken from a pulpit. The gospel is carried through conversations, relationships, hospitality, patience, teaching, and the manner in which believers respond to those who are seeking. A message about the love of Jesus can be spoken accurately while the conduct surrounding that message communicates something contradictory. The words may invite, while the atmosphere discourages. The sermon may proclaim mercy, while the people listening are made to question whether mercy has room for them. The interruption of an invitation does not always begin with an intention to harm. Sometimes the interruption begins with assumptions that have never been examined. Sometimes a believer becomes so confident in personal understanding that questions are interpreted as rebellion. Sometimes a leader believes protecting the church requires controlling every unfamiliar circumstance. Sometimes people who have spent years inside a religious community forget how intimidating the first steps toward faith can be for someone who has never felt welcomed. The danger lies in allowing personal discomfort to become spiritual authority. The Encounter Philip Was Willing to Interrupt His Own Journey For Acts 8 presents a remarkable account of a man traveling from Jerusalem who was reading the prophet Isaiah. The Ethiopian eunuch possessed influence, education, and responsibility, yet the passage he was reading remained difficult to understand. He was searching for meaning, and his questions were sincere. Philip was directed toward the man’s journey. When Philip approached, he did not assume the man was incapable of understanding Scripture. He did not dismiss the question. He did not begin with a judgment about the man’s background, status, nationality, or circumstances. Philip asked whether the man understood what he was reading, and the man invited Philip to explain. The encounter developed through Scripture. Philip began with the passage the man was reading and proclaimed Jesus. The conversation did not require the seeker to arrive with every answer already settled. His questions became the starting point for instruction, and the instruction led toward understanding. The eunuch eventually asked about baptism. Philip responded to the request, and the man was baptized. Acts 8:26–39 provides a powerful picture of someone seeking understanding and a believer willing to meet him along the way. The account does not present Philip as controlling the man’s journey or demanding that every question be resolved before the conversation could begin. Philip served the purpose of the encounter by bringing the man toward an understanding of Jesus. The passage deserves careful attention because the man was not standing inside a familiar religious environment surrounded by people who knew the customs. He was traveling, reading, questioning, and searching. His encounter required someone willing to approach him without making his unfamiliarity a barrier. The church can learn from Philip’s willingness to recognize a searching heart. People rarely arrive with identical backgrounds, knowledge, experiences, or questions. Some have spent years studying Scripture. Others know only a few passages. Some carry painful experiences with religion. Others have never attended a church. Some understand the language of faith, while others struggle to know how to ask what they need to ask. A sincere question should not become a reason to humiliate someone. A search for understanding should not be met with the assumption that the person is dangerous, dishonest, or spiritually inferior. Questions may reveal confusion, but confusion is not the same as rebellion. A person asking for clarification may be standing at the beginning of understanding rather than demonstrating an unwillingness to learn. Philip did not have to possess every answer to serve the moment faithfully. He needed to be willing to explain what he understood and direct the conversation toward Jesus. Imagine the consequences had Philip approached the encounter differently. Suppose he had mocked the man’s inability to understand. Suppose he had treated the man’s questions as evidence that he did not belong among those seeking God. Suppose he had decided that the man needed to correct his entire life before Scripture could be explained to him. The account would have become a story about an opportunity interrupted rather than an encounter facilitated. We cannot claim to know every possible outcome of an imagined interruption. We can, however, recognize the responsibility revealed by the actual account. Philip was willing to participate in what God was doing rather than make his own assumptions the center of the encounter. The question for the church is not whether every person seeking Jesus will have a simple journey. The question is whether believers are willing to help people understand the gospel or whether their conduct adds unnecessary obstacles to the search. The Cost of an Unnecessary Barrier Jesus repeatedly encountered people whose circumstances made them unpopular, unfamiliar, or difficult for religious communities to accept. The reactions of those communities often revealed more about their assumptions than about the people seeking Him. In Luke 19, Zacchaeus was a chief tax collector and a wealthy man. His profession carried a reputation that made him despised among the people. When Jesus entered Jericho, Zacchaeus wanted to see Him. His position and stature created a practical difficulty, so he climbed a tree to see Jesus as He passed. Jesus stopped, looked up, and called Zacchaeus by name. He told him to come down because He intended to stay at his house. The crowd responded with murmuring. Their objection was directed toward Jesus’ decision to enter the home of someone they regarded as a sinner. The crowd saw Zacchaeus through his reputation. Jesus saw the person standing before Him and initiated a relationship. The encounter produced a response in Zacchaeus. He declared his intention to give to the poor and make restitution for those he had wronged. Jesus then announced that salvation had come to the house and affirmed that the Son of Man had come to seek and save the lost. The crowd’s murmuring did not prevent Jesus from entering the house, yet the account reveals how quickly people can criticize the very action through which mercy reaches someone. Religious observers sometimes become so focused on the perceived unworthiness of another person that they struggle to recognize the significance of Christ drawing near. The crowd had an opinion about Zacchaeus before the encounter had produced its fruit. Jesus did not permit the crowd’s opinion to dictate His willingness to engage. The church must consider the possibility that some of the people most eager to discuss who deserves attention may be overlooking the very work of grace they claim to defend. The gospel does not ask believers to pretend wrongdoing has never occurred. Zacchaeus demonstrated repentance through a willingness to address the harm he had caused. The encounter involved transformation, responsibility, and restoration. Yet the process began with Jesus extending a personal invitation to relationship. The invitation was not a reward for having already perfected his conduct. The encounter became a moment through which change took place. That distinction matters when considering people who arrive carrying histories, questions, reputations, or identities that have already been judged by others. A church may become so preoccupied with deciding whether someone is acceptable that the congregation never creates an environment where honest spiritual conversation can occur. The people who are seeking Jesus should not have to convince every observer that they deserve the opportunity to seek Him. When the Message Is Heard Through the Conduct of the Messenger Paul wrote to the Corinthians about the responsibility of believers to live as representatives of Christ. In 2 Corinthians 5:20, he described them as ambassadors for Christ, carrying a message of reconciliation. An ambassador does not become the owner of the message. The responsibility belongs to the one who commissioned the ambassador. Faithfulness requires the representative to communicate the message accurately while remembering the authority from which the message comes. The church carries the message of reconciliation. Believers are entrusted with proclaiming the death and resurrection of Jesus, explaining the gospel, teaching His commandments, and encouraging people toward a life of discipleship. The manner of that representation matters. Someone who encounters a believer may not immediately separate the believer’s conduct from the believer’s claims about Jesus. A person who experiences patience may begin to understand patience as a characteristic of the faith being presented. A person who encounters compassion may recognize something of the mercy described in Scripture. A person who experiences contempt may struggle to reconcile the treatment received with the message of love being proclaimed. This does not mean the conduct of every believer perfectly defines Christ. Jesus remains the standard by which the church must examine itself. The failures of His followers do not erase His character or change His truth. Nevertheless, the church cannot dismiss the consequences of its own conduct simply by telling wounded people to look beyond the people who hurt them. The responsibility of an ambassador is serious precisely because the message belongs to someone greater than the representative. When believers interrupt a sincere search through unnecessary humiliation, suspicion, or exclusion, the interruption may affect the way the message is heard. The person may remember the correction more clearly than the Scripture offered afterward. The person may remember being treated as a problem rather than being taught. The person may struggle to trust the voice of someone who spoke about the love of Christ while showing little patience. The appropriate response is not defensiveness. The appropriate response is examination. The church must ask whether the message was communicated faithfully, whether the conduct reflected the character of Jesus, whether the correction served restoration, and whether the interaction helped someone understand the gospel more clearly. A sincere desire to protect biblical truth cannot excuse careless treatment of human beings. The People Who Leave Without Saying Why Not every interrupted invitation produces an argument. Some people leave quietly. A person may stop attending after several uncomfortable experiences. Someone may withdraw from a ministry after a conversation that made them feel unwelcome. Another may continue sitting in the congregation while privately deciding never to disclose their struggles again. Someone may remain in the building but stop asking questions, stop seeking counsel, and stop trusting the people who once seemed safe. Silence can be mistaken for agreement. Absence can be mistaken for indifference. Withdrawal can be interpreted as evidence that a person never truly wanted God. Such conclusions may be unfair. A person who leaves a spiritual environment may carry complicated feelings. They may still love Jesus. They may still pray. They may still read Scripture. They may still hope for a relationship with God while trying to recover from an experience that damaged their trust in religious people. The church must resist the temptation to explain every departure through assumptions about the person’s character. Some people leave because they have found another congregation. Some leave because of practical circumstances. Some leave because they are grieving, struggling, overwhelmed, or searching for a different environment. Some leave after experiencing treatment that they could not reconcile with the message being taught. The responsibility to care for people should not disappear simply because they are no longer physically present. Jesus described the shepherd’s concern for the sheep in John 10. His language reveals a relationship characterized by care, protection, knowledge, and sacrifice. The shepherd knows the sheep. The relationship is personal rather than merely organizational. Church attendance is not the only measure of spiritual life, and the people who have stopped attending should not automatically become subjects of speculation. They remain human beings deserving compassion. Their questions remain worthy of attention. Their experiences may reveal areas where the church needs correction. The absence of someone who once sought fellowship should invite concern rather than gossip. A congregation that recognizes someone has withdrawn may choose to reach out with sincerity, without pressure or manipulation. A simple expression of care may communicate that the person remains valued even when attendance has changed. Listening may reveal a misunderstanding, a practical need, or a genuine wound requiring accountability. The purpose of reaching out should not be to restore appearances. The purpose should be to care for the person. A church cannot force someone to return. A church can make certain that its efforts to reconnect do not become another attempt to control the person’s decisions. The invitation belongs to Jesus, and the relationship between a believer and Christ cannot be reduced to a congregation’s ability to retain attendance. When We Make the Journey More Difficult Than Jesus Made It There is a difference between teaching someone the cost of discipleship and creating additional burdens through personal preferences. Jesus spoke clearly about following Him. Discipleship involves surrender, obedience, sacrifice, humility, and a willingness to carry the cross. The gospel does not promise that following Christ will always be comfortable. The church has a responsibility to teach those truths without weakening the message. However, the difficulty of discipleship should never become an excuse for adding human obstacles that Scripture does not require. Jesus addressed this concern directly in Matthew 23 when He confronted religious leaders who placed heavy burdens upon others while failing to embody the mercy, justice, and faithfulness that mattered deeply to God. His criticism was not directed against faithful teaching. His criticism exposed a religious system in which burdens were imposed without the character and responsibility necessary to serve people properly. The church must examine the burdens placed upon those who seek fellowship and understanding. Are people being taught Scripture, or are they being required to satisfy personal expectations before anyone will listen to them? Are boundaries established to protect people and support responsible ministry, or are boundaries being used to protect the comfort of those who hold authority? Are questions being answered through patient teaching, or are people being silenced through intimidation? Are people being directed toward Christ, or are they becoming dependent upon approval from individuals who have placed themselves between the seeker and the Savior? The answers require humility. Some boundaries are necessary. Some situations require accountability. Some forms of conduct must be addressed responsibly. Some people need careful pastoral guidance. Biblical teaching must remain faithful, and the church must not confuse compassion with the abandonment of responsibility. Nevertheless, unnecessary burdens can obscure the purpose of discipleship. A person should be able to seek understanding without being treated as though every question represents an attack. A person should be able to approach the church without being required to pretend that painful experiences never occurred. A person should be able to learn without being humiliated for the knowledge they have not yet acquired. The church was entrusted with teaching people to observe the commandments of Jesus. Teaching requires patience, explanation, relationship, and a willingness to recognize that growth takes time. The Invitation Must Remain Clear The gospel announces that Jesus Christ came into the world to save sinners. His death and resurrection provide the foundation of Christian hope. The invitation to come to Him is not dependent upon a congregation’s ability to make every person comfortable with another person’s presence. The church must never lose the clarity of the gospel while examining the manner in which the gospel is presented. People need to hear about repentance, faith, grace, forgiveness, obedience, and the transforming work of the Holy Spirit. They need biblical teaching that does not reduce the Christian life to vague encouragement or personal affirmation. They also need to encounter believers who understand that the message of salvation was entrusted to people who themselves depend upon mercy. The invitation should not be distorted by the arrogance of those carrying it. The church cannot determine every response to the gospel. Some people will reject the message. Some will misunderstand. Some will require time. Some will have questions that cannot be answered in a single conversation. Some will disagree with teachings they encounter. Some will make decisions that believers consider unwise. The responsibility of the church is to remain faithful in proclamation, teaching, prayer, service, and care. The responsibility is not to manufacture spiritual responses through pressure or to claim authority over the conscience of another person. Jesus called people to follow Him. He taught, corrected, warned, loved, and served. He did not need human manipulation to establish the authority of His message. A congregation should be able to examine its own conduct without fearing that self examination will weaken biblical conviction. Honest accountability strengthens the witness of the church. A willingness to apologize when wrong demonstrates humility. A willingness to listen does not erase truth. A willingness to care for someone whose experiences differ from our own does not require abandoning Scripture. Such conduct reveals that believers understand the seriousness of carrying a message that belongs to Jesus. Removing What Does Not Belong in the Way The church cannot remove every difficulty a person will encounter while following Christ. The path of discipleship contains challenges that require faith, courage, perseverance, and obedience. Believers must teach those realities honestly. The church can, however, examine the obstacles created through its own conduct. We can reconsider the way we speak to people whose backgrounds are unfamiliar. We can examine whether our traditions have become more important than the principles they were intended to serve. We can recognize the difference between biblical conviction and personal preference. We can refuse to treat a person’s questions as an inconvenience. We can create opportunities for honest conversation, responsible teaching, and spiritual growth. We can also recognize that people may carry wounds we do not immediately see. A person who appears distant may have learned to protect themselves from rejection. Someone who seems angry may be struggling to express pain. Someone who hesitates to trust spiritual leaders may have experienced authority used in ways that damaged their confidence. Careful listening may reveal what judgment would have missed. The church must remember that Jesus is capable of meeting people in circumstances that believers do not fully understand. Our responsibility is to faithfully proclaim Him and serve those seeking Him. We do not need to control every aspect of the encounter to remain faithful. The invitation should remain clear enough that people can recognize the voice of Christ through the message being presented. The Encounter We Must Not Interrupt Every believer should consider the possibility that someone may be standing near the beginning of a spiritual journey while we are standing near the beginning of an assumption. The person seeking Jesus may not look familiar. Their questions may not sound like our questions. Their history may differ from ours. Their understanding may be incomplete. Their life may contain circumstances that require careful teaching, responsible accountability, and time. None of those realities gives us permission to treat the person as though the invitation belongs to us. The Ethiopian eunuch needed someone willing to explain Scripture. Zacchaeus needed an encounter with Jesus that reached beyond the crowd’s opinion. The people described throughout the Gospels needed someone willing to recognize that the presence of Christ was not reserved for those whom religious observers considered respectable. The church must remain willing to serve the encounter rather than interrupt it. We should teach faithfully. We should love sincerely. We should correct responsibly. We should listen patiently. We should examine our conduct honestly. We should recognize the dignity of people who arrive carrying questions and experiences we may never fully understand. The gospel belongs to Jesus. The invitation belongs to Jesus. The authority to save belongs to Jesus. Our responsibility is to carry the message faithfully, live in a manner worthy of the calling, and refuse to place unnecessary obstacles before those who are seeking Him. Some interruptions may never be recognized by the people who caused them. Some wounds may never be explained. Some people may never return to the congregation where their journey became difficult. The church cannot rewrite every experience, yet the church can learn from the consequences of its conduct and become more faithful in the encounters still ahead. The invitation remains. Our responsibility is to make certain that the message we carry does not become obscured by the way we carry it. We were called to proclaim Christ, teach His Word, love our neighbors, and make disciples. We were never called to interrupt the invitation He extends.
