PART III · THE COST OF INTERFERENCE

What Happens When the Invitation Is Interrupted?

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 12 of 17Reading edition

An invitation may become a life changing encounter for someone searching for hope, carrying shame, or longing to know whether God has remembered them. Such moments are precious. The responsibility of those carrying the gospel should never be taken lightly.

An interruption is not always a formal prohibition. A question may be dismissed, a person treated as inconvenient, or a sincere search met with suspicion. Conduct surrounding the invitation may become so discouraging that someone begins to wonder whether the message is meant for them. We cannot know the outcome of every difficult encounter, nor what a brief interaction may mean to someone else.

The gospel is carried through conversations, relationships, hospitality, patience, teaching, and the manner in which believers respond to those who are seeking. A message about Jesus may be spoken accurately while surrounding conduct communicates something contradictory. Personal discomfort must never be mistaken for spiritual authority.

Philip was willing to serve the encounter

Acts 8:26–39 (AMPC) presents a remarkable account of a man traveling from Jerusalem who was reading the prophet Isaiah. The Ethiopian eunuch possessed influence and responsibility, yet the passage he was reading remained difficult to understand. He was searching for meaning, and his questions were sincere.

Philip was directed toward the man's journey. When he approached, he did not assume the man was incapable of understanding Scripture. He asked whether the man understood what he was reading, and the man invited Philip to explain. The encounter developed through the Word. Philip began with the passage the man was reading and proclaimed Jesus.

The conversation did not require the seeker to arrive with every answer already settled. His questions became the starting point for instruction, and the instruction led toward understanding. The eunuch eventually asked about baptism. Philip responded to the request, and the man was baptized.

Acts 8 provides a picture of someone seeking understanding and a believer willing to meet him along the way. Philip did not control the man's journey or abandon the responsibility of teaching. He was present, attentive, and faithful to the direction given.

People arrive with different backgrounds, knowledge, experiences, and questions. Some know Scripture well; others are learning how to ask what they do not yet understand. Confusion is not automatically rebellion, and a sincere question should not become a reason for humiliation.

Philip did not need to possess every answer to serve faithfully. He explained what he understood and directed the conversation toward Jesus. His role was neither to take ownership of the man's conscience nor to abandon the responsibility of teaching.

The Cost of an Unnecessary Barrier

Luke 19:1–10 (AMPC) tells of Zacchaeus, a chief tax collector and wealthy man whose profession carried a reputation that made him despised among the people. When Jesus entered Jericho, Zacchaeus wanted to see Him. His position and stature created a practical difficulty, so he climbed a tree to see Jesus as He passed.

Jesus stopped, looked up, and called Zacchaeus by name. He told him to come down because He intended to stay at his house. The decision was personal and deliberate. Jesus did not merely acknowledge Zacchaeus from a distance. He entered the man's home and created an opportunity for an encounter that would have lasting consequences.

The crowd responded with murmuring. Their objection was directed toward Jesus' decision to enter the home of someone they regarded as a sinner. The crowd saw Zacchaeus through his reputation. Jesus initiated an encounter with the person standing before Him.

Zacchaeus responded by declaring his intention to give to the poor and make restitution for those he had wronged. Jesus announced that salvation had come to the house and affirmed that the Son of Man had come to seek and save the lost.

The crowd's murmuring reveals how quickly people can criticize the action through which mercy reaches someone. The gospel does not ask us to pretend wrongdoing never occurred; Zacchaeus demonstrated repentance through restitution. Transformation and responsibility followed an encounter Jesus initiated.

That distinction matters when considering people who arrive carrying histories, questions, reputations, or circumstances that have already been judged by others. A church may become so preoccupied with deciding whether someone is acceptable that the congregation never creates an environment where honest spiritual conversation can occur. The people seeking Jesus should not have to convince every observer that they deserve the opportunity to seek Him.

A reputation may explain why people hold an opinion. A reputation does not give the crowd authority to determine whether Jesus may engage a person. Believers should be careful not to confuse discernment with a verdict pronounced before the person has been heard.

When the Message Is Heard Through the Conduct of the Messenger

Paul wrote to the Corinthians about the responsibility of believers to live as representatives of Christ. In 2 Corinthians 5:20 (AMPC), he described them as ambassadors for Christ, carrying a message of reconciliation.

An ambassador does not become the owner of the message. The responsibility belongs to the one who commissioned the ambassador. Faithfulness requires the representative to communicate accurately while remembering the authority from which the message comes. Personal preferences cannot replace the commission, and conduct should not contradict the character of the One represented.

The church carries the message of reconciliation. Believers are entrusted with proclaiming the death and resurrection of Jesus, explaining the gospel, teaching His commandments, and encouraging people toward a life of discipleship. The manner of that representation matters.

A person may struggle to separate a believer's conduct from the believer's claims about Jesus. Patience and compassion can help make the message recognizable; contempt may obscure it. The failures of Christ's followers do not erase His character or truth, yet the church cannot dismiss the consequences of its conduct by telling wounded people simply to look beyond those who hurt them.

Those who speak for Christ should consider how words, tone, timing, and actions affect an encounter. Truth may require a difficult conversation, but faithfulness does not require cruelty or humiliation. When we interrupt a sincere search through unnecessary suspicion or exclusion, the person may remember the treatment more clearly than the Scripture offered. We must examine whether our conduct helps people understand the gospel or obscures what we intended to carry.

When we make the journey harder than Jesus made it

There is a difference between teaching someone the cost of discipleship and creating additional burdens through personal preferences. Jesus spoke clearly about following Him. Discipleship involves surrender, obedience, sacrifice, humility, and a willingness to carry the cross. The church has a responsibility to teach those truths without weakening the message.

However, the difficulty of discipleship should never become an excuse for adding human obstacles that Scripture does not require.

Jesus addressed this concern in Matthew 23:1–4 (AMPC), confronting religious leaders who placed heavy burdens upon others while failing to embody the mercy, justice, and faithfulness God required. His criticism exposed a religious system in which burdens were imposed without the character and responsibility necessary to serve people properly.

The church must examine the burdens placed upon those seeking fellowship and understanding. Are people being taught Scripture, or required to satisfy personal expectations before anyone will listen? Are boundaries serving protection and responsible ministry, or merely preserving the comfort of those holding authority? Are questions answered patiently or silenced through intimidation?

Some boundaries are necessary, and some situations require accountability or pastoral guidance. Biblical teaching must remain faithful. Yet unnecessary burdens can obscure discipleship. People should be able to seek understanding and learn without being humiliated for questions or knowledge they have not yet acquired. Teaching requires patience, explanation, relationship, and recognition that growth takes time.

Removing what does not belong in the way

The church cannot remove every difficulty a person will encounter while following Christ. The path of discipleship contains challenges that require faith, courage, perseverance, and obedience. Believers must teach those realities honestly.

The church can, however, examine the obstacles created through its own conduct. We can reconsider the way we speak to people whose backgrounds are unfamiliar. We can examine whether traditions have become more important than the principles they were intended to serve. We can recognize the difference between biblical conviction and personal preference. We can refuse to treat a person's questions as an inconvenience. We can create opportunities for honest conversation, responsible teaching, and spiritual growth.

We can also recognize that people may carry wounds we do not immediately see. A person who appears distant may have learned to protect themselves from rejection. Someone who seems angry may be struggling to express pain. Someone who hesitates to trust spiritual leaders may have experienced authority used in ways that damaged confidence. Careful listening may reveal what judgment would have missed.

Removing an unnecessary barrier does not mean removing the cross from the message. We must stop confusing the burden Christ calls people to carry with burdens we place upon them through pride, fear, impatience, or unexamined preference. The distinction deserves careful consideration, especially when the people affected have little power to challenge the person making the decision.

The invitation should remain clear enough that people can recognize the voice of Christ through the message being presented.

The encounter we must not interrupt

Every believer should consider the possibility that someone may be standing near the beginning of a spiritual journey while we are standing near the beginning of an assumption. The person seeking Jesus may not look familiar. Their questions may not sound like our questions. Their history may differ from ours. Their understanding may be incomplete. Their life may contain circumstances that require careful teaching, responsible accountability, and time.

None of those realities gives us permission to treat the person as though the invitation belongs to us.

The Ethiopian eunuch needed someone willing to explain Scripture. Zacchaeus needed an encounter with Jesus that reached beyond the crowd's opinion. The people described throughout the Gospels needed someone willing to recognize that the presence of Christ was not reserved for those whom religious observers considered respectable.

The church must remain willing to serve the encounter rather than interrupt it. We should teach faithfully, listen patiently, and recognize the dignity of people carrying questions or experiences we may never fully understand. Some interruptions may never be recognized by those who caused them, and some opportunities may pass without anyone understanding what might have happened. We cannot rewrite every encounter, but we can learn from Scripture and become more faithful in those still ahead.

Sometimes the consequence of an interrupted encounter is not immediately visible. A person may simply stop returning, and the congregation may never know what shaped that decision. The next chapter turns toward those absences and the stories that may remain unheard.
