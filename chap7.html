Chapter Seven When Identity Becomes an Excuse for Exclusion There are moments when a person is no longer being considered according to what they believe, what they are seeking, or how they are responding to God. Instead, an assumption about their identity becomes the deciding factor in how they are treated. Before a conversation begins, before Scripture is opened together, and before anyone has taken the time to understand their story, a conclusion may already have been reached. The person becomes defined by a category, and the category begins to determine the level of compassion they receive. Such treatment deserves careful examination within the church because the gospel addresses human beings individually while also revealing the universal need for salvation. Every person carries a life shaped by experiences, decisions, relationships, circumstances, and convictions. No single description of a person’s life can replace the responsibility to see that person as someone created by God and in need of His grace. The danger becomes especially serious when identity is used as a reason to deny someone the opportunity to approach Jesus. Believers may become so focused on a particular characteristic, history, struggle, or circumstance that they lose sight of the person standing before them. The conversation becomes centered on what makes someone different, while the invitation of Christ receives less attention. Jesus never treated people as though a label provided sufficient information about the condition of their hearts. His encounters reveal a Savior who recognized the individual before Him, understood the spiritual need of humanity, and spoke with authority while demonstrating compassion. The people He encountered were not interchangeable examples in a religious argument. They were individuals whose lives mattered. The Gospels repeatedly reveal how religious assumptions influenced the way people viewed others. Certain individuals were identified by their occupations, reputations, associations, or perceived failures. Those descriptions often carried social consequences that extended beyond the actual circumstances of the person. Once a reputation became established, others could feel justified in maintaining distance without examining whether their response reflected God’s heart. Jesus challenged the confidence people placed in outward judgments. His ministry demonstrated that a person’s standing within society did not determine the value of that person before God. The wealthy and the poor, the respected and the despised, the religiously knowledgeable and the spiritually searching all encountered the reality of His ministry. Their circumstances differed, yet every person needed the grace of God. The church must remember that salvation is not reserved for people whose lives appear familiar to the congregation. The gospel is proclaimed to humanity. The Great Commission calls believers to go into the world, make disciples, baptize, and teach people to observe the commandments of Christ. The scope of that commission cannot be reduced according to the comfort, familiarity, or approval of those carrying the message. Galatians 3:28 presents a powerful declaration concerning the unity of believers in Christ. Paul writes that distinctions involving Jew and Greek, slave and free, male and female do not establish separate standing within the body of Christ. The passage does not erase every distinction within human life, nor does the context eliminate the responsibilities of discipleship. Paul is proclaiming the shared standing of those who belong to Christ and the unity found through faith. That truth should profoundly influence how believers treat people. No person should be made to feel that a human category places them beyond the reach of God’s invitation. The church must not create a hierarchy of human worth, assigning greater dignity to those whose lives resemble the congregation while treating others as though their presence requires special permission. The issue is not whether Scripture speaks about sin, holiness, repentance, or transformation. Scripture speaks clearly about all those matters. The issue is whether believers have allowed particular subjects to consume their attention until the broader responsibilities of the gospel have become neglected. A congregation may become deeply invested in identifying the sins it associates with certain people while giving far less attention to the sins Scripture repeatedly confronts within the religious community itself. Pride, hypocrisy, greed, unforgiveness, partiality, gossip, arrogance, dishonesty, and the misuse of authority receive serious attention throughout the Bible. Those sins can exist quietly within respectable religious environments, sometimes protected by reputation, position, familiarity, and the assumption that the person exhibiting such behavior must be spiritually mature. James directly confronts partiality within the assembly of believers. In James 2, he describes the treatment of people according to outward appearances and financial status. The person wearing fine clothing receives favorable attention, while the poor person is treated with less dignity. James identifies such behavior as incompatible with the faith of Jesus Christ. His instruction demonstrates that the church’s treatment of people is not a secondary matter separate from spiritual obedience. Partiality can take forms beyond financial status. People may receive different treatment according to education, family connections, reputation, physical appearance, social standing, disability, background, or other characteristics. The underlying problem remains the same. Human distinctions begin influencing the way believers demonstrate love and extend dignity. When identity becomes an excuse for exclusion, the church must ask whether the treatment being offered is consistent with the principles of Scripture. Are people being heard? Are they being treated with fairness? Are assumptions being examined? Are believers applying the same standards of humility and accountability to themselves that they expect from others? Are spiritual conversations taking place with patience, or has judgment already determined the outcome? These questions are particularly important when LGBTQIA+ people are discussed within Christian communities. Too often, conversations concerning sexuality become so focused on debating terminology, defining boundaries, and defending positions that the humanity of the people involved disappears from view. Individuals may be discussed in broad generalizations without considering their experiences, their faith, their questions, their relationships, their wounds, or their desire to know God. A person should never become the object of a conversation in which everyone speaks about them while no one is willing to speak with them. Scripture should be studied carefully, convictions should be addressed honestly, and difficult questions should not be avoided. The responsibility to engage those questions does not remove the responsibility to listen to the people whose lives are being discussed. There is a difference between examining a theological question and reducing a human being to that question. The church can study biblical passages, discuss interpretations, teach doctrine, and address discipleship without making a person’s identity the sole measure of their worth or the determining factor in whether they receive compassion. The way believers approach such conversations matters. A person may already have experienced rejection from family members, religious communities, friends, or society. Entering a church may require courage, particularly when previous encounters with Christianity have left wounds. The congregation cannot assume that a person arriving is seeking an argument. They may be seeking prayer, understanding, community, worship, or simply an opportunity to hear about Jesus. Jesus did not instruct His followers to make assumptions about people before engaging them. His ministry consistently revealed the importance of understanding the person before Him. He addressed individuals with wisdom appropriate to their circumstances. His words were not identical in every encounter, yet His purpose remained grounded in truth and the redemptive mission of God. The church should be careful not to confuse a person’s willingness to attend with an obligation to immediately disclose personal details. Visitors deserve dignity and appropriate boundaries. Spiritual growth cannot be measured by how quickly someone answers questions about their private life, nor should participation in worship become an occasion for unwanted scrutiny. There are times when believers must address difficult matters directly. Pastoral care may involve conversations concerning relationships, conduct, decisions, responsibilities, and the interpretation of Scripture. Such conversations should be approached with humility, prayer, wisdom, and a genuine desire for the spiritual well-being of those involved. The objective should never be humiliation or the satisfaction of proving someone wrong. The words of Jesus in Matthew 7 concerning judgment require every believer to examine their own life before attempting to correct another. His teaching does not prohibit discernment or correction. Rather, He exposes the hypocrisy of approaching another person’s faults while refusing to acknowledge one’s own. The image of the beam and the speck places personal accountability at the beginning of the conversation. That instruction is particularly relevant when a congregation has become accustomed to identifying the failures of people outside its own community. The ease with which someone can criticize another person’s circumstances may reveal a lack of attention to the condition of their own heart. Spiritual maturity requires the willingness to stand under the authority of Scripture rather than using Scripture primarily as a weapon against others. Every believer needs the mercy of God. Every believer depends upon grace. Every believer must remain teachable, accountable, and willing to repent. No position within the church changes those realities. The person teaching from the pulpit and the person sitting in the pew share the same dependence upon Christ. The church’s mission is not strengthened through selective compassion. A congregation cannot faithfully proclaim that Jesus saves while communicating through its treatment of people that certain individuals are less deserving of being heard. The gospel announces the need for redemption and the availability of God’s grace. The church must reflect the seriousness of that message while remaining faithful to the compassion of the Savior. Identity may describe aspects of a person’s experience, yet identity should never become a shortcut through which believers avoid the harder work of listening, understanding, studying, and loving. The person before us is more than the subject of our disagreement. They are a human being whose life is known by God and whose spiritual needs deserve serious attention. The church must also recognize that the people it judges may possess questions that challenge its own understanding. A sincere question should not automatically be treated as rebellion. A person seeking clarification should not be presumed hostile. A conversation requiring patience should not be abandoned simply because the answer cannot be delivered in a single sentence. The responsibility of discipleship requires time. Teaching requires careful attention to Scripture. Pastoral care requires wisdom. Relationships require trust. The work of helping people grow in Christ cannot be accomplished through assumptions that prevent meaningful engagement. When a person’s identity becomes the reason they are treated differently, the congregation must return to the principles Jesus demonstrated. He recognized the spiritual needs of people, spoke truth, called individuals toward God, and remained faithful to His mission. His ministry did not permit the opinions of others to determine which people were worthy of His attention. The church was entrusted with the gospel, not with the authority to determine whose humanity deserves recognition. Believers are called to teach the Word, make disciples, exercise discernment, practice holiness, and demonstrate the love of Christ. Those responsibilities must be carried with humility because the same grace proclaimed to others remains necessary within the church. No person should be reduced to a category before the church has considered the responsibility of loving that person as a neighbor. No disagreement should erase the obligation to treat another human being with dignity. No theological conversation should become an excuse for abandoning compassion. The question confronting the church is not simply what it believes about a particular group of people. The question is whether the way those beliefs are communicated and practiced reflects the character of Jesus Christ. A congregation may speak confidently about doctrine while failing to recognize the spiritual damage caused by partiality, careless judgment, or unnecessary exclusion. Faithfulness requires more than defending a position. Faithfulness requires examining whether the position is being carried with the humility, truth, mercy, and love Scripture commands. The church must remain willing to correct its own behavior whenever human assumptions begin taking the place of biblical responsibility. People seeking Jesus should encounter a community committed to His Word and willing to walk with them through the process of discipleship. The church may need to teach, encourage, challenge, and correct along that journey. Such ministry must never lose sight of the person receiving the message. Christ remains the Savior. His invitation remains His own. The church has been entrusted with proclaiming the gospel and helping people follow Him. Our responsibility is to represent Him faithfully, remembering that no human identity gives us permission to abandon the compassion, dignity, and love that belong to the ministry of Jesus Christ.
