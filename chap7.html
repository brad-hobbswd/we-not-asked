CHAPTER SEVEN When Identity Becomes an Excuse for Exclusion A person can be treated according to a category before anyone has listened to what they believe, what they are seeking, or how they are responding to God. An assumption may shape the conversation before Scripture is opened or the person’s story is understood. The individual becomes reduced to a description, and that description begins determining the compassion they receive. Such treatment deserves examination within the church. The gospel addresses human beings individually while revealing our shared need for salvation. Every life is shaped by experiences, relationships, decisions, circumstances, and convictions. No single description can replace the responsibility to recognize a person as someone created by God and in need of His grace. The danger becomes serious when identity is used as a reason to deny someone the opportunity to approach Jesus. A congregation may focus so heavily on what makes a person different that the person standing before them disappears from view. The discussion centers on a category, while the invitation of Christ receives less attention. Jesus did not treat a label as sufficient information about someone’s heart. His encounters reveal a Savior who recognized the person before Him, spoke truth, and demonstrated compassion. People were not merely examples in a religious argument; they were individuals whose lives mattered. Outward distinctions and shared need The Gospels show how reputations, occupations, associations, and perceived failures shaped the way people were treated. Once a description became attached to someone, others could feel justified in maintaining distance without examining whether their response reflected the heart of God. Jesus challenged the confidence placed in outward judgments. People with different social standing and circumstances encountered His ministry, yet all needed grace. The church must remember that the gospel is not reserved for those whose lives appear familiar to the congregation. Galatians 3:28 (AMPC) speaks of the unity of those who belong to Christ. Paul’s statement concerning distinctions of ethnicity, social status, and sex does not erase every distinction within human life or remove the responsibilities of discipleship. The passage declares the shared standing of believers in Christ and the unity found through faith. That truth should challenge any practice that treats one group as though their dignity or spiritual access is inferior. The church must not create a hierarchy of human worth, assigning greater compassion to people whose lives resemble the congregation while treating others as though their presence requires special permission. The responsibility to proclaim the gospel and make disciples cannot be narrowed according to comfort, familiarity, or approval. Partiality within the church Scripture addresses more than the sins often associated with people outside a congregation. Pride, hypocrisy, greed, unforgiveness, partiality, gossip, arrogance, dishonesty, and misuse of authority can also exist within respectable religious environments, sometimes protected by reputation or position. James 2:1–9 (AMPC) confronts partiality within the assembly. He describes people receiving different treatment according to outward appearance and financial status. The wealthy visitor is honored, while the poor person is treated with less dignity. James identifies such behavior as incompatible with the faith of Jesus Christ. The treatment of people is not a secondary matter separated from obedience. Partiality can take forms beyond wealth. Education, family connections, reputation, appearance, disability, background, or other characteristics may influence the way people are received. The underlying problem remains: human distinctions begin shaping the expression of love. The church should ask whether people are being heard fairly, whether assumptions are being examined, and whether believers apply the same humility and accountability to themselves that they expect from others. The standards of Scripture must not become a tool for scrutinizing some people while overlooking the conduct of others. When a person becomes the subject instead of a neighbor Conversations concerning LGBTQIA+ people can become so focused on terminology, boundaries, and defending positions that the humanity of those involved disappears. People may be discussed broadly without consideration of their experiences, faith, questions, relationships, wounds, or desire to know God. A person should not become the subject of a conversation in which everyone speaks about them while no one is willing to speak with them. Scripture should be studied carefully, convictions addressed honestly, and difficult questions considered. None of those responsibilities removes the obligation to listen to people whose lives are being discussed. Examining a theological question is not the same as reducing a human being to that question. The church can study biblical passages, discuss interpretations, teach doctrine, and address discipleship without making one aspect of a person’s life the sole measure of their worth or the determining factor in whether they receive compassion. Some people may arrive after experiencing rejection from family, friends, religious communities, or society. Entering a church may require courage, especially when earlier encounters with Christianity have left wounds. We should not assume a visitor is seeking argument. They may be looking for prayer, community, worship, understanding, or simply an opportunity to hear about Jesus. Listening does not require agreement with every conclusion. It does require recognizing the person before us and treating their experience with dignity. The church should not demand that visitors disclose private details as the price of being treated respectfully. A category can become a substitute for listening when the congregation assumes that everyone associated with a particular description thinks alike, wants the same thing, or has arrived for the same reason. Such assumptions make genuine conversation difficult. People may be carrying faith, uncertainty, questions, family concerns, grief, or experiences they have not chosen to disclose. A label cannot tell us what the person is ready to discuss, what support may be needed, or how the individual understands their own spiritual journey. The church should resist speaking as though a group of people were a single voice. Generalizations may simplify a discussion, yet they can also obscure the differences between individuals and turn people into illustrations of a position. Careful language allows room for accuracy. A person should not be assigned motives, beliefs, or behavior merely because others place them within a category. The responsibility to speak truthfully includes refusing to claim knowledge we do not possess. This principle also matters when a congregation is making decisions about ministry, hospitality, participation, or pastoral care. Decisions may involve genuine questions of doctrine, safeguarding, leadership responsibility, and the expectations of a particular ministry. Those questions should be addressed clearly and consistently, with Scripture, appropriate accountability, and a willingness to explain the reasoning. The person affected should not be left to discover a decision through embarrassment, gossip, or public speculation. Responsible communication can preserve clarity while treating the individual with respect. The church should also consider the difference between welcoming someone into a conversation and making promises about every question that may arise later. Hospitality is not a substitute for teaching, and a visitor should not be misled about the congregation's convictions. Honesty and kindness belong together. People deserve to know what a community believes, while believers remain responsible to communicate those beliefs without contempt or unnecessary exposure of someone's private life. A person may not be ready to discuss every issue the congregation believes important. That does not mean the church must abandon discipleship; it means discipleship should be approached with patience and discernment. Trust, understanding, and spiritual growth are cultivated through relationship and teaching, not through interrogation. The church should be able to hold a meaningful conversation without requiring the person to become a public case study. Pastoral conversations may address personal matters when appropriate, but those conversations should be guided by wisdom, consent, and care rather than curiosity or scrutiny. The responsibility of discipleship Christian discipleship includes teaching, repentance, obedience, correction, and transformation. The church must not abandon those responsibilities. The manner in which they are carried out, however, must reflect the character of Christ. Correction should be offered with humility and a genuine concern for spiritual well-being, never for humiliation or the satisfaction of proving someone wrong. Jesus addressed individuals according to the circumstances before Him. His words were not identical in every encounter, yet His purpose remained grounded in truth and the redemptive mission of God. His ministry did not allow the opinions of others to determine which people were worthy of His attention. The church must also remember Jesus’ teaching in Matthew 7:1–5 (AMPC) concerning judgment. He does not eliminate discernment or correction; He confronts hypocrisy and calls people to examine themselves before attempting to correct others. Personal accountability belongs at the beginning of the conversation. Every believer depends upon grace. The person teaching from the pulpit and the person sitting in the pew share the same need for mercy, instruction, and transformation. Selective compassion cannot strengthen the gospel. A congregation cannot faithfully proclaim that Jesus saves while communicating through its treatment of people that some are less deserving of being heard. No person should be reduced to a category before we consider our responsibility to love that person as a neighbor. Disagreement does not erase dignity, and theological discussion cannot become an excuse for abandoning compassion. The question is not only what a congregation believes about a particular group. The question is whether its beliefs are communicated and practiced with the humility, truth, mercy, and love Scripture commands. Faithfulness requires examining our conduct whenever assumptions begin taking the place of biblical responsibility. People seeking Jesus should encounter a community committed to His Word and willing to walk with them through discipleship. The church may need to teach, encourage, challenge, and correct along the journey, but ministry must never lose sight of the person receiving the message. Christ remains the Savior. His invitation remains His own. We are entrusted with proclaiming the gospel and helping people follow Him. No human identity gives us permission to abandon the compassion, dignity, and love that belong to the ministry of Jesus Christ.
