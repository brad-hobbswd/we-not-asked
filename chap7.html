PART II · THE PEOPLE WE DECIDED AGAINST

When Identity Becomes an Excuse for Exclusion

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 7 of 17Reading edition

Labels are convenient. They allow us to summarize someone in a word and move on without learning much about them. A label may describe one part of a person's experience, but when we allow the label to become the whole story, we stop seeing the person.

Identity matters deeply to people, and Scripture speaks about who we are in relation to God, our choices, our relationships, and the life He calls us to. The church should not reduce anyone to a single category, whether that category comes from a person's past, a struggle, a reputation, a family circumstance, or a disagreement.

When a label becomes the lens through which every interaction is interpreted, even ordinary moments can feel like scrutiny. A greeting becomes an assessment. A question becomes suspicion. A person's presence becomes a topic. We may not intend to create that atmosphere, but we should be willing to notice when our habits have done so.

Jesus often saw people beyond the descriptions others used for them. Zacchaeus was known as a tax collector, yet Luke 19 records Jesus calling him by name and entering his home.1 The encounter brought repentance and changed conduct, but the conversation began with Jesus addressing a person rather than merely responding to a reputation.

That does not mean labels are never useful or that distinctions in Scripture should be ignored. It means descriptions must not replace discernment, and discernment must not replace compassion. A person is not a discussion topic, a symbol, or a test of our convictions.

We can teach, ask questions, and address conduct without turning someone into a category. The next time we are tempted to speak about a person in shorthand, perhaps we should ask whether we know enough to speak at all, and whether our words help us love our neighbor more faithfully.

1. Luke 19:1–10 tells of Jesus meeting Zacchaeus, entering his home, and bringing a response marked by repentance and restitution. The account shows Jesus engaging a person rather than reducing him to his reputation.
