PART III · THE COST OF INTERFERENCE

The Wound Religion Left Behind

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 10 of 17Reading edition

Some wounds are difficult to explain because they develop in places where people expected safety. Someone may enter a church seeking prayer, guidance, understanding, or a new beginning. Their trust may be sincere, and seeking help may require courage. The experience that follows can strengthen that trust or leave wounds they never anticipated.

Religious wounds can be especially painful when people believe they are acting on behalf of God. Words spoken with spiritual authority may be remembered for years. Public embarrassment, rejection, pressure, or a careless use of Scripture can leave someone questioning not only the people who spoke, but also the God those people claimed to represent.

Spiritual authority carries responsibility beyond teaching, leading, correcting, or making decisions. Influence can shape how people understand God, themselves, relationships, and their place within the body of Christ. When that influence is exercised carelessly, the consequences may reach far beyond one encounter.

Some people remember a moment when church no longer felt safe. Others recognize a pattern of exclusion, unanswered questions, humiliating correction, gossip, pressure, or fear. Someone may continue attending while trust quietly erodes; another may leave without explaining. A person can remain devoted to God while struggling to trust religious leadership. Withdrawal from fellowship does not necessarily mean abandonment of God. People may leave because of grief, disappointment, exhaustion, fear, or a need to protect themselves from further harm. Their circumstances deserve understanding rather than immediate condemnation.

Ezekiel 34:1–6 confronts shepherds who neglected the people entrusted to their care. The chapter describes leaders who served themselves rather than strengthening the weak, healing the sick, binding the injured, bringing back the scattered, and seeking the lost. The image makes clear that leadership was never intended to be personal advantage. Shepherding requires care for people who are vulnerable, wounded, or disconnected.

The church should examine whether its practices reflect that responsibility. Are people who have been hurt receiving care? Are sincere questions heard? Are leaders willing to acknowledge when decisions have caused unnecessary harm? Are those who have withdrawn approached with genuine concern rather than assumptions about their motives?

A congregation may be known for worship, teaching, programs, growth, and public ministry while people within the community quietly carry experiences that have never been addressed. The appearance of success does not eliminate the responsibility to examine the condition of those entrusted to its care.

Scripture must not be abandoned, yet communicating truth faithfully includes considering the circumstances of the person receiving instruction. The Word should not become an instrument through which someone expresses personal anger, establishes superiority, or silences a sincere question.

Jesus demonstrated that truth and compassion belong together. He confronted wrongdoing, called people toward repentance, and challenged religious hypocrisy, yet His authority was never self-serving. The church must not imitate the authority of Christ while neglecting His character.

Leaders are not exempt from correction. A respected position does not require others to pretend that harmful experiences never occurred. Honoring leadership is not the same as excusing every action performed by someone holding a position. Scripture calls for respect and humility while also requiring truth, accountability, and protection of those who may be harmed.

When someone speaks about a wound, immediate defensiveness can deepen the injury. Dismissing the person as bitter, rebellious, unforgiving, or spiritually immature may prevent an honest examination. The congregation can become more concerned about protecting its reputation than understanding what happened.

Listening does not require assuming every account is complete or every allegation established, nor does it require an immediate judgment. Careful examination may take time and involve appropriate conversations, documentation, consultation, and safeguards. The goal should be truth rather than protection of a preferred narrative. A fair process can acknowledge the humanity of everyone involved while taking seriously the possibility of harm. The church should neither rush to condemn nor use uncertainty as an excuse to do nothing.

People who have experienced spiritual harm may need time before they can trust again. Their relationship with worship, prayer, Scripture, leadership, and community may require careful rebuilding. Healing should not be forced into a timeline determined by someone else.

Forgiveness and reconciliation are related, but not identical. Forgiveness may involve releasing bitterness and placing the matter before God. Reconciliation requires participation from those involved and may depend upon honesty, repentance, changed behavior, accountability, and rebuilding trust. A person can pursue forgiveness while maintaining appropriate boundaries.

The command to forgive must never be used to silence someone who has been harmed. Forgiveness does not permit wrongdoing to continue, require concealing abuse, eliminate responsible reporting, or place a vulnerable person back into an unsafe situation. Compassion must include wisdom and protection.

The church should make room for people who need healing without pressuring them to resume every former relationship or ministry role. Some may need a different congregation, different leadership, or time away from a particular environment. The purpose of spiritual care is to help people move toward Christ, not to insist that everyone follow the same path through recovery.

Jesus invited the weary and burdened to come to Him, describing Himself as meek and lowly in heart and offering rest (Matthew 11:28–30). The passage reveals the character of the Savior to whom people are directed.

The church should consider what people experience when they are told to come to Jesus. Are they encountering hope, or additional burdens created by human expectations? Are they being directed toward the Savior, or required to satisfy unwritten rules established by those who have placed themselves between the person and the invitation?

Mark 7:6–13 records Jesus confronting traditions that had been elevated above God’s commandments. Tradition can provide continuity and meaningful expressions of faith. The danger arises when human practices become unquestionable and interfere with the responsibilities God has established. Familiarity alone does not make a tradition authoritative.

Unclear or inconsistent expectations can leave people confused and discouraged. Someone may believe acceptance depends upon meeting standards that have never been clearly explained, receive conflicting messages from different leaders, or be judged according to expectations that change depending upon who is involved. Faithful spiritual care requires clarity: teaching grounded in Scripture, expectations communicated honestly, correction handled responsibly, and leadership held accountable. People should not be manipulated through fear, shame, or threats concerning their relationship with God.

Religious injury may affect families. A person rejected within a congregation may carry pain into relationships with parents, children, spouses, siblings, and friends. Words spoken during spiritual conflict can shape how someone understands their worth, belonging, or relationship with God.

Families should not underestimate the lasting effect of words spoken during spiritual conflict. A harsh statement intended to encourage repentance may be remembered as rejection; pressure intended to protect faith may be experienced as control; silence may communicate that a person is no longer welcome. Faithfulness to Scripture does not require cruelty toward relatives or the abandonment of relationships simply because difficult questions arise. Believers should approach family relationships with patience, humility, kindness, and love, even when hard conversations remain necessary.

A congregation should also acknowledge that spiritual harm can occur without dismissing the church as a whole. Genuine fellowship, faithful teaching, worship, prayer, service, and growth remain valuable. The purpose is to confront harmful conduct and strengthen what reflects the heart of Christ.

Healing may begin when someone is heard without being immediately corrected. They may need to describe what happened, express grief, ask questions, and recognize the difference between human failure and the character of God. Such conversations require patience rather than defensiveness.

The responsibility to address wounds does not rest entirely upon those who have been hurt.

There may be practical consequences to repair as well. Where a person was publicly embarrassed, the correction may need to be public enough to address the harm. Where confidential information was mishandled, the church must take steps to protect privacy and prevent further exposure. Where leadership decisions caused injury, responsible leaders should review those decisions and make changes when necessary. A private apology alone may not address harm that affected a wider community. Repair should be shaped by the nature of the injury rather than by what is easiest for those responsible.

The church must examine its own conduct and become willing to change. Leaders may need to acknowledge mistakes, congregations may need to reconsider practices, relationships may require apologies, and systems of accountability may need strengthening.

Those responses are not signs of weakness. They demonstrate a willingness to submit church life to Scripture. The church cannot faithfully proclaim reconciliation while refusing to address wounds created within its own community.

People who have been wounded by religion deserve to know that their experiences matter. They deserve dignity, appropriate opportunities to ask questions, and spiritual care that does not dismiss pain or pressure them into silence. The church must distinguish helping someone grow in faith from demanding that they conceal the wounds created by religious failure.

Honesty is the beginning of healing. The church must look carefully at its conduct, recognize where people have been harmed, and pursue correction with humility. Wounded people must never become an inconvenience to the mission of Christ.

Jesus remains the One who offers rest to the weary, hope to the broken, forgiveness to those who repent, and salvation to those who turn toward Him. Our responsibility is to direct people toward that Savior while ensuring that our conduct does not become another obstacle between the wounded and the One who heals.

Spiritual authority was entrusted for the care of people. Its misuse must not be ignored, minimized, or defended. Harm should be examined honestly, addressed responsibly, and met with the compassion that reflects the heart of Jesus Christ.
