PART III · THE COST OF INTERFERENCE

The Wound Religion Left Behind

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 10 of 17Reading edition

Some of the hardest wounds to describe are the ones received in places where we expected to be safe. A person may come to church looking for prayer, guidance, or a fresh beginning and leave carrying something they never expected: shame, fear, confusion, or the feeling that God must be disappointed in them too.

Religious harm can happen through a single encounter or through a pattern that slowly wears down trust. A careless use of Scripture, public embarrassment, pressure, gossip, or a refusal to hear a concern can stay with someone for years. We should not dismiss those experiences simply because the people involved believed they were doing the right thing.

Ezekiel 34:1–6 confronts shepherds who cared for themselves while neglecting the people entrusted to them. The passage names the responsibilities of tending, strengthening, healing, and seeking. Leadership is not measured only by what happens on a platform. The condition of the people under care matters.

When someone tells us church hurt them, our first response may be to explain, defend, or correct their description. We may worry that acknowledging pain will make the church look bad. A better first step is to listen. We can ask what happened, take the concern seriously, and investigate fairly without deciding in advance what the story must be.

Forgiveness and reconciliation are not identical. Forgiveness can be pursued before trust is rebuilt. Reconciliation requires honesty, participation, and often changed behavior. No one should use a command to forgive as a reason to conceal abuse, continue harm, or place a vulnerable person back into an unsafe situation.

Some people need time, support, or a different setting before they can trust again. We should not force a timetable for healing or insist that returning to the same relationship proves spiritual maturity. The aim of care is to help people move toward Christ, not to make them comfortable for the institution.

Repair may require more than saying sorry. A public humiliation may need a public correction; mishandled private information may require steps to protect confidentiality; a harmful practice may need to change. The response should fit the harm, and the people responsible should remain willing to be accountable.

Jesus invited the weary to come to Him and promised rest (Matthew 11:28–30). That invitation should shape the way we care for wounded people. The church cannot prevent every hurt, but we can stop defending avoidable harm and become willing to listen, repair, and learn.
