Chapter Ten The Wound Religion Left Behind Some wounds are difficult to explain because they were created in places where people expected to find safety. A person may enter a church believing they will encounter compassion, guidance, prayer, understanding, and the love of God. They may arrive carrying questions, grief, uncertainty, or a desire to begin again. Their trust may be sincere, and their willingness to seek spiritual help may require considerable courage. The experience that follows can either strengthen that trust or leave the person carrying wounds they never anticipated receiving. Religious wounds are particularly painful when the people involved believe they are acting on behalf of God. Words spoken with spiritual authority can carry extraordinary weight. A correction may be remembered for years. A public embarrassment may become a defining experience. A rejection communicated through Scripture may leave someone questioning not only the people who spoke but also the God those people claimed to represent. The church must recognize that spiritual authority carries responsibility beyond the ability to teach, lead, correct, or make decisions. Authority influences how people understand God, themselves, their relationships, and their place within the body of Christ. When that influence is exercised carelessly, the consequences may reach far beyond the original encounter. Some people remember the moment they stopped feeling safe in church. Others cannot identify a single event because the wound developed through repeated experiences. A pattern of exclusion, unanswered questions, humiliating correction, gossip, pressure, or fear may gradually change the way a person approaches spiritual community. The person may continue attending while quietly losing trust. Another may leave without explaining the reasons. Someone else may remain deeply devoted to God while struggling to believe that religious leaders can be trusted. The church should never assume that a person who has withdrawn from fellowship has abandoned God. Their departure may reflect grief, disappointment, exhaustion, fear, or an attempt to protect themselves from further harm. The circumstances deserve understanding rather than immediate condemnation. Scripture provides a serious warning concerning the responsibility of those who lead God’s people. Ezekiel 34 addresses shepherds who were failing in their responsibilities toward the flock. The chapter confronts leaders who were concerned with themselves while neglecting the needs of those entrusted to their care. The imagery reveals that leadership was never intended to be a position of personal advantage. The shepherd’s responsibility was to care for the flock. The passage describes the failure to strengthen the weak, heal the sick, bind up the injured, bring back the scattered, and seek the lost. Those responsibilities reveal the heart of faithful spiritual care. Leadership requires attention to people who are struggling, wounded, vulnerable, or disconnected. A shepherd who neglects those needs while maintaining the appearance of authority has misunderstood the purpose of leadership. The church must examine whether its practices reflect that responsibility. Are people who are wounded receiving care? Are those who have questions being listened to? Are people who have experienced spiritual harm being treated with patience? Are leaders willing to acknowledge when their decisions have caused unnecessary pain? Are those who have been scattered being pursued with genuine compassion? The answers cannot be determined through reputation alone. A congregation may be known for its worship, teaching, programs, growth, and public ministry while people within the community quietly carry experiences that have never been addressed. The appearance of success does not eliminate the responsibility to examine the condition of those entrusted to its care. Spiritual wounds may develop when Scripture is used without consideration for the circumstances of the person receiving instruction. Biblical truth must never be abandoned, yet the responsibility to communicate truth faithfully includes the manner in which the message is delivered. The Word of God should not become an instrument through which someone expresses personal anger, establishes superiority, or silences a sincere question. Jesus demonstrated that truth and compassion belong together. His ministry did not avoid difficult conversations, yet He approached people with wisdom, understanding, and a clear sense of purpose. He knew the hearts of those He encountered. His words could challenge, correct, expose, and call people toward repentance. His authority was never separated from His righteousness. The church must be careful not to imitate the authority of Christ while neglecting the character of Christ. A person may possess knowledge of Scripture and still need to grow in patience. A person may hold a leadership position and still need correction. A person may sincerely believe they are protecting the church while making decisions that require reconsideration. Spiritual authority should never become a shield against accountability. Leaders are not exempt from examining their conduct. The people they serve are not obligated to pretend that harmful experiences never occurred simply because the person responsible holds a respected position. Honoring leadership does not require denying the reality of harm. There is a difference between respecting a position and excusing every action performed by someone occupying that position. Scripture calls believers to humility, submission, wisdom, and respect, while also establishing the responsibility to recognize wrongdoing and protect those who may be harmed. These principles must be held together rather than used selectively. When someone speaks about a wound caused within the church, the response matters. Immediate defensiveness can deepen the injury. Accusations that the person is bitter, rebellious, unforgiving, or spiritually immature may prevent an honest examination of what happened. The church may become more concerned about protecting its reputation than understanding the experience of the person who has been hurt. Such responses can create a second wound. The original experience may have caused pain, and the attempt to discuss that pain may lead to further rejection. The person may conclude that their suffering has no place within the community and that speaking honestly will only create additional consequences. The church must learn to listen without assuming that every account is complete or that every allegation has already been established. Responsible listening does not require abandoning discernment. It requires allowing the matter to be examined carefully, treating those involved with dignity, and refusing to dismiss someone’s experience merely because acknowledging the possibility of harm is uncomfortable. People who have experienced spiritual wounds may need time before they can trust again. Their questions may not be resolved through a single conversation. Their relationship with worship, prayer, Scripture, leadership, and community may require careful rebuilding. The church should not place an unnecessary deadline upon healing or assume that forgiveness means the person must immediately resume every former relationship. Forgiveness and reconciliation are related, yet they are not identical. Forgiveness may involve releasing bitterness and placing the matter before God. Reconciliation requires participation from those involved and may depend upon honesty, repentance, changed behavior, accountability, and the rebuilding of trust. A person can pursue forgiveness while maintaining appropriate boundaries. The church should never use the command to forgive as a means of silencing someone who has been harmed. Biblical forgiveness is not permission for wrongdoing to continue. Forgiveness does not require concealing abuse, abandoning responsible reporting, or placing a vulnerable person back into an unsafe situation. Christian compassion must include wisdom and protection. The ministry of Jesus offers a powerful contrast to religious environments where people are made to feel that their wounds are inconvenient. Throughout the Gospels, He responded to people who needed help. He recognized suffering. He listened to those who were overlooked. He addressed the needs of people whose circumstances made them vulnerable. His ministry consistently demonstrated that human suffering deserved attention. The church must ask whether its own practices make room for people who need healing. A congregation may have extensive programs for worship, education, outreach, and fellowship while lacking a meaningful response when someone experiences harm within the community. The ability to organize ministry does not replace the responsibility to care for wounded people. Some wounds remain hidden because the person fears consequences. They may worry about losing friendships, being removed from ministry, damaging family relationships, or becoming the subject of gossip. Others may have been taught that questioning leadership is equivalent to questioning God. Such beliefs can make honest communication extremely difficult. The church should never create an environment in which people believe they must choose between protecting themselves and remaining faithful to God. Spiritual leadership should encourage responsible communication, appropriate accountability, and the pursuit of truth. A community that fears honest examination may eventually protect harmful practices simply because no one feels safe enough to speak. The wounds religion leaves behind can also affect how people read Scripture. Passages once associated with worship may become connected with fear. Prayer may feel difficult. The language of spiritual authority may create anxiety. A person may struggle to distinguish the voice of God from the voices of those who previously used religious language to control or condemn them. Such experiences require compassion. The church should not assume that spiritual healing can occur through pressure, confrontation, or repeated demands for participation. People may need opportunities to encounter Scripture in a healthier environment, rediscover the character of Jesus, and rebuild trust through relationships that demonstrate patience and consistency. Jesus invited people to learn from Him and described Himself as meek and lowly in heart in Matthew 11:28–30. His invitation was directed toward those who were weary and burdened. He offered rest and described His yoke as easy and His burden as light. The passage reveals the character of the One to whom people are being directed. The church should consider what people experience when they are told to come to Jesus. Are they encountering a message that offers hope, or are they receiving additional burdens created by human expectations? Are they being directed toward the Savior, or are they being required to satisfy the demands of people who have placed themselves between the person and the invitation? The burden of religious expectations can become overwhelming when people are required to navigate unwritten rules that have little connection to the actual teaching of Scripture. Expectations concerning appearance, background, family circumstances, social behavior, or personal history may become so deeply embedded within a congregation that questioning those expectations feels like questioning the faith itself. The church must distinguish between the commandments of God and the traditions of people. Jesus addressed this distinction in Mark 7, where He confronted religious practices that had become more important than obedience to God’s Word. His rebuke revealed the danger of allowing human traditions to interfere with the responsibilities God had established. Tradition can provide continuity, structure, and meaningful expressions of faith. The danger arises when tradition becomes an unquestionable authority that prevents the church from examining its conduct. A practice may be familiar and longstanding while still requiring careful evaluation according to Scripture. The wounds created by religious expectations may become especially painful when people believe that acceptance depends upon meeting standards that have never been clearly explained. They may receive conflicting messages from different leaders. They may be judged according to expectations that change depending upon who is involved. Such inconsistency can leave a person confused and discouraged. Faithful spiritual care requires clarity. Teaching should be grounded in Scripture. Expectations should be communicated honestly. Correction should be handled responsibly. Leadership should remain accountable. People should not be manipulated through fear, shame, or the threat of losing their relationship with God. The church must also recognize that spiritual wounds can affect families. A person who experiences rejection within a congregation may carry that pain into relationships with parents, children, spouses, siblings, and friends. Religious disagreements may become deeply personal when the language of faith is used to communicate that someone’s worth, belonging, or relationship with God is in question. Families should not underestimate the lasting effect of words spoken during moments of spiritual conflict. A parent may believe a harsh statement will encourage repentance, yet the child may remember the statement as a declaration of rejection. A spouse may believe pressure is necessary to protect faith, yet the other person may experience the pressure as a loss of safety. A family member may believe silence is appropriate, yet silence may communicate that the person is no longer welcome. The church must encourage believers to approach family relationships with the same biblical responsibilities of patience, humility, kindness, and love. Faithfulness to Scripture does not require cruelty toward relatives or the abandonment of relationships simply because difficult questions arise. A congregation should also be willing to acknowledge that some people have been hurt by religious communities without dismissing the church as a whole. The presence of harmful experiences does not erase the beauty of genuine Christian fellowship, faithful teaching, prayer, worship, service, and spiritual growth. The goal should be to confront what has caused harm while preserving and strengthening what reflects the heart of Christ. Healing may begin when someone is finally heard without being immediately corrected. A person may need an opportunity to describe what happened, express grief, ask questions, and recognize the difference between the actions of human beings and the character of God. The church should approach such conversations with patience rather than treating the person’s pain as a threat to its identity. The responsibility to heal wounded people cannot rest entirely upon those who have been hurt. The church must examine its own conduct and become willing to make changes where necessary. Leaders may need to acknowledge mistakes. Congregations may need to reconsider practices. Relationships may require apologies. Systems of accountability may need to become stronger. Such responses are not signs of weakness. They are expressions of a willingness to submit every part of church life to the authority of Scripture. The church cannot faithfully proclaim the ministry of reconciliation while refusing to address wounds created within its own community. The message of Christ calls believers toward reconciliation with God and toward relationships shaped by truth, humility, and love. Those responsibilities must remain visible within the congregation. The wounds religion left behind may take time to heal, and some people may never return to the same congregation where the harm occurred. Their decisions deserve careful consideration rather than immediate condemnation. A person may need a different community, different leadership, or time away from a particular environment to recover spiritually and emotionally. The church should not measure healing solely by whether someone returns to a former place. Healing may involve rediscovering prayer, reading Scripture, worshiping with others, establishing healthy boundaries, receiving support, or gradually rebuilding a relationship with God. The purpose of spiritual care is to help people move toward Christ, not to insist that every person follow the same path through recovery. The ministry of Jesus remains the standard by which the church must examine its treatment of people. His compassion was not superficial. His truth was not careless. His authority was not self-serving. His ministry revealed the character of God through the way He addressed people, taught them, corrected them, and offered hope. The church must remember that people are not merely hearing the words spoken from a pulpit. They are observing how believers treat one another. They are watching how leaders respond to questions, how congregations handle conflict, how families address disagreement, and whether compassion remains present when circumstances become difficult. The gospel should not become associated with the wounds created by people who failed to represent Christ faithfully. The church must remain willing to acknowledge that human beings can misuse religious authority and that such misuse deserves serious attention. Honesty about those failures does not weaken faith. Honest repentance can strengthen the church’s witness. The responsibility of spiritual leadership is to care for people, teach Scripture, pursue truth, protect the vulnerable, and direct the congregation toward Jesus Christ. Those responsibilities must never become secondary to preserving appearances, defending personal reputations, or maintaining traditions that cannot withstand biblical examination. People who have been wounded by religion deserve to hear that their experiences matter. They deserve to be treated with dignity. They deserve opportunities to ask sincere questions. They deserve spiritual care that does not dismiss their pain or pressure them into silence. The church must recognize the difference between helping someone grow in faith and demanding that someone conceal the wounds created by religious failure. The healing of spiritual wounds begins with honesty. The church must be willing to look carefully at its own conduct, recognize where people have been harmed, and pursue correction with humility. The people who have been wounded must never become an inconvenience to the mission of Christ. Jesus remains the One who offers rest to the weary, hope to the broken, forgiveness to those who repent, and salvation to those who turn toward Him. The church’s responsibility is to point people toward that Savior while ensuring that its own conduct does not become another obstacle between the wounded and the One who can heal. The church must never forget that spiritual authority was entrusted for the care of people. The wounds created through its misuse should not be ignored, minimized, or defended. They should be examined with honesty, addressed with responsibility, and met with the compassion that reflects the heart of Jesus Christ.
