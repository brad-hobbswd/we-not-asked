PART III · THE COST OF INTERFERENCE

When Rejection Speaks Louder Than the Gospel

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 11 of 17Reading edition

The gospel announces the love of God demonstrated through Jesus Christ. The gospel calls people toward repentance, faith, salvation, reconciliation, and new life. Yet the message can become difficult to hear when the behavior of those proclaiming it creates a different impression.

People encounter Christianity through more than Scripture. They may first meet the faith through a family member, conversation, church service, public statement, online post, or interaction with someone who identifies as a follower of Jesus. Such encounters can influence how they understand the church and, sometimes, how they understand God. Believers therefore carry a responsibility not only to know what they proclaim, but to reflect the message through their conduct.

Someone may remember the words spoken during a conversation, but also the manner in which those words were delivered. A person treated with dignity may remember being listened to, even during disagreement. Someone humiliated may remember the embarrassment long after the exact words have faded. The manner in which truth is carried can either clarify the message or obscure it.

The church must recognize that its witness can be damaged when conduct contradicts the gospel. We proclaim reconciliation while sometimes deepening divisions, announce mercy while demonstrating impatience, and speak of God’s love while communicating hostility. Those contradictions deserve examination rather than dismissal as ordinary imperfections.

Salt and light

Jesus described His followers as salt and light in Matthew 5:13–16 (AMPC). He called them to let their light shine so others might see their good works and glorify the Father. The purpose was not personal recognition, but a life that directs attention toward God.

The church must examine the relationship between its message and daily conduct. A congregation may preach salvation, holiness, forgiveness, and mercy while communicating something different through the way people are treated. The inconsistency becomes visible when someone disagrees, asks a difficult question, or arrives carrying circumstances that challenge expectations.

Standing firmly upon Scripture is not the same as treating another person as though disagreement removes their dignity. Explaining a conviction is not the same as shaming someone into accepting it. Correction intended to encourage spiritual growth differs from communication designed to establish dominance.

Biblical truth does not automatically make every expression of truth faithful. The message may be accurate while the manner is harmful. Believers remain responsible for both what they proclaim and the conduct through which they proclaim it.

Love must shape our witness

In 1 Corinthians 13:1–8 (AMPC), Paul places love at the center of spiritual gifts and Christian service. The chapter is often read at weddings, but its context addresses the life of the church. Knowledge, spiritual gifts, sacrifice, and service cannot replace love.

The passage challenges anyone more concerned with being recognized as correct than with demonstrating the character of Christ. Truth matters. Knowledge matters. Service matters. Love must remain present within each responsibility.

Contempt cannot become an accepted form of communication in a church that proclaims reconciliation. Compassion is not weakness, and patience is not surrender. The gospel must be carried through conduct that reflects the Savior.

Listening before defending

Some people continue believing in Jesus while struggling to trust religious communities. Others have withdrawn from church but still pray or remain open to spiritual conversation. The church should not assume that every person questioning Christianity has rejected Christ. Someone may be responding to hypocrisy, inconsistency, cruelty, abuse, or profound disappointment.

When such concerns are raised, the instinct to defend the church can overshadow the opportunity to hear what is being said. Listening does not require agreeing with every conclusion. Listening allows us to understand the experience before responding, acknowledge harm where it occurred, and examine our own conduct without reflexive defensiveness. A conversation may remain difficult, but humility can keep the door open.

Witness is formed through repeated encounters, not a single public moment. Trust is often built slowly through ordinary faithfulness: a promise kept, a private confidence respected, a person remembered after a difficult week, or a willingness to remain in conversation when disagreement becomes uncomfortable. Such details may appear small, yet they help communicate whether Christian love is a principle spoken about or a responsibility practiced.

Trust cannot be demanded as evidence of someone else's spiritual maturity. The church can offer care without insisting that the recipient respond in a particular way or provide reassurance to the people offering help. Our responsibility is to remain consistent and humble, even when the response is uncertain.

Our public conduct

Public statements, online behavior, church policies, and community interactions all influence the witness of the church. Someone may never attend a service but encounter Christians through the way they speak about others. Those encounters can demonstrate Christ’s character or create confusion concerning the gospel.

Online communication requires particular care. Ridicule, degrading language, careless accusations, and the celebration of another person’s suffering may be presented by people who claim to represent Jesus. Believers should consider whether their words are wise, necessary, and consistent with the message they proclaim. The ability to respond immediately does not mean every response should be published.

James 3:1–12 (AMPC) warns about the power of speech and the difficulty of controlling the tongue. Words influence relationships, create damage, and reveal inconsistencies within our spiritual lives. Religious language does not make speech harmless. Public remarks can damage reputations, while private conversations can create fear that remains long after the interaction.

Every believer must guard their speech, exercise patience, and consider whether their words build up or tear down. Spiritual maturity should become visible through communication, particularly when we are frustrated or confronted with circumstances we do not understand.

The church should not measure the strength of its witness by the volume of its public statements. A response may be widely shared and still fail to reflect the character of Christ. The desire to defend a position must not become more important than the responsibility to speak with integrity.

A witness that acknowledges failure

The gospel remains true despite human failures, yet those failures must not be ignored. Believers must acknowledge when their conduct contradicts the message they intended to communicate. Honest admission is not surrendering the truth of Scripture. Honest admission means refusing to pretend that our actions are beyond examination.

When harm has occurred, acknowledgment should be specific enough to be meaningful. General statements about everyone making mistakes may avoid the very responsibility that needs to be addressed. A sincere apology does not require the injured person to immediately forgive, restore trust, or resume a relationship. The church should understand repair as a process that may include listening, correction, changed behavior, and appropriate safeguards.

Repair should also be visible through changed conduct over time. A public apology may be necessary, yet the credibility of an apology is strengthened by consistent behavior afterward. The person who acknowledged a mistake should avoid repeating the conduct, respect appropriate boundaries, and remain willing to receive correction. The congregation should not treat a single statement as proof that every concern has been resolved, nor should it use the need for continued accountability as a reason to deny the possibility of change.

The credibility of the message

The church must not measure its witness solely through attendance, programs, public recognition, or visible success. Credibility is also shaped by the consistency of its conduct: how it responds to conflict, speaks about those who disagree, acknowledges mistakes, and handles circumstances that challenge its assumptions. Personal anger, cultural frustration, or the desire to defend its reputation must not overshadow the purpose of the gospel.

We cannot control every response to the message or guarantee that every conversation will produce understanding. We remain responsible for how we represent Christ. Rejection may become the loudest part of someone's memory, and the church must recognize when its conduct has contributed to that experience.

Our lives should demonstrate patience, mercy, humility, integrity, and love. Those who encounter us should have an opportunity to recognize Christ through the way we speak, listen, serve, and respond. The gospel must not be obscured by the behavior of those who proclaim it. People deserve to hear the truth and experience the love of the One we proclaim.
