CHAPTER NINE Who Decided They Were Too Far Gone? The question of whether someone is too far gone is not ours to answer by reputation, personal disappointment, or the limits of our patience. Scripture tells the truth about sin and its consequences, while also revealing the reach of God’s mercy. The gospel calls people to repentance and announces that forgiveness, transformation, and new life are possible through Jesus Christ. Some people have heard the message of grace but struggle to believe the message includes them. Their shame may be connected to choices they regret, harm they caused, relationships they damaged, or years spent in circumstances they now want to leave behind. Others have received words from people who treated their failures as though the failures had permanently settled their spiritual future. The church must be careful not to communicate a hopelessness Scripture does not proclaim. Jesus came to seek and save the lost. His mission did not depend upon the approval of observers who believed certain people were unsuitable for His attention. In Matthew 9:9–13 (AMPC), He called Matthew, a tax collector, and later ate with tax collectors and sinners. Religious observers questioned His association with them. Jesus answered by describing a physician’s purpose and declaring that He came to call sinners. The encounter does not minimize sin. The physician image acknowledges the need for healing, and Jesus’ call carries the expectation of response. Yet people needing mercy were not evidence that His mission had failed. Their need was the reason He came. Saul: when the persecutor became a witness Saul’s persecution of believers was serious and destructive. Acts records his determination to oppose the followers of Jesus. The church had reason to fear him because his reputation was based on real actions, not rumor. Acts 9:1–22 records Saul’s encounter with Jesus on the road to Damascus. The man who had persecuted believers became a servant of the gospel he once opposed. His transformation did not require pretending the past had never happened. Paul later acknowledged his former conduct and spoke openly about his persecution of the church. Grace became visible through the contrast between his former life and his devotion to Christ. Ananias also reveals the difficulty of responding to someone whose history creates fear. When the Lord instructed him to go to Saul, Ananias expressed concern about Saul’s reputation and actions. His concern was understandable. The instruction nevertheless required him to recognize that God was working beyond the conclusions formed through Saul’s past. This account does not require every person to trust immediately or ignore a legitimate safety concern. It shows that a reputation cannot contain the full future God may bring about. Discernment may require time, and responsible boundaries may remain appropriate. We must not confuse the need for caution with a declaration that transformation is impossible. Peter: failure was not the final word Peter denied Jesus during the hours surrounding the crucifixion. His failure was painful and significant. Yet the risen Christ met Peter again, addressed their relationship, and entrusted him with responsibility for caring for others. John 21:15–19 records that encounter. Jesus did not pretend Peter’s denial had never occurred. Their conversation addressed Peter’s love and the responsibility that followed. Peter’s failure became part of his testimony without becoming the final definition of his life. The church should not treat failure as though the story has ended. Some people may need correction before responsibility is restored. Relationships may require rebuilding, and decisions may carry lasting consequences. Still, the possibility of deeper faithfulness after a painful failure must remain open. The returning son Luke 15:11–32 (AMPC) offers another perspective through the parable of the prodigal son. The younger son’s decisions damaged his life and separated him from his father’s household. After reaching a place of deep need, he recognized his condition and returned. The father saw him approaching and responded with compassion. The son acknowledged wrongdoing and expressed his unworthiness, yet the father welcomed him and celebrated his return. The older brother viewed the celebration through resentment and comparison. The father redirected attention toward the significance of restoration: the son who had been lost was home. The parable does not erase the son’s choices or suggest that repentance is unnecessary. The son recognized his wrongdoing and returned. The father's response reveals a willingness to receive rather than to keep the son defined by the distance he had traveled. The older brother's resentment challenges those who struggle to rejoice when mercy reaches someone whose history they know. The church must consider how it responds when people turn toward God. We may need to offer teaching, support, accountability, or practical help. Those responsibilities should not become a reason to withhold hope or make a returning person feel that the community would rather remember their failure than recognize their present response. When our words close what Scripture leaves open Some people have stopped seeking God because they believe their history has permanently disqualified them. Others have been told directly that they are beyond hope or have reached that conclusion through repeated experiences of rejection. Words spoken in anger or judgment may remain in someone’s memory long after the conversation has ended. Believers must speak carefully when discussing redemption. Scripture calls people to repentance, and that call carries hope. Forgiveness would have little meaning if the possibility of mercy had already been removed from those who need it. We must not pronounce a final verdict where Scripture calls us to proclaim the gospel and leave judgment to God. This does not mean every claim of transformation must be accepted without discernment or that trust must be restored immediately. Genuine repentance involves turning toward God and a changed direction. The church may need to assess behavior, establish safeguards, and protect those affected by past harm. These responsibilities should be handled with fairness and care, not as a means of declaring someone spiritually unreachable. Restoration and responsibility Restoration is not a single outcome that looks identical in every circumstance. A person may be reconciled to God while continuing to face consequences. Forgiveness does not automatically restore every relationship, leadership role, or level of trust. Those affected by harm may need time, support, and boundaries, and their needs must not be ignored in the name of celebrating someone else's progress. At the same time, consequences should not be treated as proof that grace has failed. Someone may be forgiven and still need to make restitution. Someone may be growing spiritually while continuing to receive help. A person may demonstrate sincere change without returning to the same position they once held. These realities can coexist without denying the work of God. The church should resist both careless extremes. We must not dismiss harm or offer immediate trust without wisdom. We must also not allow caution to become hopelessness, or accountability to become a permanent declaration that change is impossible. Decisions should consider the actual circumstances, the safety and needs of those affected, and evidence of responsible growth. The limits of our judgment The question of whether someone is too far gone reveals the limits of human judgment. We may know portions of a person's history, recognize patterns, and understand consequences. We do not possess the authority to determine the ultimate reach of God’s mercy. The cross reveals the seriousness of sin and the extraordinary reach of God’s love. Jesus gave Himself because humanity needed salvation. The gospel cannot be faithfully proclaimed while believers behave as though grace is available only to people whose histories they find acceptable. The person whose life once caused concern may become someone who serves faithfully. Someone carrying shame may discover the freedom of forgiveness. Someone who believed there was no future may begin walking toward a life shaped by obedience and hope. We cannot predict every outcome, but we must not close the door through our own assumptions. Jesus came to seek and save the lost. That mission should shape how we view people whose lives have been marked by failure. The church is entrusted with the message of salvation, and the message must remain available to those who recognize their need for God. We must be careful not to become so confident in our own judgment that we forget the power of the grace we proclaim. Our responsibility is to remain faithful to Scripture, exercise wisdom, pursue restoration where possible, and proclaim Jesus Christ with the conviction that His saving power reaches people whose lives have been broken by sin, circumstances, and failure. The gospel remains an invitation to repentance, faith, and new life in Jesus Christ. We are called to carry that invitation faithfully, remembering that no human being has been given authority to place a limit upon the saving power of God. The mercy we proclaim must also shape how we respond when religious failure has wounded others. Hope cannot be announced while the pain of those seeking it is dismissed.
