CHAPTER THREE The Disciples Who Became the Obstacle The account of Jesus receiving children is brief, yet the encounter reveals a conflict between the expectations of those accompanying Him and the response He desired from them. Mark records that people brought children to Jesus so that He might touch them, while the disciples rebuked those bringing them. The narrative does not explain the disciples’ reasoning in detail, but their response made access to Jesus more difficult for those who were seeking His attention. The people had come with a simple desire, and the disciples introduced resistance where none had been requested by the Lord. The distinction between the people approaching Jesus and the disciples attempting to regulate that approach is central to the passage. The children were not recorded as demanding authority, challenging His teaching, or disrupting His ministry through misconduct. They were being brought to Him. The disciples’ response therefore raises a question concerning the responsibility they assumed and the authority upon which they believed that responsibility rested. Mark’s account places the correction in unmistakable terms. Jesus became indignant and instructed the disciples to allow the children to come to Him, telling them not to hinder them. His response exposes the seriousness of their interference. Their actions were not merely an inconvenience that Jesus preferred to handle differently. They were standing in opposition to what He wanted to happen. The disciples were not the ones extending the invitation, yet they were acting as though they possessed the authority to regulate those responding to it. Their intervention had placed an unnecessary obstacle between the people and the Lord. Jesus corrected their conduct and demonstrated that their assumptions could not be allowed to override His intention. The people were bringing, and the disciples were rebuking The wording of the passage presents two contrasting actions. People were bringing children to Jesus, while the disciples were rebuking those who brought them. The contrast is important because the people were moving toward Christ, and the disciples were responding in a way that discouraged that movement. The word rebuking communicates more than a quiet suggestion or a neutral attempt to manage a gathering. The disciples were responding negatively to those bringing the children. Their actions communicated that the people should not proceed in the manner they were attempting. The narrative does not tell us what words were spoken, how loudly the disciples responded, or what emotions the parents experienced. We should not invent details to strengthen the scene. The text itself is sufficient: the disciples rebuked those bringing the children, and Jesus objected to their conduct. That distinction allows us to examine the passage without adding circumstances Scripture does not provide. We do not need to assume that the disciples were cruel, that they hated children, or that they deliberately intended to keep anyone from salvation. The importance of the account lies in the fact that their action hindered people who were seeking Jesus, regardless of what motivated the action. A person can become an obstacle without intending to cause lasting harm. The result of a decision may reveal a problem that the person making the decision never considered. Someone may believe a restriction is reasonable, necessary, or appropriate while failing to recognize that the restriction is interfering with something the Lord has welcomed. The disciples’ correction demonstrates that the consequences of their actions mattered, even though Scripture does not establish their motives. The church must be willing to examine the same distinction. We cannot evaluate every action solely according to what we intended. We must also consider whether our conduct is consistent with Scripture and whether our decisions have placed unnecessary resistance in the path of people seeking the Lord. Jesus’ indignation deserves our attention Mark specifically records that Jesus became indignant. The detail prevents us from treating the encounter as an insignificant disagreement among followers. The Lord’s response reveals that the disciples’ conduct required serious correction. The emotion of Jesus should not be separated from the action that provoked it. He was responding to people being hindered from approaching Him. His indignation was directed toward the obstruction, and His instruction immediately addressed the problem. He called for the children to be allowed to come and commanded the disciples to stop hindering them. The passage offers a sobering picture of the difference between the priorities of Jesus and the assumptions of those following Him. The disciples may have believed that they were managing the demands surrounding His ministry, but their management had become contrary to His desire. Jesus did not permit their judgment to remain the controlling factor. This is particularly important for those who carry spiritual responsibility. A position of leadership does not guarantee that every decision will reflect the priorities of Christ. A leader may sincerely attempt to protect a ministry, preserve order, maintain expectations, or guard against perceived problems while overlooking the people affected by those decisions. When the Lord’s teaching reveals that a response has become an obstruction, the response must be corrected. The indignation of Jesus also reminds us that the treatment of people seeking Him is not a peripheral concern. The manner in which His followers respond to those approaching Him matters to the Lord. Compassion cannot be treated as an optional quality added to ministry after the more important responsibilities have been completed. The encounter itself demonstrates that Jesus considered the obstruction significant enough to address directly. The correction was immediate and unmistakable Jesus did not leave the disciples to determine whether their response had been appropriate. He corrected them and directed them to allow the children to come. The instruction removed the restriction they had imposed. The correction was not directed toward the children or the people bringing them. Jesus did not instruct the parents to return at a more convenient time, seek approval from the disciples, or demonstrate that their children were worthy of receiving His attention. He addressed those who had introduced the obstruction. That distinction is essential to the meaning of the passage. The people seeking Jesus were not required to resolve the disciples’ misunderstanding before the invitation could be honored. Jesus addressed the misunderstanding Himself. The passage does not suggest that the disciples possessed no responsibilities or that their role in ministry was meaningless. Rather, it reveals that their responsibility had been exercised in a manner inconsistent with the Lord’s direction. Their correction was necessary because their conduct had interfered with the encounter. The church must be willing to recognize when correction belongs to those exercising authority rather than to those who have been discouraged or rejected. Not every conflict requires the person seeking God to be treated as the source of the problem. Sometimes the obstruction exists within the attitudes, practices, or assumptions of those who believe they are protecting the church. Recognizing that possibility requires humility. It is easier to examine the conduct of those who approach us than to examine our own response toward them. It is easier to defend a decision than to acknowledge that the decision may have contradicted the heart of Christ. The disciples’ example reminds us that the people closest to a ministry may still need correction concerning the way they respond to others. The kingdom revealed through the children Jesus connected His instruction concerning the children with the kingdom of God. He taught that those who receive the kingdom must receive it as a child. The statement directs attention beyond the immediate disagreement and toward the manner in which the kingdom is received. The children were not presented as people who had established their worth through accomplishments, religious standing, or personal authority. They were being brought to Jesus, and He welcomed them. His response placed the kingdom’s significance beyond the assumptions of those who attempted to regulate the encounter. The disciples had introduced a distinction between those who could approach and those whose approach they considered inappropriate. Jesus redirected their understanding by identifying the children with the very lesson they needed to learn about receiving the kingdom. Their response had failed to reflect the humility and openness required of those who belonged to Him. The passage does not require us to idealize children or assume that every aspect of childhood represents spiritual perfection. Jesus’ teaching concerns receiving the kingdom as a child, and the point should be considered within that context. His words challenge the assumptions of those who believe that position, achievement, status, or personal authority establish their standing within the kingdom. The disciples were learning that the kingdom could not be managed according to the expectations they had developed. Their understanding of importance, access, and responsibility required correction. The children became part of the lesson because their presence exposed the difference between the assumptions of the disciples and the response of Jesus. Jesus did more than remove the obstruction Mark records that Jesus took the children into His arms, laid His hands on them, and blessed them. His response was not limited to telling the disciples to stop rebuking those who brought them. He personally received the children and demonstrated the care they had come seeking. The movement of the passage is significant. The children were brought to Jesus, the disciples hindered those bringing them, Jesus corrected the disciples, and the children were received and blessed. The encounter ends with the Lord doing what the people had hoped He would do. The contrast between obstruction and reception is therefore not merely a difference in policy. It is a difference in response toward the people standing before Him. The disciples had discouraged the approach; Jesus welcomed the children into His presence. The church should consider what that contrast reveals about the responsibility of representing Christ. A ministry may communicate the gospel through preaching, teaching, and public statements, yet the manner in which people are treated also communicates something about the Savior being proclaimed. The words spoken from a pulpit and the decisions made at the entrance to a congregation are not unrelated to the witness of the church. People encounter the message through both. This does not mean that every person who receives a welcome will immediately understand every doctrine, embrace every teaching, or complete every step of discipleship. The encounter in Mark does not address those questions. It establishes the responsibility of the disciples not to hinder the children who were being brought to Jesus and demonstrates the response of Christ toward them. We must allow the passage to speak within the boundaries of what it records while also recognizing the principle it places before us. Those who follow Jesus must not allow their own assumptions to obstruct the approach of people whom He is receiving. The obstruction was corrected, not defended One of the most important details of the encounter is that Jesus corrected the disciples rather than defending their intervention. The passage does not record Him explaining why their concern might have been reasonable under different circumstances. He addressed the action itself and instructed them to stop. The absence of a defense should make us cautious whenever we become determined to justify our own interference. A decision may have been made for reasons that appear understandable, but those reasons cannot replace the authority of Christ. When Scripture exposes a contradiction between our actions and the direction of the Lord, the faithful response is correction. This principle applies to religious traditions that have developed around familiar practices, to leaders who have assumed responsibilities beyond what Scripture establishes, and to believers whose judgments have discouraged others from approaching Christ. The purpose of examining such circumstances is not to presume that every restriction is wrong or every decision by authority is unjustified. The purpose is to recognize that authority must remain accountable to the Word and that no assumption should be permitted to contradict the instruction of Jesus. The disciples’ conduct was corrected because they had hindered those coming to Him. The lesson remains relevant whenever the actions of believers create an obstruction that the Lord has not commanded. The people seeking Jesus must remain the focus The account concludes with Jesus receiving and blessing the children. The final emphasis belongs not to the disciples’ position, their reasoning, or their embarrassment, but to the children who were brought into the presence of Christ. That conclusion should influence the way the church approaches this passage. The purpose of examining the disciples’ failure is not to create a new group of people to condemn. The purpose is to recognize the obstruction, receive the correction, and return our attention to the people seeking the Lord. It is possible to become so absorbed in defending our position that we lose sight of the person standing before us. The church must remain attentive to those who come seeking spiritual understanding, prayer, fellowship, healing, forgiveness, or direction. Their presence should not automatically be treated as a problem requiring resolution before ministry can begin. The responsibility of believers is to faithfully communicate the gospel and direct people toward Christ. The children in this passage were not the ones who needed rebuke. The disciples were. Jesus made that distinction clear through His words and actions. The encounter leaves us with a responsibility that cannot be dismissed through explanations of good intentions, religious expectations, or familiar practices. When our actions hinder people from approaching Jesus, we must be willing to examine those actions in the light of His instruction. The disciples were close to the Lord, yet they had become an obstruction. Jesus corrected them, received those who were being brought to Him, and demonstrated the response His followers were expected to learn. The people were seeking Jesus. The disciples were standing in their way. Jesus told the disciples to stop.
