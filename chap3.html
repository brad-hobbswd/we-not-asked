PART I · THE AUTHORITY WE ASSUMED

The Disciples Who Became the Obstacle

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 3 of 17Reading edition

Mark 10:13–16 moves quickly. Children are brought to Jesus so He might touch them. The disciples rebuke those bringing them. Jesus becomes indignant, tells His followers to allow the children to come, and warns them not to hinder the children. He then takes the children into His arms and blesses them. The text gives us a clear contrast without telling us every detail behind the moment.

Notice who receives the correction. Jesus addresses the disciples, not the children or the people who brought them. He does not ask the families to defend their request. He does not make the children wait while the disciples explain themselves. The obstacle was created by the disciples, so Jesus speaks to them.

Mark does not tell us what the disciples were thinking. We should resist filling in the blanks with a story that sounds plausible. Perhaps they thought they were managing the crowd; perhaps another concern was in their minds. Scripture leaves that unanswered. What we know is that their action was wrong in the eyes of Jesus.

His indignation shows that the moment mattered. The disciples were not simply being corrected for an inconvenient interruption. They were preventing people from doing something Jesus welcomed. His words remove any uncertainty about what should happen next: let them come, and do not hinder them.

Jesus then speaks about receiving the kingdom of God as a child. The comparison is not a claim that children are flawless. His words turn the scene toward the way the kingdom is received, rather than toward status, achievement, or a person's ability to establish their importance. The passage should be allowed to say what Jesus says without being made to answer every question we might bring to it.

The ending is just as important as the correction. Jesus takes the children into His arms, lays His hands on them, and blesses them. He does more than tell the disciples to stop. He gives the children the attention they were brought to receive. The scene closes with His welcome, not with the disciples’ explanation.

Matthew 19:13–15 and Luke 18:15–17 preserve the same encounter. The accounts reinforce the central movement: the children are brought, the disciples try to prevent the approach, Jesus corrects them, and the children are received. The original setting remains children. Any application to other circumstances should be described honestly as an application of the principle, not as though the passage directly names every modern situation.

That principle invites us to examine our own response to people seeking prayer, understanding, fellowship, or help. Have we added resistance that Scripture did not require? Have we defended a decision without considering its effect? Have we focused on the person approaching us and forgotten to examine the way we responded?

Some situations do require boundaries, accountability, and careful discernment. Mark's passage does not create a universal answer for every church decision. It does, however, give us a reason to examine whether a barrier serves a legitimate responsibility or simply reflects an assumption we have never questioned.

The disciples were corrected, and Jesus welcomed the children. We do not need to condemn the disciples to learn from the scene. We need to be willing to recognize our own mistakes with the same honesty. When Christ shows us that our response has hindered rather than helped, we must listen and change.
