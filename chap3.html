CHAPTER THREE The Disciples Who Became the Obstacle Mark 10:13–16 (AMPC) records a brief encounter with a clear movement: people brought children to Jesus, the disciples rebuked those bringing them, Jesus became indignant and corrected the disciples, and He received and blessed the children. The account does not explain the disciples’ motives, describe the exchange in detail, or tell us what the parents felt. The passage itself is enough to establish that the disciples hindered an approach Jesus welcomed. The people were bringing children so that Jesus might touch them. The disciples responded by rebuking those who brought them. Mark does not say that the children were misbehaving or that the parents were demanding something improper. We should not add details the text does not provide. The contrast is straightforward: people sought Jesus, and His followers discouraged them. The rebuke was a real obstruction The disciples’ rebuke was more than a neutral attempt to manage the gathering. Their action communicated resistance to those bringing the children. Whatever they believed they were accomplishing, Jesus did not approve. He became indignant and told them to allow the children to come, adding that they must not hinder them. The force of the response matters. Jesus did not treat the disciples’ decision as a minor inconvenience or leave the matter for them to reconsider at another time. He addressed their conduct directly. Their action stood in the way of what He intended, and His instruction removed the obstruction. We do not need to assign a motive to the disciples in order to understand the correction. The text does not tell us whether they were concerned about time, order, importance, or something else. Speculating about their reasoning may distract from the point Mark actually records. They rebuked those bringing the children; Jesus objected and told them to stop hindering the children. The passage is brief, but its brevity should not make us careless. The recorded actions carry the weight of the narrative. The people bring; the disciples rebuke; Jesus becomes indignant; Jesus commands; Jesus receives and blesses. Each action moves the scene forward. A responsible interpretation does not need an elaborate reconstruction of motives to recognize that the disciples were wrong. Jesus’ indignation Mark specifically tells us that Jesus became indignant. The detail communicates the seriousness with which He regarded the disciples’ conduct. His response was connected to their attempt to hinder the children, and His instruction immediately addressed that behavior. The scene challenges the assumption that the treatment of people seeking Jesus is a secondary concern. The disciples were His followers, yet their conduct required correction. Their responsibilities did not excuse the obstruction, and their closeness to Him did not make their judgment right. The Lord’s response also keeps the focus on what needed to change. He did not direct the children or those bringing them to prove themselves worthy of His attention. He addressed the disciples who had rebuked them. The responsibility for removing the barrier rested with those who had created it. This is an important distinction when applying the passage. The people who were hindered should not be made responsible for resolving the misunderstanding of those who hindered them. When our conduct discourages someone, we must be willing to examine our actions rather than require the other person to explain why our response was painful or inappropriate. The command to allow and not hinder Jesus’ instruction contains both permission and prohibition. He told the disciples to allow the children to come and not to hinder them. The command did not leave the disciples free to continue their rebuke while claiming that the children were technically welcome. Their conduct needed to stop. The difference between an invitation in words and an invitation in practice deserves attention. A congregation may say that everyone is welcome while its behavior communicates that certain people should remain unseen, avoid asking questions, or keep their circumstances hidden. Words and actions must agree. A welcome that is contradicted by unnecessary resistance is not being faithfully carried out. This does not mean that every boundary is an obstruction. The church has responsibilities concerning teaching, safety, accountability, and order. Such responsibilities must be exercised with discernment and should be grounded in legitimate purpose. The passage challenges barriers that oppose what Christ has directed, not every decision that requires careful judgment. Our application must be honest about that distinction. Receiving the kingdom as a child Jesus connected His instruction with the kingdom of God, teaching that whoever does not receive the kingdom as a child will not enter it. The statement moves beyond the immediate question of access and directs attention to the way the kingdom is received. The passage does not ask us to idealize children or assume childhood represents perfection. Jesus’ comparison concerns receiving the kingdom as a child. His words challenge assumptions that standing, accomplishment, status, or personal authority establish a person’s place before God. The children were being brought to Jesus rather than presenting themselves through achievements or claims of importance. Their presence exposed the disciples’ mistaken priorities and provided the setting for Jesus’ teaching. The kingdom cannot be approached through confidence in our own status or through the belief that we possess authority to manage another person's encounter with the Lord. The meaning must remain grounded in Jesus’ words. He teaches about receiving the kingdom, and His correction of the disciples demonstrates the importance of allowing those who were being brought to Him to come near. We should not make the passage answer questions it does not address, but neither should we overlook the correction it clearly provides. Jesus received and blessed them Mark’s account does not end with the correction. Jesus took the children into His arms, laid His hands on them, and blessed them. The people who had brought them received the response they had hoped for, and the disciples’ obstruction was no longer in place. The sequence matters. Jesus did not merely remove the rebuke and return to the previous activity. He gave the children His attention. His actions demonstrate the welcome the disciples had failed to recognize. The children were not left at a distance, defined by the interruption, or required to prove the importance of their request. Matthew 19:13–15 and Luke 18:15–17 also record this encounter. Each account preserves the essential movement: children are brought to Jesus, the disciples attempt to prevent the approach, Jesus corrects them, and the children are received. The parallel accounts reinforce the importance of the moment while leaving the central meaning clear. Jesus’ response was not approval of the disciples’ obstruction. From the passage to our responsibility The original encounter concerns children. The broader principle must be presented as an application rather than as though Mark directly addresses every modern circumstance. The disciples’ conduct invites believers to examine whether our assumptions, decisions, or attitudes have discouraged people whom we should be helping toward Christ. That examination is not an invitation to condemn the disciples or to replace one judgment with another. Their correction reminds us that followers of Jesus can misunderstand what their responsibility requires. When Scripture exposes an obstruction, the faithful response is to receive correction and change our conduct. The church should be willing to ask whether people seeking spiritual understanding, prayer, fellowship, or help have been met with unnecessary resistance. We must consider whether we have treated preferences as commands, defended a decision without examining its effect, or focused so heavily on the person approaching us that we failed to consider our own response. We should also be careful not to presume every difficult experience has the same cause. People may disagree about doctrine, expectations, or decisions, and some situations require boundaries or accountability. The text gives us a principle for examination, not a universal policy for every circumstance. We must distinguish what Scripture states from what we infer and remain willing to evaluate our interpretation. The passage directs attention toward the people being brought to Jesus and toward the responsibility of those who follow Him. The children were not the ones Jesus rebuked. The disciples were. His instruction was clear, and His action followed it. The account ends with Jesus receiving and blessing the children. That is the detail we should carry forward: when the disciples hindered the approach, Jesus corrected them and welcomed those who had been brought to Him.
