CHAPTER FOUR Decisions Jesus Never Delegated The authority of Jesus was never uncertain, borrowed, or dependent upon the approval of those who followed Him. Throughout the Gospels, His words and actions reveal the authority with which He taught, healed, forgave, confronted wrongdoing, and called people into the kingdom of God. Those who followed Him were invited to participate in His ministry, but participation did not transfer ownership of His mission. They were entrusted with responsibilities that required obedience, faithfulness, and humility, yet the authority of Christ remained the foundation beneath every responsibility they received. This distinction becomes essential when examining the relationship between Christian leadership and the invitation of the gospel. Jesus commissioned His followers to carry His message, teach His commandments, make disciples, and bear witness to the truth. He did not commission them to become the source of salvation or the owners of the invitation extended through His name. The work entrusted to believers was significant, but the boundaries of that work were established by Christ rather than by the preferences of those who carried it out. The temptation to exceed those boundaries can arise when responsibility begins to feel like ownership. A person entrusted with teaching may begin assuming that every personal interpretation carries the weight of divine instruction. A leader responsible for the spiritual care of others may become convinced that maintaining order requires control over circumstances Scripture has not placed under personal authority. A family may sincerely desire to preserve faith across generations while treating inherited expectations as though they cannot be examined. In each circumstance, the question of responsibility becomes inseparable from the question of authority. What has Jesus entrusted to His followers, and what remains His alone? The authority of the One who sends Matthew records the words Jesus spoke following His resurrection: “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me.” Matthew 28:18, AMPC. The statement establishes the foundation upon which the Great Commission rests. Jesus did not begin by announcing the authority of the disciples. He declared His own authority and then instructed them concerning the mission they were to carry out. Their commission existed because He sent them. Their responsibility was defined by His instruction, and their confidence rested upon His authority rather than their own standing. The order matters. Christian ministry does not begin with the authority people assume for themselves. It begins with the authority of Christ and the responsibility He entrusts to those who follow Him. The commission is not an opportunity to expand personal influence beyond the instructions of the Lord. It is a calling to faithfully carry out what He commanded. The disciples were instructed to make disciples of all nations, baptize them, and teach them to observe everything Jesus had commanded. The commission involved proclamation, instruction, and the formation of believers who would learn to live according to His teaching. Such responsibilities required spiritual maturity, discernment, and accountability. They did not establish the disciples as independent authorities whose personal preferences could redefine the mission. The authority of Christ must remain central whenever the church considers its responsibility toward people seeking Him. Our role is to communicate what He has commanded, not to create additional requirements that make His invitation dependent upon our approval. We must distinguish between the instruction of Scripture and the assumptions that may develop around that instruction. Faithfulness requires us to carry the message accurately, even when our own expectations would make the message easier to manage by narrowing its reach. A commission to reach, not to possess The Great Commission sends believers outward. The command to make disciples of all nations establishes a mission that extends beyond familiar relationships, communities, cultures, and circumstances. Jesus did not restrict the commission to people who resembled the disciples, shared their background, or possessed an understanding of the kingdom identical to their own. The scope of the command required them to carry the gospel beyond the boundaries of familiarity. The responsibility to make disciples involves teaching people to observe the commandments of Christ. Such teaching is not superficial, nor does discipleship mean that the expectations of Scripture disappear. The commission includes the formation of people who learn to follow Jesus faithfully. Yet the order of responsibility cannot be reversed by making human approval the controlling condition for hearing the gospel. The church cannot make disciples of people whom it refuses to engage. The commission cannot be faithfully carried out through an attitude that treats unfamiliar people as though their presence is inherently a threat to the mission. Believers are called to proclaim the gospel, teach the truth, and remain faithful to Christ, while recognizing that the invitation to encounter Him is not a privilege reserved for those whom we personally find comfortable. The mission entrusted to the church requires us to engage humanity with the understanding that every person needs the saving grace of God. The differences among people may influence the circumstances of ministry, the questions that arise, and the instruction that may be needed, but those differences do not transfer the authority of Christ to the messenger. The messenger must remain a messenger. The responsibility to teach does not establish ownership Teaching is one of the responsibilities Christ entrusted to His followers. The Scriptures call believers to instruct, encourage, correct, and help one another grow in understanding. Such responsibility must be exercised faithfully, with careful attention to the Word and the purpose of discipleship. The difficulty arises when teaching becomes confused with ownership over another person’s spiritual journey. The responsibility to communicate truth does not give a person unrestricted authority over another person’s conscience, nor does the responsibility to correct establish permission to treat someone without dignity. The distinction between faithful instruction and personal control must remain clear. The apostle Paul addressed this distinction when he wrote to the Corinthians concerning the responsibility of those who ministered among them. He described himself and Apollos as servants through whom the Corinthians had believed, according to the ministry assigned to each. Paul planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase. His words placed the work of ministry within the proper relationship between human responsibility and divine authority. The passage reminds us that ministry is a form of service. Those who teach, preach, counsel, shepherd, and lead participate in the work of God, but they do not become the source of spiritual life. Their responsibility is real, and their accountability is equally real. The growth of another person cannot be claimed as personal ownership, nor can the authority of Christ be replaced by the influence of those serving in His name. This understanding should shape the manner in which correction is offered and responsibility is exercised. A person who teaches must remain accountable to the Scriptures being taught. A person who leads must recognize that leadership is entrusted for the benefit of those being served. A person who exercises influence must remain aware that influence can either help people understand the gospel or distort their understanding of the Savior. The church does not become faithful merely by having people in positions of authority. Faithfulness is demonstrated when those positions are exercised in accordance with the purpose and instruction of Christ. The difference between discernment and presumption The Scriptures call believers to discernment. Christians are instructed to examine teaching, recognize error, exercise wisdom, and remain attentive to the truth. Discernment is necessary because not every message, practice, or interpretation accurately reflects the Word of God. Faithfulness requires thoughtful examination rather than indiscriminate acceptance. Yet discernment can be distorted when personal judgments are treated as though they carry the same authority as Scripture. The ability to examine a matter does not establish the right to make every decision concerning another person’s access to Christ. A believer may recognize a need for instruction without assuming that the person should be discouraged from approaching the Lord. A leader may address conduct within the responsibilities of ministry without making personal approval the prerequisite for hearing the gospel. The difference lies in the purpose and boundaries of the responsibility being exercised. Biblical discernment serves the truth. Presumption extends personal authority beyond what the truth establishes. One requires humility before Scripture; the other can become a means of elevating human judgment above the instruction of Christ. This distinction deserves particular attention when religious communities make decisions concerning people whose lives, identities, histories, or circumstances differ from the expectations of those communities. Scripture must be examined carefully, and conclusions must be reached responsibly. Yet the seriousness of biblical examination should not become an excuse for treating people as though they are beyond the reach of Christ’s invitation. The responsibility to understand Scripture faithfully and the responsibility to treat people with compassion are not opposing obligations. The church must never assume that its ability to evaluate a question gives it authority to obstruct a person’s approach to Jesus. The commission remains the proclamation of the gospel, the teaching of the Word, and the making of disciples. Our judgments must remain accountable to the authority of Christ. The authority to save belongs to Christ The New Testament consistently places salvation within the work of God through Jesus Christ. Human beings proclaim the gospel, but they do not become the Savior. Believers testify, teach, pray, serve, and encourage, yet the power to redeem belongs to the Lord. This truth should produce humility in everyone who participates in ministry. The temptation to control access can arise when people begin treating the acceptance of their own judgments as though it were necessary for another person’s encounter with God. The church may begin functioning as though the approval of its members determines whether a person is permitted to seek the Savior. Such a posture confuses the responsibility of the messenger with the authority of the One being proclaimed. Jesus alone is the foundation of salvation. The invitation to come to Him cannot be converted into an invitation to satisfy the preferences of those who happen to be carrying the message. The church has a responsibility to teach the truth of the gospel, but the church must never imagine that its authority supersedes the authority of Christ. This understanding does not remove accountability from ministry. It establishes accountability in its proper place. Those entrusted with spiritual responsibility must answer to the Lord for the manner in which they exercise that responsibility. Their decisions, teaching, conduct, and treatment of people must remain subject to the Word of God. The authority they carry is not independent of Christ, and the mission they serve is not theirs to redefine. When responsibility becomes a reason to restrict Some restrictions are necessary within particular responsibilities. Scripture provides instruction concerning conduct, teaching, discipline, and the order of the church. Those matters cannot be dismissed simply because a person desires unrestricted participation in every circumstance. The church must remain faithful to the biblical responsibilities entrusted to it. The concern addressed in this book, however, is the presumption that those responsibilities authorize believers to obstruct people from approaching Jesus. A distinction must be maintained between carrying out biblical instruction and creating personal restrictions that Scripture does not establish. When those distinctions become blurred, religious responsibility can become a justification for decisions that have never been carefully examined. The disciples’ actions in Mark 10 demonstrate that such a misunderstanding can occur among those following Jesus. They attempted to regulate the approach of people bringing children, but the Lord corrected their conduct. Their responsibility as followers did not authorize them to override His response toward those seeking Him. That account does not answer every question concerning the administration of the church, and it should not be used to dismiss the biblical responsibilities of leadership. It does, however, establish a principle that must remain before every believer: our authority cannot contradict the instruction of Christ, and our responsibilities cannot become permission to obstruct His invitation. The church must therefore examine the reasons behind its decisions, the Scriptures supporting those decisions, and the consequences of the way those decisions are carried out. A restriction may be defended as necessary, but the question of necessity must be examined rather than assumed. A tradition may be presented as essential, but its relationship to Scripture must be considered. A position may be held by respected authorities, but respect does not eliminate the responsibility to examine the Word. The authority we must return to The central issue is not whether believers have responsibilities. They do. The issue is whether those responsibilities remain faithful to the authority of Christ. Jesus commissioned His followers to carry the gospel, make disciples, teach His commandments, and bear witness to Him. The mission is substantial, and the responsibility is serious. Neither can be fulfilled faithfully when human assumptions become more authoritative than the instructions of the Lord. Every generation of believers must remain willing to examine the boundaries of its own authority. The church must recognize the difference between teaching Scripture and elevating personal interpretation, between shepherding and controlling, between discernment and presumption, and between faithful correction and unnecessary obstruction. Those distinctions are not intended to weaken the authority of God’s Word. They are intended to protect the church from claiming authority that belongs to Christ alone. The disciples were entrusted with following Jesus. They were not entrusted with ownership of His invitation. Their correction reminds us that responsibility must never be confused with permission to make decisions the Lord has not delegated. The authority of Christ remains the foundation of the church, the source of its commission, and the standard by which its conduct must be examined. We serve under His authority. We carry His message. We teach His Word. We make disciples according to His command. The invitation remains His. Jesus commissioned us to carry the gospel. He never commissioned us to control who could come to Him.
