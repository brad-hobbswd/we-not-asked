CHAPTER FOUR Decisions Jesus Never Delegated Jesus’ authority was never dependent upon the approval of those who followed Him. He taught with authority, healed, forgave, confronted wrongdoing, and called people into the kingdom. The disciples were invited to participate in His ministry, but participation did not make them the source of His power or the owners of His invitation. This chapter considers the boundary between the responsibilities Christ entrusted to His followers and the authority that remains His alone. The church must teach Scripture, make disciples, provide pastoral care, and address matters of conduct. Those responsibilities are real. They do not permit us to claim the place of Christ in determining the ultimate worth of a person, controlling the mercy of God, or treating our own approval as the condition of approaching the Savior. The authority of the One who sends After His resurrection, Jesus declared, “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me” (Matthew 28:18–20, AMPC). He then commissioned His disciples to go, make disciples of all nations, baptize, and teach them to observe His commandments. His authority established the foundation of their mission. They were sent because He commanded them, and their responsibility was defined by His instruction. The order matters. Ministry begins with the authority of Christ, not authority people assume for themselves. The commission is not an opportunity to expand personal influence beyond the Lord’s instructions. The commission calls them to carry out what He commanded. Making disciples involves proclamation, instruction, baptism, and the formation of believers who learn to follow Jesus. These responsibilities require discernment and accountability. They do not establish independent authority to redefine the mission according to the preferences of those carrying it. The messenger must remain a messenger. The authority to save Human beings proclaim the gospel, but they do not become the Savior. Believers testify, teach, pray, serve, and encourage; the power to redeem belongs to God. This truth should produce humility in everyone participating in ministry. The temptation to control access arises when acceptance of our own judgments begins to seem necessary for another person’s encounter with God. A congregation may function as though its approval determines whether someone is permitted to seek the Savior. Such a posture confuses the responsibility of the messenger with the authority of the One being proclaimed. Jesus alone is the foundation of salvation. The invitation to come to Him cannot be converted into an invitation to satisfy the preferences of those carrying the message. This does not remove accountability from ministry; it places accountability where it belongs. Our decisions, teaching, conduct, and treatment of people remain subject to Scripture. The church must be careful not to speak as though it knows the final standing of every person before God. Scripture gives clear instruction concerning faith, repentance, obedience, and judgment. Yet the Lord knows the heart, understands every circumstance, and sees what human beings cannot. Our responsibility is to teach what Scripture reveals and avoid claiming knowledge or authority that has not been given. The heart is not ours to possess Jesus repeatedly encountered people whose circumstances were interpreted by others before they were heard. He knew the thoughts and motives of people in ways no human observer could. The church must remember that our understanding is limited. We can observe actions, hear testimony, examine teaching, and respond to what is known. We cannot claim the Lord’s complete knowledge of another person’s heart. This limitation should make our judgments careful. A person's history may be relevant to a specific concern, but history does not provide permission to assume every present motive. A question may require teaching without proving rebellion. A struggle may call for pastoral care without authorizing public exposure. A disagreement may need to be addressed without turning the person into an enemy. The distinction is not an argument against discernment. Scripture requires discernment. The distinction is between evaluating what is properly before us and claiming certainty about matters only God fully knows. Humility should shape how we speak when our knowledge is partial. The invitation and the message Jesus spoke truth, called people to respond, and demonstrated the mercy and authority of God. The church must proclaim the gospel and teach repentance, obedience, and discipleship faithfully. The messenger, however, cannot add personal approval as a prerequisite for hearing the message. Conditions placed around people should be examined against Scripture and the responsibility they are intended to serve. A restriction may be necessary in a particular circumstance, but its purpose should be clear and proportionate. Our decisions must not imply that the grace of God is beyond reach. Teaching is entrusted; ownership is not The Scriptures call believers to instruct, encourage, correct, and help one another grow. Teaching must be faithful to the Word and directed toward the purpose of discipleship. The difficulty begins when instruction is confused with ownership over another person’s spiritual journey. Paul reminded the Corinthians that he and Apollos were servants through whom they had believed, each according to the ministry assigned. Paul planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase (1 Corinthians 3:5–7, AMPC). The work belonged to God. Those who preach, teach, counsel, shepherd, and lead participate in His work, but they do not become the source of spiritual life. Their responsibility is real, and their accountability is equally real. The growth of another person cannot be claimed as personal ownership. A leader may teach and encourage, yet cannot force genuine faith. A congregation may provide a place for discipleship, yet cannot replace the work of God in the heart. This understanding should shape correction. The purpose of teaching is not to establish the teacher’s superiority, but to help people understand and follow Christ. Correction may be necessary, and the church must not avoid difficult matters. The manner of correction should reflect the purpose of restoration and the dignity of the person receiving it. Discernment without presumption Believers are called to discernment. Scripture instructs us to examine teaching, recognize error, exercise wisdom, and remain attentive to truth. Discernment is necessary, yet can be distorted when personal judgments are treated as though they carry the same authority as Scripture. Recognizing a need for instruction does not establish the right to discourage someone from approaching the Lord. Addressing conduct within the responsibilities of ministry does not make personal approval a prerequisite for hearing the gospel. Biblical discernment serves truth; presumption extends personal authority beyond what truth establishes. This distinction matters when communities make decisions concerning people whose lives, identities, histories, or circumstances differ from their expectations. Scripture must be examined carefully, and conclusions must be reached responsibly. The seriousness of biblical examination should never become an excuse to treat people as beyond the reach of Christ’s invitation. Faithful teaching and compassionate treatment remain obligations of the church. Boundaries and barriers Scripture provides instruction concerning conduct, teaching, discipline, and the order of the church. Those responsibilities cannot be dismissed, and not every decision concerning participation or ministry is an obstruction. The concern is the presumption that responsibility authorizes us to hinder people from approaching Jesus. Decisions affecting participation, access, or ministry should have a legitimate purpose, be grounded in Scripture, and be carried out with dignity. Safety and accountability matter; unnecessary humiliation, exposure, or contempt do not become acceptable through spiritual language. The church must examine its reasons, the Scriptures supporting its decisions, and the consequences of carrying them out. Necessity should be demonstrated rather than assumed. Chapter Sixteen will return to the practical difference between responsible stewardship and personal control. What remains His alone Believers have responsibilities, but those responsibilities must remain faithful to the authority of Christ. We are commissioned to carry the gospel, make disciples, teach His commandments, and bear witness. None of those duties can be fulfilled faithfully when human assumptions become more authoritative than the Lord’s instructions. Every generation must examine the boundaries of its responsibility. We must distinguish teaching Scripture from elevating personal interpretation, discernment from presumption, faithful correction from unnecessary obstruction, and stewardship from ownership. Such distinctions do not weaken the Word; they protect us from claiming authority that belongs to Christ alone. The church is not the Savior. Our role is to proclaim Him, teach His Word, serve people faithfully, and remain accountable for the way we carry the message. We do not possess the invitation, and we cannot control the work of God in another person's life. The authority of Christ is the foundation of our commission and the boundary of our responsibility. Every decision must remain subject to His Word.
