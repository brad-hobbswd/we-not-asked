PART I · THE AUTHORITY WE ASSUMED

Decisions Jesus Never Delegated

WE WERE NOT ASKED · Bradley Hobbs

Chapter 4 of 17Reading edition

After the resurrection, Jesus declared that all authority in heaven and on earth belonged to Him, then sent His disciples to make disciples, baptize, and teach people to observe His commandments (Matthew 28:18–20). The order is important. The authority belongs to Christ, and the work entrusted to His followers comes from His commission.

That commission gives the church real work to do. We proclaim the gospel, teach Scripture, make disciples, care for people, and address matters that require accountability. None of those responsibilities makes us the source of salvation or gives us permission to expand our role beyond what Christ has commanded.

Sometimes the line gets blurred. We may begin to think our approval is necessary before someone can seek Jesus, or that our judgment settles a person's standing before God. Scripture gives clear teaching about repentance, faith, obedience, and judgment. The Lord also knows the heart in a way we do not. Our responsibility is to teach what He has revealed, not claim knowledge He has not given us.

Paul reminded the Corinthians that he planted and Apollos watered, but God gave the increase (1 Corinthians 3:5–7). The image keeps ministry in its proper place. We can teach, encourage, pray, and serve, but spiritual life belongs to God. We are participants in His work, not owners of the people whose lives we touch.

That understanding should shape how we correct. The goal is not to establish the teacher's superiority or protect a leader's status. Correction should help a person understand truth and grow in Christ. Sometimes correction must be firm. Even then, dignity is not optional, and humiliation does not become holy simply because someone attaches spiritual language to it.

Discernment remains necessary. Churches must examine teaching, address conduct, and make decisions about safety and participation. A restriction may serve a legitimate purpose. Such decisions should be grounded in Scripture, explained honestly where possible, and carried out with care. The concern is not responsibility itself, but the moment responsibility becomes personal control.

We cannot decide the ultimate worth of another person, and we should be cautious about speaking as though we know the whole of someone's heart. We can respond to what is known, ask questions, seek counsel, and teach faithfully. Humility reminds us that our knowledge is partial and our decisions remain accountable.

The invitation to Christ cannot become a demand for people to satisfy our preferences first. We are called to make Him known and to teach His way. The church is not the Savior. Our authority is borrowed, our responsibility is serious, and our work is faithful only when it remains under the authority of Christ.
