CHAPTER FOUR Decisions Jesus Never Delegated Jesus’ authority was never dependent upon the approval of those who followed Him. His words and actions reveal authority to teach, heal, forgive, confront wrongdoing, and call people into the kingdom. His disciples were invited to participate in His ministry, but participation did not transfer ownership of His mission. They were entrusted with responsibilities, and those responsibilities remained defined by the One who sent them. This distinction is essential when considering Christian leadership and the invitation of the gospel. Jesus commissioned His followers to carry His message, make disciples, teach His commandments, and bear witness. He did not make them the source of salvation or owners of the invitation extended through His name. Their work mattered, but its boundaries were established by Christ, not by personal preference. Responsibility can begin to feel like ownership. A teacher may assume that every interpretation carries the weight of divine instruction. A leader may believe that maintaining order requires control over circumstances Scripture has not placed under personal authority. A family may seek to preserve faith while treating inherited expectations as beyond examination. Each situation raises the same question: what has Jesus entrusted to His followers, and what remains His alone? The authority of the One who sends After His resurrection, Jesus declared, “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me” (Matthew 28:18, AMPC). He then commissioned His disciples to go, make disciples of all nations, baptize, and teach them to observe His commandments. His authority established the foundation of their mission. They were sent because He commanded them, and their responsibility was defined by His instruction. The order matters. Ministry begins with the authority of Christ, not the authority people assume for themselves. The commission is not an opportunity to expand personal influence beyond the Lord’s instructions. It is a calling to carry out what He commanded. Making disciples involves proclamation, instruction, baptism, and the formation of believers who learn to follow Jesus. These responsibilities require discernment and accountability. They do not establish independent authority to redefine the mission according to the preferences of those carrying it. The church must distinguish the teaching of Scripture from additional requirements that may develop around that teaching. Faithfulness requires us to carry the message accurately, even when our expectations might make ministry easier by narrowing whom we engage. A commission to reach The Great Commission sends believers beyond familiar relationships, communities, cultures, and circumstances. Jesus directed His followers toward all nations. The scope of the command required them to carry the gospel beyond the boundaries of familiarity. Discipleship includes teaching people to observe the commandments of Christ. The expectations of Scripture do not disappear, and the commission is not superficial welcome without instruction. Yet the order cannot be reversed by making human approval the controlling condition for hearing the gospel. The church cannot make disciples of people whom it refuses to engage. Believers are called to proclaim truth and teach faithfully while recognizing that the invitation to encounter Christ is not reserved for people whom we personally find comfortable. Differences may shape the questions that arise and the instruction that is needed, but those differences do not transfer the authority of Christ to the messenger. The messenger must remain a messenger. Teaching is not ownership The Scriptures call believers to instruct, encourage, correct, and help one another grow. Teaching must be faithful to the Word and directed toward the purpose of discipleship. The difficulty begins when instruction is confused with ownership over another person’s spiritual journey. The responsibility to communicate truth does not give unrestricted authority over another person’s conscience, nor does the responsibility to correct permit treating someone without dignity. Paul reminded the Corinthians that he and Apollos were servants through whom they had believed, each according to the ministry assigned. Paul planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase (1 Corinthians 3:5–7). The work belonged to God. Those who preach, teach, counsel, shepherd, and lead participate in His work, but they do not become the source of spiritual life. Their responsibility is real, and their accountability is equally real. The growth of another person cannot be claimed as personal ownership. This understanding should shape correction and leadership. Teachers must remain accountable to the Scriptures they teach. Leaders must recognize that responsibility is entrusted for the benefit of those being served. Influence can help people understand the gospel, or distort their understanding of the Savior. The church does not become faithful merely by possessing positions of authority. Faithfulness is demonstrated when those positions are exercised according to the purpose and instruction of Christ. Discernment and presumption Believers are called to discernment. Scripture instructs us to examine teaching, recognize error, exercise wisdom, and remain attentive to truth. Discernment is necessary, yet can be distorted when personal judgments are treated as though they carry the same authority as Scripture. Recognizing a need for instruction does not establish the right to discourage someone from approaching the Lord. Addressing conduct within the responsibilities of ministry does not make personal approval a prerequisite for hearing the gospel. Biblical discernment serves truth; presumption extends personal authority beyond what truth establishes. This distinction matters when communities make decisions concerning people whose lives, identities, histories, or circumstances differ from their expectations. Scripture must be examined carefully, and conclusions must be reached responsibly. The seriousness of biblical examination should never become an excuse to treat people as beyond the reach of Christ’s invitation. Faithful teaching and compassionate treatment remain obligations of the church. The authority to save belongs to Christ Human beings proclaim the gospel, but they do not become the Savior. Believers testify, teach, pray, serve, and encourage; the power to redeem belongs to God. This truth should produce humility in everyone participating in ministry. The temptation to control access arises when acceptance of our own judgments begins to seem necessary for another person’s encounter with God. The church may function as though its approval determines whether someone is permitted to seek the Savior. Such a posture confuses the responsibility of the messenger with the authority of the One being proclaimed. Jesus alone is the foundation of salvation. The invitation to come to Him cannot be converted into an invitation to satisfy the preferences of those carrying the message. This does not remove accountability from ministry; it places accountability where it belongs. Our decisions, teaching, conduct, and treatment of people remain subject to Scripture. When responsibility becomes restriction Some boundaries are necessary. Scripture provides instruction concerning conduct, teaching, discipline, and the order of the church. Those responsibilities cannot be dismissed simply because someone desires unrestricted participation in every circumstance. The concern addressed in this book is the presumption that such responsibilities authorize believers to obstruct people from approaching Jesus. We must distinguish biblical instruction from restrictions created by personal preference. When those distinctions blur, religious responsibility can become a justification for decisions that have not been carefully examined. Mark 10 shows that such misunderstanding can occur among Jesus’ own followers. The disciples attempted to regulate the approach of those bringing children, and the Lord corrected them. Their responsibility did not authorize them to override His response. The passage does not answer every question concerning church administration, nor does it dismiss the responsibilities of leadership. It does establish that our authority cannot contradict the instruction of Christ or become permission to obstruct His invitation. The church must examine the reasons behind its decisions, the Scriptures supporting those decisions, and the consequences of carrying them out. Necessity should be demonstrated rather than assumed. Tradition should be considered in relation to Scripture. Respect for leadership does not eliminate the responsibility to examine the Word. What remains His alone Believers have responsibilities, but those responsibilities must remain faithful to the authority of Christ. We are commissioned to carry the gospel, make disciples, teach His commandments, and bear witness. None of those duties can be fulfilled faithfully when human assumptions become more authoritative than the Lord’s instructions. Every generation must examine the boundaries of its authority. We must distinguish teaching Scripture from elevating personal interpretation, shepherding from controlling, discernment from presumption, and faithful correction from unnecessary obstruction. Such distinctions do not weaken the Word; they protect us from claiming authority that belongs to Christ alone. The disciples were entrusted with following Jesus, not owning His invitation. Their correction reminds us that responsibility must never be confused with permission to make decisions the Lord has not delegated. The authority of Christ remains the foundation of the church, the source of its commission, and the standard by which its conduct must be examined. We serve under His authority, carry His message, teach His Word, and make disciples according to His command. The invitation remains His. Jesus commissioned us to carry the gospel. He never commissioned us to control who could come to Him.
